Glossary

Every word the product and its lessons use, defined in plain language — planning and tax vocabulary on one side, Tutilis' own words on the other. Definitions are starting points for reading a plan, not legal or tax advice.

Financial

10-year ruleThe SECURE Act rule for most people who inherit a retirement account after 2019: the account must be empty by December 31 of the tenth year after the owner's death. When the owner had already started RMDs, the heir must also take a distribution every year along the way. An estate gets a 5-year version when the owner had not started RMDs.Financial401(k)An employer-sponsored retirement account, named after the section of tax law that created it — so the name says nothing about what it does. Contributions are usually payroll-deducted, often matched by the employer, and come in traditional (pre-tax) and Roth flavors. A 403(b) is the same idea for schools and nonprofits, a 457 for government workers.Financial529 plan529 plan — a state-sponsored account for education costs, named after the section of tax law that created it. Money goes in after tax, grows untaxed, and comes out tax-free when spent on qualified education. Spent on anything else, its earnings are taxed and carry a 10% penalty. Leftover money can, within limits, roll into the student's Roth IRA.FinancialACAAffordable Care Act — the law behind the individual health-insurance marketplace and its income-based subsidies. It matters to a plan mostly before Medicare age: retiring early means buying coverage here, and the subsidy is tested against income you partly control.FinancialaccumulationThe saving years — the phase when contributions and growth are building the portfolio rather than spending drawing it down. Most plan levers push in one direction in accumulation and the other in drawdown, so the phase a year belongs to changes what "good" means for it.FinancialAdditional Medicare taxAn extra slice of Medicare payroll tax on earnings above a threshold — the employee's side only, with no employer match and no wage cap. Distinct from IRMAA, which is a premium surcharge rather than a tax, and from the investment surtax, which hits a different kind of income.Financialafter-taxMoney that has already had income tax taken out of it. In an account it means you own that basis outright and will not be taxed on it again — only on the growth, and how *that* is taxed depends on the wrapper it grew in.FinancialAGIAdjusted Gross Income — total income minus a specific set of adjustments, and the number many other tax rules build on. It is a midpoint, not your taxable income: deductions still come off after it.FinancialAMTAlternative Minimum Tax — a parallel calculation with its own rules and exemption. You owe the higher of it and the ordinary calculation, so it acts as a floor rather than an extra bill. It bites far less often than it once did.FinancialAMTIAlternative Minimum Taxable Income — the income figure the AMT calculation starts from: ordinary taxable income with certain deductions added back. A plan that adds nothing back treats AMTI as roughly equal to AGI, which is why its AMT line is so often zero.FinancialannuityA contract that pays a stream of income, usually for life, in exchange for a sum up front. It trades flexibility and any leftover balance for the one thing a portfolio cannot promise: a payment that does not stop if you live a long time. Products vary enormously; the word alone says very little.Financialarithmetic meanThe plain average of yearly returns — add them up and divide by the count. It is the number most often quoted, and it is not the growth you live through: a year of +50% followed by a year of −50% averages zero and leaves you down a quarter. The gap between this and the geometric mean is volatility drag, and it widens as σ rises.FinancialAUMAssets under management — the market value of the portfolio an advisor or firm manages for you, and the usual base for their fee. A fee quoted as a percentage of AUM (say 1% a year) is charged on the balance rather than on gains, so it is owed in flat and losing years too, and it grows in dollar terms as the portfolio does. In the Transforms advisor fee what-if it costs a plan twice over: once as its own cash-flow line each year, and again as the compounding those dollars would have done for every year that follows — which is the part a fee schedule never shows.Financialbalance sheetWhat the household owns and what it owes, at one moment. Assets on one side, liabilities on the other, and the difference is net worth. It is a snapshot rather than a story: it says nothing about money moving through the year, which is what the cash flow is for.FinancialbasisWhat you paid for an investment — the starting point tax measures your gain against. Sell for more than basis and the difference is a taxable gain; the money you originally put in is not taxed again. It is the reason two accounts with identical balances can cost very different amounts to spend from.Financialbenefit inclusionHow much of a Social Security benefit counts as taxable income — under US rules none, up to half, or up to 85% of the benefit, depending on other income. Worth separating from the payroll tax that funded it: FICA taxes the wages going in, this taxes the check coming out, and a plan showing both is not double-counting anything.FinancialBMFBundesministerium der Finanzen — Germany's federal finance ministry, whose published income-tax formula and tables are the authority a German sketch is approximating.Financialbond fundA fund that holds many bonds and never matures — it keeps buying new ones as old ones mature. Its yield is paid out of its total return, like any fund; an individual bond's coupon is paid on top of its price. Tutilis models the two differently, so which one a holding is matters.FinancialbracketA band of income taxed at a given rate. The common misreading is that entering a higher bracket taxes all your income at that rate — it does not. Only the dollars inside that band pay the higher rate, so a raise never leaves you with less after tax.FinancialbrokerageAn ordinary taxable investment account — no special retirement wrapper, no contribution limit, and no withdrawal age rules. The trade-off is that dividends and realized gains are taxed as they happen rather than deferred. Often called the taxable bucket next to traditional and Roth.FinancialCAPECyclically Adjusted Price-to-Earnings — a valuation measure that averages earnings over ten years to smooth the cycle. It is used as a rough gauge of whether a market is expensive, and it is a weak short-term signal: high CAPE has preceded both poor decades and long rallies.Financialcapital gain distributionA fund passing its own realized gains through to you. The trap is that it is taxable in the year it is paid even though you sold nothing — the fund did the selling. Distinct from the gain you realize by selling your shares.Financialcash flowMoney moving over a period — wages and benefits in, spending and taxes out. The counterpart to the balance sheet's snapshot: a household can look wealthy on the balance sheet and still be short of cash in a given year, which is usually what forces a sale.Financialcatch-up contributionExtra room to contribute to a retirement account once you reach a set age — the ordinary limit plus an additional amount, with a still-higher amount in a narrow age band. The name is aspirational: nothing has to be caught up on to use it.FinancialCholesky decompositionA way of factoring a correlation matrix so that independent random numbers can be turned into correlated ones. The forecast uses it so that stocks, bonds and spending move together in each simulated year the way their correlations say. When a matrix is inconsistent, it is first repaired to the nearest valid one.Financialclaim ageWhen you start taking Social Security. Claim before full retirement age and the monthly check is permanently smaller; wait past it and it is permanently larger. It is one of the few decisions in a plan that is both free to change beforehand and impossible to change after.FinancialcliffA threshold where one extra dollar of income triggers a step cost — a surcharge tier, a lost subsidy — instead of a smooth increase. Cliffs are why plans care about landing just under a number, and why a small year-end decision can be worth far more than its size.FinancialCOLACost-Of-Living Adjustment — the annual raise applied to a benefit so it keeps pace with prices. Social Security has one; many pensions do not, which is the difference that decides whether a fixed payment still buys anything in twenty years.FinancialcompoundingGrowth on top of previous growth — this year's return is earned on last year's gains as well as the original money. It is why a plan curve bends upward instead of climbing in a straight line, and why the early years matter more than their size suggests.Financialcorporate bondDebt issued by a company. The plain case against which the other two are special: its interest is ordinary income federally and in the state base — no exclusion, no subtraction.FinancialcorrelationHow closely two investments tend to move together, from −1 (opposite) through 0 (unrelated) to +1 (lockstep). Written ρ (rho). It is what decides whether holding several things actually spreads risk: assets that all fall at once are less diversified than a count of holdings suggests.Financialcost of capitalThe rate a plan charges itself for money it has to borrow or leave un-invested — what capital *costs* rather than what it earns. It is why paying cash and financing are not the same plan even when the purchase is identical.FinancialCPIConsumer Price Index — a measure of how the price of a fixed basket of goods changes. CPI-U is the index for all urban consumers, and it is the basket this product's ordinary-spending growth is read from. Social Security's cost-of-living adjustment uses a related index, CPI-W, covering urban wage earners. A plan's inflation number is a long-run assumption, and it is separate from next year's print.FinancialCUSIPThe nine-character id that names one specific security. It matters here because tax character is a property of the issuer, so questions like "how much of this fund is in-state?" are answered issue by issue rather than by the fund's name.FinancialdeductibleIn insurance, what you pay before coverage starts paying — a high deductible means lower premiums and more exposure in a bad year. Watch the other sense: as an adjective in tax, "deductible" means an expense you may subtract from income. Same word, unrelated jobs.FinancialdeductionAn amount subtracted from income before the tax is computed, so it saves tax at your marginal rate rather than dollar for dollar. That is the difference from a credit, which comes off the tax itself: a $1,000 deduction is worth a few hundred dollars, a $1,000 credit is worth $1,000.FinancialdeflationA fall in the price level: the same basket of goods costs fewer dollars than it did. It sounds like good news for a retiree, but broad deflation usually comes with recession, falling asset prices and wages, and debts that grow heavier in real terms. In the US it has been brief and rare since the 1930s, though single prices, such as electronics, fall for years.FinancialdiversificationSpreading money across investments that do not all move together, so a bad year for one is not a bad year for everything. Owning many holdings is not the same thing — if they are highly correlated they behave like one large position wearing several names.FinancialdividendCash a company or fund pays out to the people who own it. In a taxable account it is income in the year it is paid, whether you spend it or reinvest it — reinvesting changes where the money goes, not whether it is taxed.FinancialdrawdownThe spending-down phase — living off the portfolio rather than adding to it. Watch out: in market commentary the same word means a peak-to-trough decline. In planning prose here it is the withdrawal phase of life, the counterpart to the accumulation years.FinancialDRIPDividend reinvestment plan — dividends buy more shares automatically instead of landing as cash. It changes where the money goes, not whether it is taxed: in a taxable account a reinvested dividend is income in the year it is paid, exactly as if it had been taken in cash.FinancialdurationHow much a bond's or bond fund's price moves when interest rates change, in years. A fund with a duration of 5 loses about 5% when rates rise one point and gains about 5% when they fall one point.Financialearly withdrawal penaltyAn extra 10% federal tax on money taken from a retirement account before age 59½, on top of the ordinary income tax the withdrawal already owes. The law has a list of exceptions; Tutilis applies the 10% and does not model them, so a plan that relies on one will show the penalty anyway.Financialearned incomeMoney you were paid for working — wages, salary, self-employment profit. The distinction is not fussiness: earned income carries payroll tax and builds Social Security credit, while investment income does not. It is also what contribution limits and some credits are measured against.Financialeligible designated beneficiaryAn heir the law lets stretch an inherited retirement account over their own life expectancy instead of the 10-year rule: the owner's surviving spouse, the owner's child under 21, someone disabled or chronically ill, or anyone not more than ten years younger than the owner. Every other individual heir is a designated beneficiary under the 10-year rule.FinancialequityIn investing, stock — an ownership share in a company, or a fund that holds many of them. “US equity” means US stocks. It is a different word from the home equity you have in a house: same spelling, unrelated idea. Equities carry the highest expected return of the common asset classes and the widest swings, and different equity holdings tend to fall together.FinancialexposureHow much of the portfolio moves with a given kind of risk — equity exposure, currency exposure. More exposure means more of the upside and more of the swings; a glide path is a schedule for changing it over time.FinancialFAFSAFree Application for Federal Student Aid — the form that decides what a family is expected to contribute toward college. It reads income and assets, and treats them differently by whose name they are in, which is why college years show up in a plan as more than an expense.FinancialFICAFederal Insurance Contributions Act — the payroll tax withheld from a paycheck for Social Security and Medicare. An employee pays half and the employer pays the other half. It is separate from income tax, which is why a plan can show income tax and payroll tax as different lines. Someone who works for themselves pays both halves instead (SECA).FinancialFiduciarySomeone legally bound to act in your best interest across the whole relationship, putting your interests ahead of their own. A registered investment adviser owes this duty for all the advice it gives. A broker owes a narrower best-interest duty under Regulation Best Interest, which applies when it recommends a security or a strategy rather than for the life of the account. Form CRS says in plain words which standard a firm is held to.Financialfiling statusWhich set of tax tables applies — single, married filing jointly, and the less common ones. It sets the bracket widths, the standard deduction, and most of the income thresholds a plan tests against, so a household's filing status changes what every other tax number means.FinancialFRAFull retirement age — the Social Security age at which you can claim your full Primary Insurance Amount (PIA) with neither an early-claim reduction nor a delayed-claim increase. For many people born in the 1960s it is 67; the calendar year of FRA depends on birth date.FinancialFXForeign exchange — converting between currencies, and the rate that conversion happens at. A plan holding money in more than one currency carries the rate as an assumption like any other, which means part of what a balance is worth depends on something no portfolio choice controls.Financialgeometric meanThe compound growth rate that actually takes a starting balance to an ending one — the return you lived through rather than the one you were quoted. It is always at or below the arithmetic mean, equal only if every year is identical, and further below as volatility rises. This is the mean the product's north-star path is built on.Financialglide pathA planned shift in allocation over time — classically toward bonds as retirement approaches, so risk comes down as the years available to recover from a loss come down with it. The word that matters is *planned*: a portfolio drifting toward whatever has been winning is not on a glide path, it is unrebalanced.Financialgross incomeIncome before anything is taken off — the top of the tax page, above adjustments and deductions. Everything else in a US return is computed downward from here: gross income, then AGI, then taxable income.FinancialgrowthHow fast a value changes from year to year. For an investment it is the same idea as return. Tutilis also gives growth to things that are not investments: a home or other real asset appreciates, and a cash flow such as a salary or an expense rises with inflation or its own rate. Each carries its own growth assumption.FinancialguardrailsSpending rules that react to how the plan is doing — raise spending when the portfolio runs ahead, trim it when it falls behind — instead of holding one number for thirty years. They trade a perfectly steady income for a much smaller chance of running out, and they are the practical answer to sequence of returns risk. Tutilis never applies guardrails in your forecast, which spends what your cash flows and residual strategy say. The Guardrails panel runs the published rules on a simple side path beside your plan, so you can compare them.Financialhigh yieldA bond — usually corporate — rated below investment grade, paying a higher interest rate because the borrower is more likely to default. The name sounds like good news; the trap is that high-yield bonds tend to fall alongside stocks in a sell-off, so the “bond” label does not make them a diversifier. Also called junk bonds or credit.FinancialHNWHigh net worth — shorthand for a household wealthy enough that different planning problems dominate. Surcharge tiers, the investment surtax, and estate questions bind earlier, while running out of money is rarely the binding risk. There is no single official dollar line.Financialhome equityWhat a home is worth minus what is still owed on it. It sits on the balance sheet and counts toward net worth, but it is not liquid: turning it into spendable money means selling, borrowing against it, or downsizing.FinancialhorizonHow far the plan looks — the last year a projection covers. It usually falls out of the ages you set for the household rather than being a number you type. Every statement a forecast makes about ending wealth is a statement about the horizon year, so two plans run to different horizons are not comparable just because both say “ending net worth.”FinancialHSAHealth Savings Account — paired with a high-deductible health plan, and the only common account that is untaxed at all three points: going in, growing, and coming out for medical costs. That is why plans treat it as a retirement account that happens to pay doctors.FinancialhyperinflationA rise in prices so fast that money stops working as a way to hold value. Philip Cagan's 1956 line is a month when prices rise 50 percent or more. Germany in 1923, Hungary in 1946, Zimbabwe in 2008, and Venezuela in the 2010s passed it. A grocery slider on a household plan is a stress for one bill, and it stops far below a month like that.Financiali.i.d.Independent and identically distributed. Each draw comes from the same distribution and remembers nothing of the draws before it. In the forecast, each year's returns are i.i.d., so a bad year makes the next one neither likelier to be bad nor likelier to recover.FinancialinflationA rise in the price level. The same basket of goods costs more dollars than it did. A household plan carries a growth rate on each bill, because one published inflation number is one basket. The reporting rate that restates future dollars into today's dollars is a separate assumption.FinancialInherited Roth IRAA Roth IRA held by someone who inherited it. Qualified withdrawals stay tax-free, but unlike the original owner's Roth it must be emptied on the beneficiary schedule. Because a Roth owner is always treated as dying before the required beginning date, most heirs owe nothing until the tenth year, which lets the money grow untouched until the deadline.FinancialInherited Traditional IRAA traditional IRA held by someone who inherited it. It keeps its tax character, so every withdrawal is ordinary income, but the owner's lifetime RMD rules stop at the death and the beneficiary rules take over: for most adult children, the account must be empty by the end of the tenth year after the death. No one may contribute to it, and withdrawals carry no 10% early-withdrawal tax at any age.Financialinterest rateThe price of money: what a borrower pays each year for the use of it, as a share of the amount borrowed. Short rates follow the central bank's policy rate, and long rates are set by what the market expects short rates and inflation to do.FinancialIRAIndividual Retirement Account — a retirement wrapper you open yourself rather than through an employer. It comes in traditional (pre-tax going in) and Roth (taxed going in) flavors, so naming the flavor matters more than the acronym does.FinancialIRMAAIncome-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount — a surcharge on Medicare premiums for higher-income households. It works in tiers, so one dollar of extra income can raise the premium for a whole year. It looks back at a tax return from a couple of years earlier, which is why a one-off spike (a big conversion, a property sale) shows up as a bill much later.FinancialliquidityHow readily something turns into spendable cash without a loss. A brokerage fund is liquid; a house is not, and neither is a retirement account you cannot reach yet without a penalty. A plan can be solvent on paper and still hit a liquidity problem in a particular year.Financiallocal income taxIncome tax charged by a city, county or school district on top of the state's, as in New York City and many places in Ohio and Pennsylvania. It follows where you live, so it is part of what a move changes.FinanciallognormalA distribution whose logarithm follows a bell curve. Wealth that compounds random yearly returns comes out lognormal over a long horizon: most outcomes bunch toward the low side and a long tail of rare, very large ones stretches to the right, so the average sits well above the typical outcome.FinancialLTCLong-term care — help with everyday living (in-home aide, assisted living, nursing care) rather than medical treatment for an illness. It is modeled separately because ordinary health coverage largely does not pay for it, and because it tends to arrive late in a plan as a large, multi-year expense.FinancialLTCGLong-Term Capital Gain — profit on something held longer than a year, taxed on its own lower schedule instead of at ordinary rates. The holding period is the whole trick: the same sale a day earlier is taxed as ordinary income.FinancialMAGIModified Adjusted Gross Income — AGI with certain deductions added back. It matters far out of proportion to the small adjustment, because MAGI is the figure that triggers cliffs: Medicare surcharges, the investment surtax, and health-subsidy eligibility all test against a MAGI, and each rule uses a slightly different one.Financialmarket crash (stress period)A rare, sharp fall across markets — 2008 and March 2020 are the recent ones — lasting months to a couple of years and arriving perhaps once a decade. Not the everyday case, and not a prediction: it matters in planning because these are the moments correlations rise toward +1, so holdings that usually differ fall together and diversification helps least exactly when it is needed most.FinancialmedianThe middle outcome — half above, half below — as against the average, which a few enormous results can drag upward. Over long horizons the two come apart badly, and the median is the one that answers "what does a typical path look like?"FinancialMonte Carlo simulationThe method behind a multi-path run: draw many randomized return paths, run the plan down each one, and read the spread of what happened. Named after the casino, because the engine is repeated random sampling rather than a formula — which is why two runs of one plan differ a little, and why a success rate is a proportion of sampled futures rather than a promise.Financialmunicipal bondDebt issued by a state or local government. Its interest is normally excluded from federal AGI — which is what the industry label *tax-exempt* means, and all it means. The same dollars still count in MAGI, and most states add back interest from other states' issuers on their own return.Financialnet worthEverything owned minus everything owed — the bottom line of the balance sheet. Worth remembering that it counts dollars, not spendable dollars: a traditional account and a Roth account of the same size are not the same net worth to live on, because one still owes tax.FinancialNIITNet Investment Income Tax — an extra tax (commonly cited at 3.8%) on investment income once income passes a threshold. It applies on top of ordinary income tax rather than instead of it, which is why a plan can show the same dollar taxed on more than one line.FinancialnominalA face-value figure, with no adjustment for inflation. A balance that doubles in nominal terms over 25 years may buy little more than it does today. The counterpart is real, which is inflation-adjusted and closer to what the money will actually buy — always check which one a chart shows.FinancialOASDIOld-Age, Survivors, and Disability Insurance — Social Security's formal name, and the label on the payroll-tax line of a paycheck. Seeing OASDI and Social Security in one plan is not two things; it is the tax and the benefit it funds.Financialordinary incomeIncome taxed at the regular graduated rates — wages, interest, pension payments, and withdrawals from traditional retirement accounts. The point of the label is the contrast: long-term capital gains and qualified dividends get their own, usually lower, rate schedule.Financialpassive incomeIncome that arrives without work being done for it — rent, royalties, annuity-like payouts, a business you own but do not run. In a plan it is a flow that continues into retirement on its own; in tax law it is a specific category, and the two do not perfectly agree.Financialpassive lossA loss from a passive activity, typically a rental or a business you do not actively run. It can offset passive income, but in general not wages or portfolio income, so a loss larger than the passive income is carried forward instead of deducted. The federal statement shows that part as passive loss not deducted.FinancialpensionIncome for life from a former employer's defined-benefit plan, set by salary and years of service rather than drawn from a balance you own. The federal return taxes it as ordinary income, while many states exclude some or all of it, which is why it shows on both tax statements.FinancialpercentileA position along outcomes sorted from worst to best. The 10th percentile (P10) is the outcome only one in ten falls below; the 50th is the median; the 90th (P90) is the one only one in ten beats. Forecasts report P10 and P90 to show the downside and upside without leaning on the single best or worst run.Financialpersonal exemptionA fixed amount per person subtracted from income before the tax is computed. The federal personal exemption has been zero since 2018, but many states still allow one, and that is the row on the state statement.FinancialPIAPrimary Insurance Amount — the monthly Social Security benefit you would receive if you claim at full retirement age (FRA). Claim earlier and the monthly check is permanently lower; claim later and it is higher. Plans often state a PIA and then choose when to claim relative to FRA.Financialpre-taxMoney that went in before income tax was taken — the traditional bucket. Not the same as untaxed: the tax was deferred, not forgiven, and it comes due as ordinary income on the way out. A pre-tax balance is worth less to spend than the same number in a Roth.FinancialpremiumWhat you pay for coverage — a monthly insurance or Medicare cost — and not a bonus or a prize. Confusingly, the same word means "above face value" when applied to a bond. In a plan it is nearly always the insurance sense: a recurring outflow that rises with age and, past a threshold, with income.Financialpremium tax creditThe ACA subsidy that pays part of a marketplace health premium, sized by income. It is the reason a dollar of extra income can cost far more than its tax: crossing the wrong line trims or ends the subsidy for the whole year.Financialpresent valueWhat a future amount is worth today, after discounting for the years between. A multi-year outflow shown as one present value is not a sum of its rows — a dollar due in twenty years counts for less than one due next year. The product's PV rollups and "amount mode" switches are this idea applied to a whole flow.FinancialprincipalThe amount itself — the money lent, borrowed, or invested, as against the interest or growth on top of it. On a mortgage payment it is the part that actually reduces the debt; early on, most of the payment is not principal. (Not *principle*, and not a person.)Financialproperty taxA yearly tax on what real estate is worth, set by the state and the local governments where it sits. It is owed whether or not the property earns anything, and rates differ several times over from one state to the next, so it is one of the larger costs a move can change. In Tutilis a home or other real asset takes its rate from its state and locality, or from a rate you set.Financialprovisional incomeThe test income that decides how much of a Social Security benefit is taxed: adjusted gross income, plus tax-exempt interest, plus half of the benefit itself. It is not AGI and not MAGI, and the tax-exempt line is the trap — municipal-bond interest that owes nothing on its own can still pull more of a benefit into taxable income.FinancialQBIQualified Business Income — the deduction that lets owners of pass-through businesses and the self-employed deduct a share of business profit from taxable income, subject to income limits and a list of excluded trades.FinancialqualifiedA tax word meaning the conditions were met, not a judgment of quality. A qualified Roth withdrawal is tax-free; an unqualified one is not. Qualified dividends get the lower capital-gain rates; ordinary ones do not. Whenever you see it, the interesting question is *which* conditions.Financialqualified dividendA dividend that meets the holding-period and issuer conditions, so it is taxed on the lower long-term capital-gain schedule rather than at ordinary rates. Same cash as an unqualified one; a materially smaller tax bill.FinancialrealAdjusted for inflation — a purchasing-power figure rather than a face-value one. The counterpart to nominal, and the more honest of the two over a long plan: a balance that triples in nominal dollars across thirty years may buy only a little more than it does now. Always check which one a chart is drawn in before comparing a future number to today's prices.Financialrealized gainA gain that has been locked in by selling — and, in a taxable account, the moment it becomes taxable. Until then it is unrealized: the holding is worth more than you paid, but nothing has happened yet and no tax is due. The same word cuts the other way: a loss is only realized once you sell, which is why a downturn you ride through costs nothing you did not sell.FinancialrebalanceSelling some of what grew and buying what lagged, to bring the mix back to the target you chose. Without it a portfolio quietly drifts toward whatever has been winning, and gets riskier than intended precisely when it feels safest.Financialrequired beginning dateApril 1 of the year after a retirement-account owner reaches RMD age, the deadline for their first required distribution. It matters most at death: an owner who died on or after it had started RMDs, so their heirs must keep taking a distribution every year; one who died before it had not.Financialretirement date riskThe chance that a bad market in the last years of work pushes the retirement date back. Near the end of a career the portfolio is so large that one poor year can undo several years of saving, so two savers with the same average return can reach their goal years apart depending on when the poor years fall. Michael Kitces named it in a 2016 article, *Retirement Date Risk and Sequence of Returns*. It is the working-years side of sequence of returns, and holding less stock as the date approaches is the usual answer when the plan needs only a modest return.FinancialreturnWhat an investment earns over a period, as a percentage of what it was worth at the start: price change plus any income it pays. A plan never knows its returns in advance, so it assumes them, and the assumption is an average (μ) paired with a spread (σ). Each holding carries its own.FinancialRMDRequired Minimum Distribution — once you reach an age set by statute, the law makes you withdraw a minimum amount each year from traditional retirement accounts, whether or not you need the money. The withdrawal is taxable income, so RMDs can push a household into a higher bracket or over a surcharge threshold. Roth accounts are generally not subject to them.FinancialrolloverMoving money from one tax-advantaged account to another without it counting as a withdrawal — a 401(k) into an IRA, or leftover 529 money into a Roth IRA. Done right it is a non-event for tax; done as a cash-out and re-deposit it can become a taxable distribution with a deadline attached.FinancialRothA retirement account where you pay income tax on the money going in, and qualified withdrawals later come out tax-free — the mirror image of traditional. Named after the senator behind the law, so the word tells you nothing on its own. Plans care because Roth dollars do not add to taxable income later, which keeps them clear of income-driven cliffs.FinancialRoth conversionDeliberately moving money from a traditional account into a Roth one and paying the income tax on it now, so it grows and comes out tax-free later. The usual reason is a low-income year — between retiring and claiming benefits, say — where the conversion is taxed at a lower rate than the future withdrawal would be. It also shrinks later RMDs.FinancialSAFEMAXThe largest starting withdrawal rate that would have survived the worst historical stretch — the study behind the familiar "4% rule". It is a worst-case floor drawn from history, not a promise about the future, and the number moves with the horizon and the mix it was computed for.FinancialSAIStudent Aid Index — the number the FAFSA produces from a family's income and assets, which colleges use to decide how much need-based aid to offer. It replaced the Expected Family Contribution. Parent assets count lightly, student assets heavily, and retirement accounts not at all.FinancialSECASelf-Employment Contributions Act — the self-employed counterpart to FICA. With no employer to pay half, you owe both halves on net self-employment earnings, so the same dollar of income carries a higher payroll-tax rate than wage income does. This is why plans model wage income and self-employment income as different kinds of earnings rather than one.FinancialSECURE ActThe law (and its sequel) that moved several retirement-account rules: the age RMDs start, catch-up contributions, and how quickly an inherited account must be emptied. Its practical effect on a plan is that dates you may remember from a decade ago are no longer the dates.Financialsequence of returnsThe order the good and bad years arrive in. Two plans with identical average returns can end in very different places if one takes its losses early while money is being withdrawn — selling into a fall removes shares that are then not there for the recovery. It is why retirement risk is not summarized by an average, and why the years either side of the day you stop working carry more weight than their count suggests.FinancialsimulationA multi-path run of the plan — typically Monte Carlo — that samples many randomized market and growth paths and summarizes outcomes (success rate, net-worth cloud, key metrics). Distinct from a single-year or single-path forecast detail view.FinancialsolventAble to meet obligations — in a plan, that the money did not run out before the horizon. It is a lower bar than it sounds: a path can stay solvent while spending far less than intended, which is why solvency and success are not the same test.FinancialSPIASingle Premium Immediate Annuity — one lump sum handed over, income starting almost at once and continuing for life. The plainest annuity there is, which makes it the useful comparison point: whatever a more complicated product offers, it is offering it instead of this.FinancialSSSocial Security — the US government retirement and disability benefit, funded by payroll tax while you work and paid as a monthly check once you claim. How much you get depends on your earnings history (your PIA) and the age you claim relative to full retirement age.Financialstandard deductionThe flat amount most households subtract instead of itemizing individual deductions. You take the larger of the two, so it acts as a floor: below it, an extra deductible expense saves nothing at all, which surprises people who gave to charity expecting a break.Financialstate income exclusionIncome a state leaves out of its tax base even though the federal return counts it. Social Security is the common one, and many states also exclude some or all pension and retirement income, a few exclude capital gains. Each shows as its own row under Adjustments on the state statement.Financialsuccess rateThe share of simulated paths that counted as a success — 85% means 85 of every 100 sampled futures cleared the bar. It is a proportion of sampled futures, not a probability the world owes you, and it means nothing until you know which bar was used.FinancialsurchargeAn extra amount added on top of a normal charge once income passes a line — in planning, almost always the Medicare premium surcharge. It is not a tax rate, so it does not show up in a bracket; it arrives as a higher premium, in tiers, based on a tax return from two years earlier.FinancialsurtaxAn extra tax charged on top of the regular one, usually on income above a high threshold, such as Massachusetts' 4% on income over about $1 million. Unlike a Medicare premium surcharge, it is a tax rate and is computed on the return. Each one shows by name under Surtaxes on the state statement.Financialtax bucketWhich tax rule a dollar lives under, rather than which institution holds it: taxable (taxed as you go), traditional (taxed on the way out), Roth (taxed on the way in). Two accounts showing the same balance in different buckets are not worth the same amount to spend, which is why a plan tracks the mix and not only the total. Legal wrappers differ by country; the three buckets are the durable idea.Financialtax creditAn amount off the tax itself, dollar for dollar — not off income. A credit is worth the same to everyone, while a deduction is worth more to someone in a higher bracket. Some credits are refundable, meaning they can pay out past the tax owed; most are not.Financialtax-deferredTax postponed, not avoided. Growth compounds without a yearly tax drag, and the bill arrives when the money comes out — which is why a large tax-deferred balance and a required withdrawal age are the same conversation.Financialtax-freeNo income tax due when the money comes out — the Roth promise, and the reason Roth dollars keep a withdrawal clear of income-driven thresholds. The word hides a condition: the withdrawal has to be qualified, which turns on age and how long the account has been open. It also hides a scope: municipal-bond interest is tax-free on the federal return yet still counts toward whether Social Security is taxed.Financialtaxable incomeWhat the rates are finally applied to — income after adjustments and deductions, not what you earned. It sits at the bottom of the page: gross income, then AGI, then this. Most people's taxable income is meaningfully smaller than their pay, which is why a bracket alone never tells you the tax.Financialterminal wealthEnding net worth (or a close cousin) at the horizon of the forecast — often the flag year on Key Forecasts (Min / GMF / Max and percentiles) and the spread of endings on Net Worth Distribution. Use it to see whether a what-if left the household richer or poorer at the end of the plan, not as a single dollar target.FinancialTIPSTreasury Inflation-Protected Securities — government bonds whose principal rises with inflation, so the return is stated in real terms. Like other Treasuries, the interest is exempt from state income tax.FinancialtraditionalA retirement account where the tax is paid on the way out — not a description of an old-fashioned account. Money went in before income tax, grew untaxed, and is taxed as ordinary income when you take it out — traditional 401(k)s and IRAs work this way. The contrast is Roth (taxed going in, tax-free coming out) and taxable (brokerage, taxed as you go).FinancialTreasuryDebt issued by the US federal government. The mirror image of a municipal bond: the interest is federally taxable, and states are barred from taxing it — so it is subtracted on a state return rather than added back.FinancialturnoverHow much of a portfolio is sold and replaced in a year — a fund with 30% turnover trades away about a third of its holdings annually. In a taxable account the selling realizes gains, so turnover is a tax cost as well as a trading one. Tutilis does not model turnover; the nearest input is a holding's capital gain distribution yield.FinancialUGMA / UTMACustodial accounts — Uniform Gifts / Transfers to Minors Act — that hold money in a child's name with an adult managing it until the child comes of age. Simple to open, but the money is the child's outright, and for financial aid it counts as a student asset, which is assessed far more heavily than a parent's.Financialunqualified dividendA dividend that does not meet those conditions, so it is taxed as ordinary income at your regular rates. Also called a non-qualified or ordinary dividend. REITs and money-market funds pay mostly this kind.FinancialvolatilityHow much an investment's returns bounce around from year to year, not whether it goes up or down overall. Written σ (sigma) in the product. Two holdings can have the same average return and feel completely different to live through — and high volatility drags the growth you actually compound below that average.Financialvolatility dragThe gap between a portfolio's average yearly return and the return it actually compounds at. Lose 50% and gain 50% and you are down 25%, not even — swings cost growth. The larger the volatility, the further the typical outcome falls below the arithmetic average.Financialwage baseThe annual cap on wages that Social Security's payroll tax applies to. Earn past it and the Social Security piece stops for the year, which is why a high earner's take-home can jump partway through — the Medicare piece has no cap and keeps going.Financialwealth taxA tax on what a household owns rather than on what it earns: a percentage of net worth above an exemption. The United States has none, though proposals recur. In Tutilis it is a hypothetical what-if, so you can see what a levy would cost the plan.Financialwithdrawal orderWhich accounts a plan spends from first — taxable, then traditional, then Roth, or a deliberate mix — as against drawing from everything pro rata. The order changes lifetime tax and how long the money lasts without changing a single dollar of what the household owns, which makes it one of the cheapest levers available.Financialwithdrawal rateYearly spending from the portfolio as a share of its value. A safe withdrawal rate is the highest rate that historically never ran out over a chosen horizon — the origin of the "4% rule". Variable approaches (VPW, guardrails) recompute the rate each year instead of fixing it at the start.FinancialyieldThe income an investment pays out — interest or dividends — as a percentage of its price. It is not total return: a holding can yield 4% and still lose value over the year. Chasing yield alone is how a portfolio quietly takes on risk it was not paid for.Financialμ (mu)The Greek letter the product uses for an assumed average return — in plain speech, about what something earns in a typical year. It is an input to the projection rather than a result of it, and it never travels alone: μ paired with σ is the whole growth assumption, because an average return says nothing about how rough the ride is.Financialρ (rho)The Greek letter the product uses for correlation — how closely two holdings move together, from −1 (opposite) through 0 (unrelated) to +1 (lockstep). Positive ρ between equities is the normal case rather than the exception, which is why assuming zero quietly understates how much of a portfolio can fall at once. The product lets you run the plan with system ρ applied or not.Financialσ (sigma)The Greek letter the product uses for volatility — how widely returns scatter around their average. A bare σ on a chart axis is measuring risk, not return: two holdings can sit at the same height (same μ) and be completely different things to live through. Higher σ also drags the growth you actually compound below the average you were quoted.Financial
