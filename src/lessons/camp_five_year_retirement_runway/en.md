# The five-year retirement runway

Rachael Camp is a CFP® who teaches retirement planning on her YouTube channel, and in [this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JrARrBDY9Hg) she lays out a checklist for the five years before a planned retirement date. Her reasoning is that most problems found just before retirement are not fatal, but each one is much cheaper to fix three, four or five years earlier, while there are still paychecks to fix it with. So she gives one checkpoint for each of the last five years and follows a single couple through all of them. This lesson rebuilds that couple in Tutilis, checks her numbers, and shows each checkpoint on the plan.

Her couple are Dan and Sarah, both 57 in 2027, the first year the plans forecast. They want to retire at 62 with $2 million invested. They have about $1.5 million today, almost all of it in two [[term:traditional]] [[term:401k|401(k)s]], and they put $50,000 a year into those accounts, including the employer match. They take home about $15,000 a month and spend it, and they plan to live on $9,000 a month in retirement. Medicare starts at 65, both of them will claim Social Security at 70, and their [[term:rmd|required minimum distributions]] begin at 75.

Check the household for yourself before reading on. Open [[nav:birds-eye]] on the [Glide path](#canvas-dossier:runway_glide) plan with the **Account** chip selected. [[term:hover|Hover]] over each account to see its balance and what it holds: the two 401(k)s carry nearly all of the $1.5 million, and the joint brokerage account and Savings are small. Then unfold **People & events** and hover over Dan and Sarah to see their ages, the retirement year, Medicare, and the Social Security claim at 70 on their lanes.

[[img:runway-birds-eye]]

*Glide path: the household's accounts on Bird's Eye.*

[[jump:birds-eye]]

She does not give their salaries, their Social Security benefits, where they live, or what their health insurance costs, so the plans supply those. The couple live in Texas, so no state income tax enters the picture. Their salaries are set so that their paychecks cover the $15,000 a month and the $50,000 of savings with little left over, and the plans carry health insurance as its own line rather than inside the $9,000. The table at the foot of the lesson lists everything else.

The lesson opens three plans for the same couple:

- [Glide path](#canvas-dossier:runway_glide) holds about 70% stocks today and steps down to 50% by the retirement date. Every dollar of savings still goes into the 401(k)s.
- [90/10 to the finish](#canvas-dossier:runway_ninety_ten) holds 90% stocks all the way to 62 and only then moves to the same 50/50 mix. This is the allocation she questions at the first checkpoint, and the two plans differ only in the five-year runway.
- [Full runway](#canvas-dossier:runway_full) is the glide-path couple following every checkpoint. From 59 they put into their 401(k)s only what earns the match and send $20,000 a year to a [[term:brokerage]] account instead, and from 60 they live on $9,000 a month and sweep the rest into savings. In retirement they spend Savings and the brokerage account before they touch a 401(k).

## Five years out: the return

The first checkpoint asks how much the portfolio has to earn over the last five working years to reach the goal. For Dan and Sarah the answer is small. Starting from $1.5 million and adding $50,000 at the end of each year, the portfolio reaches $2 million in five years at a return of about 2.95% a year. Her "about 3%" is right.

You can ask the app the same question. Open the Growth Calculator from the bottom bar and choose **Return needed**. Enter the couple's $1.5 million as the starting balance, $50,000 as the yearly saving at the end of each year, and a goal of $2 million in five years:

[[img:runway-growth-calc-return-needed|control]]

*The Growth Calculator's Return needed mode on the couple's numbers.*

[[jump:compounding|output__growth-calculator__growth-calculator.return-needed]]

The calculator answers with the yearly return a balance needs to reach the goal, and it reads about 2.95%. Its second line compares that with the plan's report [[term:inflation]] of 3.5% a year, and the required return is about half a point below it. In today's dollars, then, the couple's $2 million goal asks for no real growth at all. The jump opens the calculator already set to the couple's case. Change the goal or the saving to see how the required return moves.

You can see how small that requirement is on the plan itself. Open [[nav:risk]] and look at **Risk vs. Return** with the **Holdings** chip on, or click the figure below to open it on the [[term:canvas]] as a close-up with the same chips lit:

[[img:runway-risk-glide]]

*Glide path: every holding, and the portfolio star, sits above a 3% return.*

The star, which is the whole portfolio, expects about 6.7% a year on the [Glide path](#canvas-dossier:runway_glide) plan and about 7.2% on [90/10 to the finish](#canvas-dossier:runway_ninety_ten). The Treasury fund expects about 4.6%, and even the money market fund in Savings, the safest holding on the chart, expects about 3.8%. The couple need less than their safest holding is expected to earn, which is what leaves room to take risk off the table.

The balance sheet shows the same cushion in dollars. Open [[nav:bs-table]] on the [Glide path](#canvas-dossier:runway_glide) plan and select 2031, the last working year:

[[img:runway-bs-2031-glide]]

*Glide path: the North Star path at the end of 2031.*

[[jump:balance-sheet|output]]

On the plan's North Star path the couple hold about $2.44 million when the paychecks stop, roughly $440,000 more than the goal. The table rounds to $2.4MM; hover the pill beside Net Worth to see the exact figure. Their salaries pay the bills until then, so the portfolio only grows and receives their savings. The extra comes from expected returns well above the 3% they need. The brokerage account has also grown from $60,000 to about $140,000, because the plans invest the little each paycheck leaves over after spending and the 401(k) savings.

<details class="lesson-math">
<summary class="lesson-math-label">For the math lovers — where 2.95% comes from</summary>
<div class="lesson-math-body">

Write the starting balance as \(B_0 = \$1{,}500{,}000\), the yearly saving as \(c = \$50{,}000\) added at the end of each year, and the return as \(r\). After five years the balance is

\[
B_5 = B_0 (1+r)^5 + c \, \frac{(1+r)^5 - 1}{r}
\]

At \(r = 3\%\) this is about \(\$1{,}738{,}900 + \$265{,}500 = \$2{,}004{,}400\), just over the goal, and the rate that lands exactly on \(\$2{,}000{,}000\) is about \(2.95\%\). Saving at the start of each year instead would lower the rate a little further.

</div>
</details>

That number changes the question about risk. A portfolio that needs only 3% does not need 90% stocks, and stocks are what can push the retirement date back. Near the end of a career the balance is so large that one bad year in the market moves it far more than a year of contributions can. She credits the retirement researcher Michael Kitces with a name for this, [[term:retirement-date-risk|retirement date risk]]. His illustration has two savers with the same average return who receive it in opposite orders, and the one whose bad years come last reaches the goal years later.

Her remedy is not to sell everything and hold cash. That would only work because the couple need so little. A couple who needed 7% or 8% a year would trade one problem for another. For Dan and Sarah she suggests a [[term:glide-path|glide path]], which lowers the stock share a step at a time as the date approaches. The aim is not to remove risk but to make the date depend less on what the market does in the last few years.

You can watch the [Glide path](#canvas-dossier:runway_glide) plan follow its path. Open the Forecaster's **Money Movements** card and choose **Allocations**:

[[jump:glide-path|output__forecaster__forecaster.forecast-detail__si-chart__si-chart.allocations]]

Each strip is one asset class, and each year has two bars: the colored bar is the share the plan actually holds, and the thin black bar beside it is the share the glide path asks for that year. The stock bars step down from 70% in 2027 to 62% in 2029 and 54% in 2031, and from 2032, the first year of retirement, they sit at 50%. The bond bars rise to match. The plan keeps each colored bar on its target by trading inside the two 401(k)s, where a trade costs no tax. In the last few years of the forecast the stock share drifts a few points above its target. The middle of the two clouds is almost the same, about $2.5 million, which is more than the goal because the plan's growth assumptions expect more than 3%. The tails are where the plans differ. ## Four years out: the gaps

The second checkpoint asks where the money will come from once the employer stops paying. She answers it with a map of the years after retirement. The paychecks stop at 62, Medicare starts at 65, Social Security starts at 70 and required withdrawals start at 75, and every dollar of spending that no income covers has to come out of the portfolio.

The cash-flow chart of the [Glide path](#canvas-dossier:runway_glide) plan is that map. Open [[nav:cf-chart]]:

[[img:runway-cash-flow-gap]]

*Glide path: the salaries end in 2031, and Social Security arrives in 2040.*

From 2032 through 2039 there is no income above the line at all. Every year of spending and health insurance is drawn from savings, and nearly all of the savings are in traditional 401(k)s, so every withdrawal is [[term:taxable-income|taxable income]]. Open [[nav:taxes]] and step through those years to see it. The withdrawals put the couple's [[term:magi|MAGI]] at about $183,000 in 2032 and about $160,000 in each of the next two years.

She sees the years from 62 to 70 as an opportunity, because with no salary the couple can choose how much taxable income to show. She points to 62 to 65 as the bigger problem. Until Medicare, the couple buy health insurance on the marketplace, and the help they get with the [[term:premium]] depends on their household income. Under the rules in force for 2026, a household with income above 400% of the federal poverty line gets no premium credit at all. Dan and Sarah's withdrawals put them at about six to seven times the poverty line in each of those three years, far over that line.

She does not suggest working out the tax bill to the dollar four years ahead, because too much will change. A rough map is enough to show where the gaps and the opportunities are, and four more years of salary leave time to act on them. The lesson [The years that change the rules](/learn/milestone_years) walks through each of these birthdays in more detail.

## Three years out: the mix

The third checkpoint fixes what the map found. She asks her clients to stop thinking of the portfolio as one round number. Two couples with $2 million each can have very different freedom in retirement, depending on how the money is split between traditional, [[term:roth|Roth]] and taxable accounts.

Her change for Dan and Sarah is to stop filling the 401(k)s past the employer match. They keep contributing enough to collect the match and send the rest of their savings to a brokerage account, and a Roth account would also serve if they can use one. They give up some of today's tax [[term:deduction]], and in return they get money they can reach at 62 without a penalty and without raising their taxable income. Withdrawals from a brokerage account are taxed only on the gain, so most of each dollar drawn adds nothing to MAGI.

The [Full runway](#canvas-dossier:runway_full) plan makes that change from 2029, when the couple are 59. Each of them now defers only what earns the match, and $20,000 a year goes to the joint brokerage account. That is less than the full $26,000 they stopped deferring, which leaves room for the higher tax she warns about. The plan also includes the next checkpoint's savings sweep. Open [[nav:taxes]] and compare the years before Medicare with the [Glide path](#canvas-dossier:runway_glide) plan:

| MAGI | 2032, age 62 | 2033, age 63 | 2034, age 64 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Glide path | about $183,000 | about $161,000 | about $160,000 |
| Full runway | about $38,000 | about $26,000 | about $78,000 |

[[jump:magi]]

The redirect and the savings sweep keep MAGI under 400% of the poverty line in all three years before Medicare. The [Full runway](#canvas-dossier:runway_full) plan uses the **Tax-account order** withdrawal rule, which spends the taxable accounts, Savings and the brokerage account, before it touches a 401(k). A withdrawal from those accounts adds only its gain and interest to MAGI, so the couple show little income until 2034, when the taxable money runs out partway through the year and the 401(k)s pay the rest. The plan still follows its 50/50 glide path. Its **Rebalance inside tax-sheltered accounts** setting trades stock for bonds inside the 401(k)s each year, and a trade inside a 401(k) is not taxed.

[[jump:withdrawal-order]]

Income this low has a rule of its own. In 2033 the couple sit just above the poverty line, and in states that expanded Medicaid, adults below 138% of the poverty line qualify for Medicaid instead of a premium credit. A planner would usually draw some of the 401(k) in such a year, to stay above that line and to use the lowest tax brackets, and would save the brokerage account for later years. Tutilis does not yet have a withdrawal rule that fills income up to a chosen level, so the plan shows the simpler order.

## Two years out: rehearse

The fourth checkpoint is about whether Dan and Sarah can actually live on the number the plan gives them. A plan can show that $9,000 a month is affordable, but it cannot show whether the couple will be comfortable spending it, and they may find it tighter than they expected or discover that they need less.

Her answer is a dress rehearsal two years before the paychecks stop. The couple keep taking home about $15,000 a month, but $6,000 of it is moved automatically into a savings account where they will not spend it, so only $9,000 reaches the account they pay bills from. Then they watch what happens. Do the bills get paid, does the travel they planned still fit, did they forget any expense, and how does it feel? She also suggests an optional second test for anyone unsure whether they are ready to stop working: take the longest leave the employer allows and spend it at home on an ordinary week, not on a trip, because a vacation is a poor imitation of retirement.

The [Full runway](#canvas-dossier:runway_full) plan carries out the rehearsal from 2030. Its living expenses drop to the retirement budget for 2030 and 2031, and the difference is swept into the Savings account. Open [[nav:bs-table]] on 2031 to see what it builds:

[[img:runway-bs-2031-full]]

*Full runway: Savings at the end of 2031, after two years of the rehearsal sweep.*

[[jump:balance-sheet|output]]

By the end of 2031 Savings holds about $208,000, almost two years of the couple's $108,000 retirement spending. Most of it is the rehearsal sweep. The rest is leftover pay, which this plan invests across the brokerage account and Savings in proportion to their balances. The rehearsal pays for the next checkpoint's cash reserve.

## One year out: the system

The last checkpoint asks a simple question: if the paychecks stopped next month, would everything work? By now the big question of whether they can afford to retire has been answered. What remains are the small ones that, left open together, make people hesitate. These include where the monthly money comes from, how the tax gets paid, which health plan to buy and when, and what to do with the 401(k) at the employer they are leaving.

She sets up four things in the final year. The first is a cash reserve of about a year of spending. The second is health insurance for the three years before Medicare, chosen and priced, with the date employer coverage ends. The third is the monthly transfer, $9,000 into checking on the first of each month, and the way tax on the withdrawals will be paid. The fourth is the paperwork, which covers employer benefits that need replacing, any retirement elections, and the transfers between accounts.

Tutilis can show the first of these. The cash reserve is the Savings balance from the rehearsal, and the [Full runway](#canvas-dossier:runway_full) plan spends it first. Open the Forecaster's **Money Movements** card on 2032, the first year of retirement, and read the table:

[[img:runway-si-2032-full]]

*Full runway: the money moved in 2032. Savings and the brokerage account pay for the year, while Dan's 401(k) trades stock for bonds inside the account.*

Savings falls from about $208,000 at the end of 2031 to about $109,000 a year later and is empty by the end of 2034. The brokerage account falls alongside it, from about $210,000 to about $112,000, because the withdrawal rule draws the taxable accounts in proportion to their balances. The two rows below them are the glide path at work. Dan's 401(k) sells about $121,000 of its stock fund and buys the same amount of its Treasury fund, which keeps the household at 50/50 and is not taxed. The cash is the year of spending she asks the couple to set aside, and with the brokerage account it keeps their income low through all three years before Medicare. The rest of the list is paperwork, and no forecast can do it for the couple.

## Where the tools differ

Her video does not name the planning software she used, and the plans here supply numbers she did not give, so some differences are expected. Four are worth knowing about.

- **Health insurance subsidies.** Tutilis does not calculate the marketplace premium credit. The plans carry an unsubsidized premium for the years from 62 to 65, and Tutilis flags those years with a note that the premium depends on income. The MAGI in the table above is what decides the credit, and comparing it with the 400% line is as far as the forecast goes.
- **401(k) deferrals.** Tutilis does not yet subtract an employee's [[term:pre-tax]] 401(k) deferrals from taxable wages. The working-year tax in these plans is therefore too high by roughly the deferral times the couple's tax rate, and so is the first retirement year, which pays the last working year's tax. The later retirement years are not affected.
- **When tax is paid.** Tutilis pays each year's tax in the following year, so the first retirement year also pays the tax on the last year's salary. A real household pays most of that tax through withholding.
- **Expected returns.** The plans use Tutilis's own growth assumptions, which expect more than the 3% the couple need, so the middle path ends above $2 million. Her point is the 3% the plan needs, and these plans agree with it.

## What to take away

Her checklist works because each check comes early enough to change the outcome. The forecast confirms the three that can be measured:

- The couple need only about a 3% return, so a glide path costs little and narrows the range of outcomes at the retirement date.
- With the money almost all in traditional 401(k)s, every withdrawal from 62 to 70 is taxable income, and the three years before Medicare put their income far over the health-subsidy line.
- Sweeping the difference into savings during a budget rehearsal leaves more than a year of cash on the day the paychecks stop. Spending that cash and the brokerage account before the 401(k)s keeps income under that line in all three of those years, and trades inside the 401(k)s keep the glide path while they do.

The other two checks, living on the budget before it is real and setting up the monthly transfer, are habits rather than numbers, and they are the ones she finds people skip.
