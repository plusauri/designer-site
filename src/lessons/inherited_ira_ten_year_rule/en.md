# Inheriting an IRA: the 10-year rule

An [[term:ira|IRA]] you inherit is not the same account it was for the person who left it to you. It keeps its tax character, so a [[term:traditional|traditional]] IRA still pays out [[term:ordinary-income|ordinary income]] and a [[term:roth|Roth]] still pays out [[term:tax-free|tax-free]] money. But the rules for when the money has to come out change completely at the owner's death. Most heirs must empty the account within ten years, some must also take a distribution every year along the way, and a few can stretch it over their whole life. This lesson follows one working heir through three versions of the same inheritance to show which rule applies, what waiting until the last year costs, and how an inherited Roth differs.

## Meet Jordan

Jordan is 51, single, and still working, with wages of about $95,000 a year and spending of about $70,000. Jordan lives in Texas, which has no state income tax, so every tax figure in this lesson is federal. Jordan has a [[term:401k|401(k)]] of $250,000 and a [[term:brokerage|brokerage]] account of $60,000, and plans to retire at 65.

Jordan's father died in 2025 at 79 and left Jordan his traditional IRA, worth $500,000. He had been taking his own [[term:rmd|required minimum distributions]] for years. Jordan is thirty years younger than his father and is neither disabled nor chronically ill, which turns out to matter a great deal.

The lesson opens three plans. Each one changes a single thing about the inherited account, so the difference between any two is caused by that one thing.

- [Minimum required](#canvas-dossier:inherited_ira_minimum) takes only what the rules require each year and leaves the rest until the deadline.
- [Even over ten years](#canvas-dossier:inherited_ira_even) is the same household and the same account. Each year it takes the balance divided by the years left before the deadline, so the account empties in roughly equal pieces.
- [Inherited Roth](#canvas-dossier:inherited_roth_minimum) is the same household with the same $500,000 inherited as a Roth IRA, left alone until the deadline.

Every number below is read from the forecast's most likely path, with the plan starting in 2027.

## Who inherited decides

The rules sort heirs into three groups, and the group decides almost everything that follows. The IRS spells them out in Publication 590-B.

An [[term:eligible-designated-beneficiary|eligible designated beneficiary]] is the owner's surviving spouse, the owner's own child under 21, someone who is disabled or chronically ill, or anyone who is not more than ten years younger than the owner. These heirs may stretch distributions over their own life expectancy, taking a modest amount every year for as long as they live.

A **designated beneficiary who is not eligible** is any other person. That is usually an adult child or a grandchild, and it is Jordan. Since the [[term:secure|SECURE]] Act, which took effect for deaths after 2019, these heirs must empty the account by December 31 of the tenth year after the death. That requirement is the [[term:ten-year-rule|10-year rule]]. Jordan's father died in 2025, so Jordan's deadline is the end of 2035.

An **estate** or a trust that does not qualify is not a designated beneficiary at all. It gets the shortest schedule.

<details class="lesson-reference" open>
<summary class="lesson-reference-label">Which rule applies to an inherited IRA (deaths after 2019)</summary>
<div class="lesson-reference-body">

| Who inherited | Owner had not started RMDs, and every inherited Roth | Owner had started RMDs |
|---|---|---|
| Adult child or other individual | Empty by the end of year 10, nothing due before | A distribution every year, and the rest by the end of year 10 |
| Spouse, minor child, disabled or chronically ill, or not more than 10 years younger | Life-expectancy payments every year, or choose the 10-year rule | Life-expectancy payments every year |
| Estate or non-qualifying trust | Empty by the end of year 5 | Payments over the owner's remaining life expectancy |

A minor child takes life-expectancy payments until age 21 and then has ten more years to empty the account. A surviving spouse can also treat the IRA as their own, and most do; it then follows the ordinary rules for the spouse's own IRA.

</div>
</details>

## Had the owner started RMDs

The second fact that decides the rule is whether the owner had reached their [[term:required-beginning-date|required beginning date]]. That date is April 1 of the year after the owner reached RMD age, which is 72, 73 or 75 depending on the year they were born. An owner who died before that date had never been required to take anything. An owner who died after it was already drawing the account down every year.

Jordan's father was 79 and well past it. In that case the final regulations, which apply from 2025, require Jordan to keep the withdrawals going: a distribution every year in years one through nine, and everything that is left in year ten. Tutilis works this out from the father's birth year and year of death, so you never have to answer the question yourself.

The yearly amount uses the IRS Single Life Expectancy table. Jordan looks up his own life expectancy at his age in the year after the death, and then subtracts one each year instead of looking it up again. In 2027 the divisor is 35.2, and on [Minimum required](#canvas-dossier:inherited_ira_minimum) the required distribution is **$15,087**. That is about 3% of the account, so the annual requirement barely dents it. It rises slowly each year and is **$23,315** in 2034.

A Roth IRA owner is always treated as having died before the required beginning date, however old they were. That is because the original owner of a Roth never has to take distributions at all. So an adult child who inherits a Roth after 2019 owes nothing at all until the tenth year.

## The tenth year

Taking only the minimum feels like the careful choice, because each year's distribution is small and the account keeps growing [[term:tax-deferred|tax-deferred]]. The cost arrives all at once. On [Minimum required](#canvas-dossier:inherited_ira_minimum), the 2035 column of the Brackets chart carries the entire remaining balance. Jordan takes out **$716,985** that year on top of his wages, and every dollar of it is ordinary income.

[[jump:bracket]]

[[img:inherited-brackets-minimum]]

Jordan's top rate that year is **35%**, against 22% in every year before it. The tax on that distribution is paid the following spring, so the forecast shows **$245,619** of taxes paid in 2036, more than ten times a normal year. Most of the inheritance that came out in 2035 was taxed in the two highest [[term:bracket|brackets]] Jordan will ever see.

## Spreading it out

The rules set a floor and a deadline, but nothing stops Jordan from taking more than the floor in any year. [Even over ten years](#canvas-dossier:inherited_ira_even) takes the balance divided by the years left, which means **$62,676** in 2027 and similar amounts after that. The required distribution is still part of each year's draw, so the floor is always met.

[[img:inherited-brackets-even]]

Each year now carries a slice of the inheritance instead of one year carrying all of it. Jordan's top rate is **24% in every year** from 2027 through 2035 and never reaches the 32% or 35% brackets. Spreading the withdrawals costs more tax in the early years, because Jordan pays 24% on money that would otherwise have waited. But it saves far more at the end. The tax on each year's withdrawal is paid the following year, so those bills fall from 2028 through 2036. Over those years, Jordan pays about **$92,000 less** on [Even over ten years](#canvas-dossier:inherited_ira_even) than on [Minimum required](#canvas-dossier:inherited_ira_minimum).

This is the same idea as filling the cheap brackets, which the lesson [Filling the cheap buckets](/learn/filling_the_cheap_buckets) covers in depth. The inheritance has to be taxed at some point within ten years, so the question is only which brackets it is taxed in. An even pace is a simple rule rather than an optimized one. A year with unusually low income, such as a sabbatical or the first year of retirement, is a good year to take more.

## An inherited Roth

The third plan changes only the account type. [Inherited Roth](#canvas-dossier:inherited_roth_minimum) starts with the same $500,000, but as an [[term:inherited-roth-ira|inherited Roth IRA]]. Because a Roth owner always counts as having died before the required beginning date, Jordan owes no distribution from 2027 through 2034. The whole balance comes due in 2035.

That changes the best strategy completely. Distributions from an inherited Roth are generally tax-free, so there is no bracket to manage and no reason to spread them out. Leaving the account alone lets it grow tax-free for the full ten years. By 2035 it has grown to **$888,664**, and Jordan moves all of it into the brokerage account without adding a dollar to [[term:taxable-income|taxable income]]. His top rate stays at 22% throughout.

The tax-free treatment depends on the Roth having been open for five years, counted from the original owner's first contribution rather than from the death. Nearly every inherited Roth clears that bar. If it does not, only the earnings are taxable until the five years are up, and contributions still come out tax-free first.

## Four years of confusion

When the SECURE Act passed, most people read the 10-year rule as a deadline and nothing more. In February 2022 the IRS proposed that heirs like Jordan, whose parent had already started RMDs, must also take a distribution every year inside the ten. Many heirs had already skipped those distributions. The IRS responded by waiving the 25% penalty on them, one year at a time, for 2021, 2022, 2023 and 2024.

The final regulations made the annual distributions mandatory from **2025**. Two details matter for anyone who inherited in those years. The waived years never have to be made up. And the waiver did not move the deadline, so an heir whose parent died in 2020 still has to empty the account by the end of 2030, with annual distributions in the years before it.

One more group keeps the old rules entirely. An heir whose owner died **before 2020** can still stretch distributions over their own life expectancy, even as an adult child. That includes inherited Roth IRAs, which then do have a required distribution every year. It is tax-free, but it is required, and a planning tool that ignores it will overstate how long the money can stay sheltered.

## What the forecast leaves out

The forecast models the rules for the years it covers. A few things it does not model are worth knowing.

- **The year-of-death distribution.** If the owner died after starting RMDs and had not yet taken that year's distribution, the heir must take it. The forecast starts after the year of death, so it does not include this one payment.
- **Deaths early in the year after RMD age.** Tutilis decides whether the owner had reached the required beginning date from birth and death years. An owner who died between January 1 and March 31 of the year after reaching RMD age had not reached it, but is counted as if they had.
- **Trusts named as beneficiaries.** A trust that qualifies as "see-through" can pass its beneficiaries' rules through. That analysis is not modeled.
- **Inherited 401(k) and other workplace plans.** The same rules apply to them, but the account types here are IRAs. Most heirs roll a workplace plan into an inherited IRA anyway.
- **The best possible schedule.** An even pace is one sensible rule. The cheapest schedule for a real household depends on every other income in those years, and Tutilis does not search for it.

## What to take away

An inherited IRA comes with a deadline, and for most adult children that deadline is ten years. Whether there is also a distribution every year depends on whether the owner had started RMDs. Tutilis works that out from two years you already know. For an [[term:inherited-traditional-ira|inherited traditional IRA]], the deadline is a tax decision in disguise. Waiting until the last year piles the whole balance into the highest brackets, and spreading it out keeps every year in the same bracket. For an inherited Roth the opposite holds, and waiting is usually best because the money grows tax-free and comes out tax-free.
