# Costs you keep paying

Returns are the part of investing nobody controls. Costs are the part you do. Two of them come out of a portfolio every year whether you look or not: the fund's own fee, its [[term:expense-ratio|expense ratio]], and the tax on what a fund pays out in a [[term:brokerage|taxable account]]. Both [[term:compounding|compound]], the same way returns do, so a cost that looks small in one year can be large over a working life and a retirement. Many do-it-yourself investors, and the [[term:bogleheads|Bogleheads]] community in particular, build their portfolios around keeping both low. This lesson follows one couple through three versions of the same plan to show what each cost adds up to and which choices move it.

## Meet the Marshes

Robin is 52 and Jae is 50. Both work, both plan to retire at 62, and they manage their own money. They have about $1.1 million in three funds: a US stock fund, an international stock fund and a bond fund, with a little cash. Between their [[term:401k|401(k)]] deferrals, their employers' matches and a small amount into a [[term:brokerage|brokerage]] account, they save about $45,000 a year.

| Account | Balance | What it holds in the index plan |
|---|---:|---|
| Joint brokerage | $250,000 | US and international stock funds |
| Robin's 401(k) | $420,000 | Stocks, bonds and a little cash |
| Jae's 401(k) | $300,000 | Stocks, bonds and a little cash |
| Two Roth IRAs | $110,000 | US stock fund |
| Savings | $30,000 | Cash |

The plan has no state selected, so every tax figure in this lesson is federal. The lesson opens three plans, and each changes one thing.

- [Index funds](#canvas-dossier:marsh_three_fund_index) holds the three funds as [[term:index-fund|index funds]], with expense ratios of 0.03% to 0.05%.
- [Same mix, active funds](#canvas-dossier:marsh_same_mix_active) holds the same mix in actively managed funds, at 0.5% to 0.9% a year, and the stock funds pass out capital gains every year.
- [Bonds in the taxable account](#canvas-dossier:marsh_bonds_in_taxable) goes back to the index funds and swaps where they sit: the bond fund in the brokerage, the stocks in the 401(k)s.

All three plans give every fund the same return before its costs. That is a choice made for the comparison, not a claim about any fund: an actively managed fund aims to earn more than its index, and some do. The plans ask a narrower question, which is what the costs take when the returns are the same.

## The cost on the fund page

A fund's expense ratio is a yearly percentage of what you hold, taken out of the fund's assets a little each day. It never arrives as a bill. It shows up only as a return slightly lower than the fund's holdings earned. In Tutilis it sits on the holding card, next to the fund's expected return, and every holding of that fund shares it.

## The cost on the tax return

The second cost appears only in a taxable account. A fund that sells holdings at a gain must pass that gain out to its shareholders, as a [[term:capital-gain-distribution|capital-gain distribution]], and the shareholder owes tax on it that year, even though they sold nothing. An actively managed fund trades more, so it [[term:realized-gain|realizes]] more gains; that is its [[term:turnover|turnover]]. A broad index fund rarely sells, so it rarely distributes gains.

Open the federal statement for 2030 on [Same mix, active funds](#canvas-dossier:marsh_same_mix_active).

[[img:costs-statement-active-2030]]

*The 2030 federal statement on Same mix, active funds. About $13,300 of long-term capital gains appears though the Marshes sold nothing: the brokerage funds' distributions, taxed at the 15% long-term rate.*

[[jump:ltcg|output__forecaster__forecaster.forecast-detail__taxes__taxes.federal-statement__line.tax.ltcg]]

A capital-gain distribution is taxed at the long-term capital gains rates, so for the Marshes it costs about $2,000 a year while they work. Their total 2030 tax is about $48,000 on the active plan and about $46,100 on the index plan. That is real money, and for this household it is far smaller than the fee, because only about a quarter of their money sits in the taxable account. A household with most of its savings in a brokerage account pays this cost on most of its money.

Inside the 401(k)s and [[term:roth|Roth]] IRAs the distributions are taxed at nothing, so there turnover costs only its fee.

## Where each fund lives

Which kind of account holds each fund is called [[term:asset-location|asset location]], and it is a cost choice too. Switch to [Bonds in the taxable account](#canvas-dossier:marsh_bonds_in_taxable). The funds are the same low-cost index funds and the overall mix is the same. Only the placement changed.

[[img:costs-statement-bonds-2030]]

*The 2030 federal statement on Bonds in the taxable account. About $15,600 of portfolio interest is taxed as ordinary income, where the index plan, with the bonds inside the 401(k)s, shows about $1,700.*

Bond interest is [[term:ordinary-income|ordinary income]] every year in a taxable account. A stock index fund in the same account pays mostly [[term:qualified-dividend|qualified]] dividends at lower rates, and its gains wait until it is sold. Over the whole plan, this placement pays about $1.5 million in tax, against about $1.23 million for the index plan, and its [[term:median|median]] ending net worth is about $1 million lower.

The common rule of thumb follows: hold bonds where their interest is sheltered, and broad stock index funds in the taxable account. It is a rule of thumb rather than a law, and how much room each account has, and when each will be drawn on, can bend it.

| Plan | Median ending net worth | Success | Fund fees by 2067 (GMF path) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Index funds | about $7.1 million | about 84% | about $57,000 |
| Same mix, active funds | about $3.3 million | about 74% | about $848,000 |
| Bonds in the taxable account | about $6.0 million | about 82% | about $53,000 |

The median falls by more than half on the active plan, and the success rate by about ten points. Most of that is the fee compounding for forty years, and the rest is the tax on distributions and the growth that tax would have earned. If your own active funds earn enough more than their index to cover their costs, the comparison changes; the plan lets you test that by giving a fund a return of its own.

If you also pay for advice, that fee is a separate question with its own trade-offs. [Cost and benefit of an advisor](/learn/cost_and_benefit_of_an_advisor) prices it, and what good advice can be worth.

## Try it on your own plan

- **Find your funds' expense ratios.** Each fund's fact sheet or prospectus states it. Enter it on the holding card, and the Fund expenses row under the cash-flow statement shows what it costs each year.
- **Find their distributions.** A fund's past capital-gain distributions are on its website and on your 1099-DIV. Enter them as the fund's yields, and the federal statement shows the tax in the years it arrives.
- **Check where each fund sits.** Compare the interest and dividends in your taxable account on the federal statement with what the same funds would pay inside your retirement accounts.

## What the plan leaves out

- **Returns are the same before costs.** A fund that beats its index by more than its costs would change the answer; the plans do not assume one does or does not.
- **Distributions are a steady yearly rate.** Real funds distribute unevenly, with large distributions in some years and none in others.
- **Trading costs inside a fund are not separate.** Bid-ask spreads and commissions a fund pays are part of what it earns, not of its expense ratio.
- **No state is selected.** A state income tax adds to the cost of both distributions and bond interest in a taxable account.

## What to take away

A fund's fee and the tax on its distributions both come out every year, and both compound. The fee is printed on the fund page and shows in Tutilis as Fund expenses under the cash-flow statement; the tax shows on the federal statement in the years a fund distributes. For the Marshes, low-cost index funds and bonds kept in sheltered accounts leave several million dollars more at the end, for choices that cost them nothing to make.
