# The three-fund portfolio

A [[term:three-fund-portfolio|three-fund portfolio]] holds three broad [[term:index-fund|index funds]] and nothing else: one for US stocks, one for international stocks, and one for bonds. It is the most common portfolio in the Bogleheads community, and many do-it-yourself investors hold some version of it for their whole lives. This lesson looks at what each fund does, where the idea came from, and what the three of them do in a year like 2008, on the plan of a couple who hold it.

## Three funds

Robin and Jae Marsh are 52 and 50, and they are the couple from [Costs you keep paying](/learn/bogleheads_costs_you_keep) and [Stay the course](/learn/bogleheads_stay_the_course). Their plan, [Three funds](#canvas-dossier:marsh_three_fund), holds about $1.1 million in the three funds and a little cash, spread across two [[term:401k|401(k)s]], two [[term:roth|Roth]] IRAs and a joint [[term:brokerage|brokerage]] account.

| Fund | What it holds | Its job | Share of the Marshes' mix |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Total US stock market | Nearly every listed US company, large and small | Growth | 61% |
| Total international stock | Thousands of companies outside the US | Growth from the rest of the world | 16% |
| Total US bond market | US government and investment-grade bonds | Steadiness, and money to rebalance with | 22% |

Each fund holds a whole market in proportion to its size, so no one company, sector or country decides the outcome. Each costs a few hundredths of a percent a year. The Marshes' plan carries [[term:expense-ratio|expense ratios]] of 0.03% to 0.05%, and [Costs you keep paying](/learn/bogleheads_costs_you_keep) shows what that low cost is worth over their lifetime.

Between the three funds, a household owns a share of most of the world's listed companies and most of the US bond market. The rest of the work is choosing the mix and keeping it.

## Where it came from

The three funds became possible because of [[term:bogleheads|John Bogle]], who founded Vanguard and in 1976 launched the first index fund sold to individual investors. Over the next two decades, Vanguard added a total US stock market fund, a total bond market fund and a total international fund, so a household could own nearly every market through three funds.

The person who turned them into a portfolio was Taylor Larimore. Larimore was a paratrooper with the 101st Airborne Division in the Battle of the Bulge. By his own account he once owned 16 funds, and after reading Bogle he set out to simplify them. In 1999 he described a four-fund version, with cash as the fourth fund, on the online forum where Bogle's admirers gathered. As he simplified further, the cash fell away and the three-fund portfolio took the shape it has today.

That forum began on Morningstar in 1998 and later moved to its own home at [bogleheads.org](https://www.bogleheads.org/). Larimore and Mel Lindauer hosted the first Bogleheads meeting at Larimore's Miami condo in 2000, with Bogle as the speaker, and Bogle took to calling Larimore the "King of the Bogleheads." Larimore wrote *The Bogleheads' Guide to Investing* (2006) with Lindauer and Michael LeBoeuf, and later *The Bogleheads' Guide to the Three-Fund Portfolio* (2018), with an introduction by Bogle. The community's wiki keeps a [page on the three-fund portfolio](https://www.bogleheads.org/wiki/Three-fund_portfolio).

The appeal to the community is easy to see. Bogle's ideas were low cost, broad diversification, and holding on. The three-fund portfolio is those three ideas in the fewest possible pieces. It needs no forecast, no fund picking and no advice, so it suits investors who want to do the work themselves. It also suits households who work with an advisor, and many advisors build portfolios on the same three pieces.

## Why three, not one

A US stock fund on its own is already diversified across thousands of companies. The other two funds add something different: holdings that do not rise and fall at the same time.

Open [[nav:risk]] on [Three funds](#canvas-dossier:marsh_three_fund) and light the Holdings chip.

[[img:tfp-risk-scatter]]

*Risk vs. Return for the Marshes' three funds. Each dot is a fund at its expected return and its volatility. The star is the whole portfolio.*

The two stock funds sit high and to the right: they are expected to earn the most and swing the most. The bond fund sits low and to the left. The star for the whole portfolio sits between them, with less [[term:volatility|volatility]] than either stock fund, because the funds do not move in step. In the catalog's assumptions, US and international stocks have a [[term:correlation|correlation]] of 0.67, so they often fall together but not always by the same amount. US stocks and intermediate Treasury bonds have a correlation of about −0.3, so in many years one rises while the other falls.

[[jump:diversification]]

That is [[term:diversification|diversification]], and it is the one place where a lower risk does not have to cost a lower return. [Correlation and diversification](/learn/diversification_correlation) works through the math.

In the year the stock funds fell by a third or more, the bond fund rose. In the year they recovered, it slipped a little. That difference is what the yearly [[term:rebalance|rebalance]] works with: it sells some of the fund that held up and buys the funds that fell, at their low prices. The next year the stock funds recover, and the shares bought at the bottom recover with them.

The asset mix shows the result. Through the whole crash, each of the Marshes' four classes stays within about two points of its target, because every year's rebalance brings it back. The Marshes do not decide anything in 2032. The plan holds a target mix and rebalances to it inside the 401(k)s and IRAs, where a trade owes no tax, and the crash passes through a portfolio that looks the same afterward as it did before.

## The two decisions

The three-fund portfolio leaves the investor two choices, and they matter more than anything else about it.

**How much goes into bonds.** Bonds are the brake. More of them makes a fall smaller and the long-run growth slower. An old rule of thumb, which Bogle offered as a rough starting point, is to hold about your age in bonds. The Marshes hold 22%, well under their ages, because they are still saving and have ten working years before they need the money. Many households hold more bonds as retirement approaches, and [Glide paths through retirement](/learn/glide_paths_through_retirement) compares the ways to do it.

**How much of the stock share goes abroad.** Companies outside the US make up a little under 40% of the world's stock market. Bogle himself thought a US investor needed little or none, and Vanguard's target-date funds hold about 40% of their stocks abroad. The common answer on the forum falls between, often 20% to 40% of the stock share. The Marshes hold about a fifth of their stocks internationally.

Once both are chosen, they are written into the plan as one target mix. In Tutilis that is the plan's [[term:glide-path|glide path]] under [[nav:assumptions]], with one target held every year and the box ticked to [[term:rebalance|rebalance]] inside tax-sheltered accounts.

## Why it tends to do well

A three-fund portfolio does not try to beat the market. It is built to earn the market's return and keep almost all of it, and three things help it do that.

- **It costs almost nothing.** An index fund's fee is a small fraction of an actively managed fund's. On the Marshes' plan the difference compounds into more than $750,000 of fees over their lifetime, which [Costs you keep paying](/learn/bogleheads_costs_you_keep) shows year by year.
- **It owes little tax.** Index funds rarely sell, so they pay few [[term:capital-gain-distribution|capital-gain distributions]], and with three funds it is easy to keep the bonds in [[term:tax-deferred|tax-deferred]] accounts. That is [[term:asset-location|asset location]], and the third plan in the same lesson tests it.
- **It is simple enough to keep.** The biggest risk to a portfolio is often its owner selling in a fall. Three funds and one written mix leave very little to second-guess in a bad year.

The third reason is the one that makes the other two count. Over 1,000 simulated futures with the 2007 crisis in place, [Three funds](#canvas-dossier:marsh_three_fund) succeeds on about 90% of paths with a [[term:median|median]] ending net worth of about $7.3 million. [Stay the course](/learn/bogleheads_stay_the_course) runs the same couple and the same crash with one change: they move their retirement accounts to cash after the fall. That version ends with a median of about $3.1 million.

<aside class="lesson-saying" data-saying="stay-the-course">

The three-fund portfolio does well only for the investor who keeps holding it. Its mix rebalances itself through a crash, as long as nobody sells it first.

</aside>

## What the plan leaves out

- **Two funds stand in for their markets.** The plan's international fund grows as developed markets do, and a total international fund also holds emerging markets. The bond fund grows as intermediate Treasury bonds do, and a total bond market fund also holds corporate and mortgage bonds.
- **The catalog expects more from international stocks than from US stocks** over the coming decades, 10.1% a year against 7.6%. That is a forward view built from today's prices, not a promise, and over the 2010s US stocks did far better. The point of holding both is not knowing which will lead.
- **The crash is one historical episode.** Other falls have lasted longer or recovered more slowly, and the Sequence-of-returns risk modifiable carries several.
- **The rebalance happens once a year, at the year's prices.** Real rebalancing happens on a particular day, and some investors rebalance only when a fund drifts several points from its target.
- **Many other portfolios work.** Some investors add small-company or value funds, real estate, or inflation-protected bonds, and a single target-date or balanced fund holds a similar mix in one fund. The three-fund portfolio is a sound default, not the only sound answer.

## What to take away

The three-fund portfolio is three broad index funds held at one chosen mix and rebalanced once a year. It came out of John Bogle's ideas and Taylor Larimore's work to make them as simple as possible, and it is the Bogleheads community's most common portfolio. Its funds do not fall together, its costs are tiny, and it leaves almost nothing to decide in a crash, which is when most of its advantage is won or lost.
