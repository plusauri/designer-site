# Too much equity? Stop reinvesting

Morgan is 67, retired, and has done well. The portfolio is $1.84 million: a [[term:brokerage]] account of $800,000, a [[term:ira|traditional IRA]]<!-- term-skip: traditional — one pill for the phrase "traditional IRA" --> of $1 million, and $40,000 in a money-market account. Two index funds, one in each of the first two accounts, have grown over the years into $1.6 million of that, so about nine-tenths of the portfolio is in stock; the rest is a Treasury fund in each account and the money market. That is more stock than Morgan wants to carry at this age, but the brokerage fund sits on a large unrealized gain, and selling a piece of it means paying capital-gains tax on the way out.

There is a quieter way to steer. Both funds reinvest their distributions, which is the setting most people ticked years ago when they wanted more growth. Every year the dividend buys more of the same fund, so the overweight position keeps buying itself. Turning that off does not sell anything. It stops adding.

This lesson opens two plans that are identical except for that checkbox: [Reinvesting](#canvas-dossier:too_much_equity_drip) and [Distributions to cash](#canvas-dossier:too_much_equity_cash). Switch between them on the [[term:canvas]] as you read; every number below is read from **2031**, so set the forecaster there once and it carries across the switch.

## What reinvesting does

In the [Reinvesting](#canvas-dossier:too_much_equity_drip) plan, both index funds keep their distributions. Each year the fund pays its dividend, the share price drops by what was paid, and the dividend buys the shares back. The position ends the year where the fund's total return put it, and the household never sees the money.

The reconciliation makes the round trip visible. In 2031 it shows about $34,000 of *Reinvested Distributions* on the source side and the same $34,000 of *Investments* on the use side.

[[img:too-much-equity-reconciliation-2031-drip]]

The lesson *The dividend you never see* walks through why those two lines have to be equal, and why a reinvested dividend in a brokerage account is taxed anyway.

The consequence for Morgan is simple. The stock funds are the largest positions, they pay the largest distributions, and the distributions go straight back into the stock funds. The tilt toward stock keeps [[term:compounding]].

## Turning it off

In the [Distributions to cash](#canvas-dossier:too_much_equity_cash) plan, the two index funds are switched to pay their distributions out. Nothing else changes: the same funds, the same Treasury sleeves, the same spending, the same Social Security.

In the brokerage account the effect is what you would expect. The dividend arrives as cash, about $9,500 in 2031, and the cash-flow statement lists it under *Distributions* beside Social Security:

[[img:too-much-equity-cash-flow-2031-cash]]

Morgan spends more than Social Security brings in, so the dividend covers part of the gap that would otherwise have been met by selling something. The plan sells about $62,000 that year instead of the $73,000 the reinvesting plan sells, and nothing it sells was bought with a dividend. Notice what the statement does *not* list: the IRA's fund pays a dividend of about the same size, and none of it appears here.

## Inside the IRA

The IRA is where the checkbox does something different, and it is worth understanding.

A dividend paid out of a brokerage account is accessible money. A dividend paid out of a traditional IRA is a *withdrawal*, taxed as [[term:ordinary-income|ordinary income]] and, before 59½, penalized. If the plan let the IRA's dividend land in the household's cash, it would be a way to draw on the IRA that bypassed every rule the withdrawal path enforces.

So inside a sheltered account, "pay it out" means something narrower. The dividend leaves the fund and lands in a **cash sleeve inside the account**, a money-market position that appears on the balance sheet under the IRA the first year it is needed. The money is still Morgan's, still [[term:tax-deferred]], and still in the IRA. It leaves the IRA only the way any other dollar in the IRA does, through a withdrawal that the tax return sees.

## Fourteen years on

By 2040 the difference has compounded in Morgan's favor. Compare the two balance sheets, again with the **Holdings** chip on.

In [Reinvesting](#canvas-dossier:too_much_equity_drip):

[[img:too-much-equity-balance-2040-drip]]

In [Distributions to cash](#canvas-dossier:too_much_equity_cash):

[[img:too-much-equity-balance-2040-cash]]

With reinvestment on, the two index funds hold about $2.47 million of a $2.77 million net worth, or 89%. With distributions paid out, they hold about $2.30 million of $2.78 million, or 83%. The IRA's cash sleeve has grown to about $154,000, the brokerage Treasury fund is larger, and the money-market account is larger, all of it money that would otherwise have bought more stock.

Net worth is almost identical in the two plans. That is the point. Morgan did not give anything up to get a less stock-heavy portfolio, and did not [[term:realized-gain|realize]] a dollar of the brokerage fund's gain to do it. By 2050 the stock share is 90% in one plan and 82% in the other, and the gap is still widening, because a fund that reinvests keeps compounding its own weight for as long as it is left alone.

## When the IRA pays out

From 2032 Morgan is 73 and the IRA must pay a required minimum distribution each year. Here is what [Distributions to cash](#canvas-dossier:too_much_equity_cash) sells in 2035, on [[nav:si-table]]:

[[img:too-much-equity-sales-2035-cash]]

Two things are worth seeing. The IRA's dividend, about $16,000 that year, is invested into the cash sleeve, which is the sweep described above. And the required distribution is drawn from every holding in the IRA in proportion to its size, the sleeve included, so the sleeve is drawn down a little each year as it fills. A custodian would usually take a required distribution from cash first, and a plan that prefers to hold the sleeve for exactly that purpose is a strategy setting rather than a distribution setting; Tutilis does not do it yet.

The sale is taxed like any other IRA withdrawal, because it is one. The dividend that was swept into the sleeve in 2031 was never income in 2031. It becomes income in the year it is withdrawn, which is what a traditional IRA is for.

## What to take away

- A fund that reinvests its distributions keeps buying itself. If it is already the position you have too much of, that is the wrong direction, and it is a direction you chose with a checkbox years ago.
- Turning reinvestment off is a way to stop adding to an overweight position without selling any of it. It realizes no gain and changes no return.
- In a brokerage account the distributions become cash you can spend or redirect. In a sheltered account they accumulate as cash *inside* the account, because a dividend is not allowed to be a back door around the withdrawal rules.
- This is a slow tool. It shifts a few percent of the portfolio over a decade. A [[term:glide-path|glide path]] or an outright sale moves faster; this one costs nothing.
