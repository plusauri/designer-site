# Will Social Security be there? Plan for the shortfall, not for zero

Maya is 58 and Theo is 56. Both plan to stop working in 2033, when Maya turns 65 and Theo turns 63, and both plan to claim Social Security at their [[term:claim-age|full retirement age]] of 67, Maya in 2035 and Theo in 2037. Their Social Security statements put Maya's benefit at $2,900 a month and Theo's at $2,100 in today's dollars. Together that is about $60,000 a year, and it is the largest single source of income they will have in retirement. They have $1.2 million saved and plan to spend $102,000 a year.

A friend has told them to leave Social Security out of the plan entirely, because "it won't be there." The friend is not alone. In a 2023 Nationwide survey, 45% of Gen Z adults and 39% of millennials said they expect to get none of the Social Security benefits they have earned.

The worry has a real source. The Social Security trust fund is projected to run out in the 2030s, and every year the Trustees publish the date. What the headlines usually leave out is what happens on that date, and it is not what Maya and Theo's friend thinks. This lesson looks at the official numbers, lays the projected cut over Maya and Theo's plan with a what-if in [[nav:transforms]], and compares the cost of planning for that cut with the cost of planning for nothing.

It opens two plans:

- [As scheduled](#canvas-dossier:ss_solvency_as_scheduled) is Maya and Theo's plan as it stands. It counts on every scheduled dollar of both benefits.
- [Ready for a shortfall](#canvas-dossier:ss_solvency_ready) is the same couple spending $96,000 a year instead of $102,000. Nothing else changes.

## What the trust fund is

Social Security is mostly pay-as-you-go. The payroll tax that workers and employers pay today, the 12.4% [[term:fica]] Social Security tax on wages up to the annual cap, pays today's benefits. The trust funds hold the surplus that built up when payroll taxes brought in more than benefits cost, most of it after Congress raised taxes and trimmed benefits in 1983.

There are two funds. The Old-Age and Survivors Insurance fund pays retirement and survivor benefits, and the Disability Insurance fund pays disability benefits. Together they are called [[term:oasdi|OASDI]]. The reserve is now being drawn down because benefits cost more than the program takes in. When it reaches zero, benefits can only be paid from the taxes that keep arriving.

That last sentence is the whole lesson in miniature. A depleted trust fund is an empty reserve. The payroll tax on every paycheck in the country does not stop when the reserve does.

## When it runs out

The Social Security Board of Trustees publishes a report every year. The 2026 report, released on June 9, 2026, projects:

| Fund | Reserves run out | Share of scheduled benefits payable then | Share payable in 2100 |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Old-Age and Survivors Insurance (retirement) | Late 2032 | 78% | 62% |
| OASI and DI combined | Mid 2034 | 83% | 65% |

The Congressional Budget Office makes its own projection, and it is somewhat darker. CBO's 2026 long-term projections have the retirement fund exhausted in fiscal year 2032 and the combined funds in fiscal year 2033, with a benefit reduction of 23% in 2034 that grows to 37% by 2100.

The two retirement-fund dates are closer than they look. The Trustees count calendar years and CBO counts fiscal years, which end in September, so the Trustees' "fourth quarter of 2032" falls in CBO's fiscal 2033.

The combined figure is the one most often quoted, but it assumes Congress lets the retirement fund borrow from the disability fund. Congress has shifted money between the two funds before, in 1994 and again in 2015, so the assumption is reasonable, and it is still an assumption.

## How sure are those dates?

A single depletion year sounds more certain than it is. It depends on how many people work, what they earn, how long retirees live, how many children are born and how fast prices rise. The Trustees test that uncertainty by running 5,000 simulations, each with its own path for those assumptions, and reporting how the depletion year spreads:

| Probability the combined reserves are gone | By |
|---|---|
| 50% | 2034 |
| 80% range | 2033 to 2037 |
| 95% range | 2032 to 2039 |

In other words, if Congress changes nothing, a shortfall arriving sometime in the 2030s is close to certain. What is uncertain is the year, and how large the cut would be. A plan cannot know either, which is exactly the kind of question a what-if is built for.

<details class="lesson-reference" open>
<summary class="lesson-reference-label">Reference — why the date moves from year to year</summary>
<div class="lesson-reference-body">

The 2026 report widened the 75-year shortfall from 3.82% of taxable payroll to 4.42%, mostly because the Trustees lowered their long-run fertility assumption from 1.90 to 1.75 children per woman. Fewer future workers means less future payroll tax.

Two recent laws also moved the numbers a little. The Social Security Fairness Act of January 2025 repealed the Windfall Elimination Provision and the Government Pension Offset, which raised benefits for some public-sector retirees and cost the program 0.14% of taxable payroll over 75 years. The 2025 reconciliation law added a deduction for older taxpayers, so less income tax is collected on benefits and less of that tax flows back to the trust funds. The Chief Actuary estimated its effect at 0.16% of payroll. The 2026 report still puts combined depletion in the third quarter of 2034, because faster near-term wage growth offset the loss.

</div>
</details>

## Why not zero?

The law answers this directly. The Antideficiency Act forbids a federal agency from paying out more than the law authorizes, and for Social Security the limit is what the trust fund holds. Once the reserve is empty, the program can pay only what incoming payroll taxes and the tax on benefits bring in. The Congressional Research Service describes two ways that could work: full checks paid late, or reduced checks paid on time. The Trustees' "83% payable" measures how much of each check the incoming money would cover.

History points the same way. The one time a trust fund actually ran dry, every check was still paid.

<aside class="lesson-did-you-know">
<p class="lesson-did-you-know-label">Did you know?</p>
<div class="lesson-did-you-know-body">

On November 5, 1982, Social Security's retirement fund ran out of money. The Treasury paid that month's checks with a $581 million loan from the disability fund. A national commission chaired by Alan Greenspan was already at work, and in April 1983 Congress raised the full retirement age to 67 in steps, taxed up to half of benefits for higher-income retirees and moved scheduled payroll tax increases earlier. Nobody's check stopped.

</div>
</aside>

Congress can close the gap in the other direction too. The Social Security actuaries estimate how much of the 75-year shortfall each common proposal would close. Their latest estimates are measured against the 2025 projections, when the shortfall was a little smaller than it is now. Removing the cap on taxable wages without raising benefits closes about two thirds of it, which is the what-if [Erin On The Wage Cap](/learn/erin_on_wage_cap) models. Raising the payroll tax from 12.4% to 16.4% closes all of it. A lower cost-of-living formula closes about a sixth, and raising the full retirement age to 69 closes about a third. Most packages combine several of these, and some of them would change benefits for today's workers as well.

A plan cannot know which of these Congress will choose. What it can do is ask whether it still works under current law if Congress does nothing, which is the cut.

## Lay the cut over a plan

A plan's Social Security benefit is the amount the statement shows, grown each year by the [[term:cola|cost-of-living adjustment]]. To ask what happens if only part of it is paid, open [[nav:transforms]] and unfold **Modifications**. Its Modifiables card opens on the **Global** tab, which lists the what-ifs that apply to the whole plan. On a US plan with a Social Security benefit, one of them is **Social Security shortfall**. Select it:

[[img:ss-solvency-modifiables|control]]

The editor beside the card holds the cut. Switch it on and it opens on the Trustees' combined projection: 83% of each benefit paid from 2034. Move **Share of benefit paid** to try a deeper or a shallower cut, and move **Cut starts** to try an earlier or a later year. The year slider carries the same anchors as the Forecaster's year slider, so each retirement is one click away.

[[img:ss-solvency-editor|control]]

The shortfall applies to every Social Security benefit in the plan, from the year you choose onward. It leaves the rest of the benefit alone: the claim year, the cost-of-living increases and the tax on benefits all follow the smaller check. Nothing in the plan itself changes. Transforms shows the plan as authored under *Original* and the plan with the cut under *Modified*.

Maya claims in 2035 and Theo in 2037, both after the projected depletion in 2034. For them, a cut that starts in 2034 applies to every check they will ever receive, which is the Trustees' scenario exactly.

## What the cut costs them

Here is the cash-flow comparison in 2040, the year Maya turns 72 and Theo turns 70:

[[img:ss-solvency-cash-flow]]

In 2040 the plan as authored pays Maya $50,401 and Theo $36,497, in 2040 dollars. With 83% paid, Maya receives $41,833 and Theo $30,293. That is about $14,800 a year less, and every dollar of it now has to be sold from savings. It repeats every year for the rest of their lives.

The balance-sheet comparison shows what that does over time. The two sides are identical until 2034, and after that the plan with the cut grows more slowly:

[[img:ss-solvency-balance-sheet]]

On the [[term:gmf|GMF]] path, the forecast that takes each year's expected return, net worth in 2045 is $2.74 million as authored and $2.55 million with the cut. By the end of the forecast in 2062 the gap is about $860,000, $3.33 million against $2.47 million.

The expected path is only one of many. The [[term:monte-carlo]] simulation runs the plan through 1,000 possible market histories and counts how many end with the money lasting. Here is that count, the [[term:success-rate]], for the plan as authored and with the cut:

[[img:ss-solvency-odds-trustees]]

The chance of success falls from about 92% to about 82%. That is a real loss, and it is also a long way from disaster.

## Try other numbers

The Trustees' figure is one projection among several. The editor takes any share and any year, so each projection in this lesson can be tried in a few seconds. These are the success rates for both plans over 1,000 paths:

| Social Security pays | As scheduled ($102,000) | Ready for a shortfall ($96,000) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Everything scheduled | 92% | 98% |
| 83% from 2034 (Trustees, combined funds) | 82% | 92% |
| 78% from 2033 (Trustees, retirement fund alone) | 78% | 89% |
| 77% from 2034 (CBO, combined funds) | 78% | 89% |
| 65% from 2034 (Trustees, share payable in 2100) | 68% | 81% |
| Nothing at all | 29% | 38% |

The editor holds one share for every year after the cut starts. The official projections instead have the payable share sliding slowly downward over the century. A share of 65% from 2034 is a harsher test than any single year of the Trustees' path until about 2100, so it works as a floor for the later decades.

## Planning for nothing

Now try the friend's advice. Set **Share of benefit paid** to 0% and start it in 2027:

[[img:ss-solvency-odds-nothing]]

The chance of success falls to about 29%. On the expected path, Maya and Theo run out of money in 2055, when Maya is 87.

To make a plan with no Social Security as safe as their plan with it, Maya and Theo would have to cut their spending from $102,000 a year to about $65,000. That is about $37,000 a year, or more than a third of everything they spend, for the rest of their lives, to insure against a check that stops completely.

That is the cost of "count on nothing." It sounds cautious, but it gives up a third of a retirement to prepare for an outcome that current law does not produce.

## Ready for a shortfall

Now switch to [Ready for a shortfall](#canvas-dossier:ss_solvency_ready). This plan spends $96,000 a year, $6,000 less than the plan as scheduled. Lay the same cut over it, 83% from 2034:

[[img:ss-solvency-odds-ready]]

With the cut, this plan succeeds about 92% of the time. That matches Maya and Theo's original plan with Social Security paid in full. A 6% trim in spending buys back everything the Trustees' cut takes away. If Congress fixes the program and the full benefit arrives, the trimmed plan simply succeeds about 98% of the time and leaves more behind.

Spending less is not the only lever. Working another year or two, or delaying the claim past 67, moves the plan in the same direction. [Claim early vs live long](/learn/springfield_claim_longevity) shows what claim timing does to a household, and a shortfall is a good reason to run those what-ifs together. The approach does not change: pick the cut you want to be ready for, lay it over the plan, and adjust until the Modified side is a plan you would accept.

## What this does not claim

The shortfall what-if is a stress test, and it is not a forecast of what Congress will do. Every solution Congress has passed before has changed the program in its own way. A future law might protect current retirees and change benefits for younger workers, raise the full retirement age, change the cost-of-living formula, raise taxes, or combine all four. None of those looks like a flat cut applied to everyone, and any of them could affect Maya and Theo differently than the what-if does.

The what-if also applies one share from one year onward. The Trustees' projections have the payable share falling gradually, and CBO's fall faster, which is why the table above tests a lower share too.

What the lesson does claim is narrower and more useful. Under current law, the trust fund running out means a smaller check. A plan can be tested against that smaller check today, and being ready for it usually costs far less than giving the benefit up entirely.
