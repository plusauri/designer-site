College is often a household’s **largest multi-year outflow** after housing. This lesson starts with
**what a 529 plan is**, then looks at **what college roughly costs**, how **saving into a 529** shows
up in a plan, and a few **ownership and leftover-money** points that change planning more than
people expect.

The live plan on the right is **Springfield College 529**. Homer and Marge have two children,
**Bart** and **Lisa**, as dependents. Each child has a **parent-owned 529**, a **college expense**
cash-flow template linked to that account, and **\$500 a month going in** until that child starts
school.

## What a 529 is (in plain language)

A **529 plan** is a tax-advantaged account for education savings (and some related education uses
under current law). In broad terms:

| Idea | Plain meaning |
|------|----------------|
| **Contributions** | After-tax money you put in (no federal income tax deduction; some states offer a state break — **state-specific**). **Modeled here** — see **Putting money in** below |
| **Growth** | Earnings can grow **federal income tax deferred** while in the plan |
| **Qualified withdrawals** | For qualified education expenses, earnings can come out **federal income tax free** (and often state-tax free) |
| **Non-qualified withdrawals** | Earnings may be taxed and can face an **additional tax** — rules and exceptions exist; check current IRS guidance |
| **Investments** | You choose among the **plan’s** menu (often age-based or static fund options) — not every broker’s full lineup |

This product models a **529 account type** with a **beneficiary** (the child it is for) and can
**link college spending** to a 529, so the forecast **draws that account first** in the linked
college years. It does **not** simulate every state tax [[term:deduction]], plan fee schedule, or
financial-aid formula.

**Where to look:** open [[nav:dossier]] → Assets. You will find **Bart College 529** and **Lisa
College 529**, both of type **College 529**. Each holds one U.S. stock fund (**Bart 529 Equity** and
**Lisa 529 Equity**), and each balance is a **partial** pre-fund. It is enough to get started and
not enough to pay four years of college on its own.

The monthly saving that tops each account up is authored on the account itself, in a section called
**Contributions**. Only the desktop app shows that section (**Assets → Bart College 529 →
Contributions**). On the live plan beside this lesson, the same saving appears as a **College
saving** row under **Transfers** on [[nav:birds-eye]]. The section *Putting money in* below walks
through it.

## Ballpark costs

The table below gives **order-of-magnitude** figures. These are rough sizes, rounded to show whether
a year of school costs closer to \$5,000, \$30,000 or \$90,000, rather than precise prices. They are
the same figures the app shows in its **College expense** help, so the lesson and the editor do not
quote different numbers at you.

Published “sticker” prices are only a starting point. Many students pay less after grants, and
**living costs** matter as much as tuition. In the product, a college template takes a **full annual
budget** (tuition + housing/food or a commuter equivalent), a **start year** (often near age 18 for
the linked student), **years of attendance**, and a **cost-growth** assumption that compounds the
series. **Net price after aid** can land well below sticker for the same school. The table shows
rough sizes and says nothing about any one household’s aid package.

| Path | Tuition & fees (rough) | All-in per year (rough) |
|------|-------------------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| Community college | ~$4k | ~$15–22k |
| State college (in-state) | ~$12k | ~$28–35k |
| Private (avg) | ~$45k | ~$60–65k |
| Elite / highly selective | ~$65–75k+ | ~$85–95k+ |

Figures are U.S. ballparks for about 2025–26. They are **not** forecasts of what Bart or Lisa will
pay.

**On this sample:** each college template uses about **$32k/year** in plan-year dollars — inside the
**state all-in** band — for **four** academic years. Bart starts around **2032**, Lisa around
**2034**. Amounts grow with the template’s cost-growth assumption.

## Putting money in: saving is not spending

The 529 balances did not appear out of nowhere. Each 529 carries a **contribution** — \$500 a month,
\$6,000 a year — and it stops the year that child starts school: **2032** for Bart, **2034** for Lisa.

### The thing worth checking yourself

A contribution is a **transfer**, not a spend. The money leaves what the household can spend and
turns up inside the 529 — it does not leave the household.

The check that shows it: **net worth does not move when the saving starts.** Not "moves a little" —
it is the *same number*, because nothing was consumed and nothing was earned. The family moves
\$12,000 a year out of its everyday money and into the two 529s. All of it is still the family's
own money, so the total the family owns stays the same.

That is why these rows appear under **Transfers** rather than beside groceries and the mortgage —
see [[nav:birds-eye]] → Assets & liabilities with **Cash Flow** on, where a **Transfers** section sits
below the balance and is deliberately *not* part of `Net = Assets − Liabilities`. On
[[nav:cf-chart]], hovering a year shows a **⇄ Transfers** line under In and Out, never inside Out.

[[img:springfield-college-529-birds-eye-transfers]]

*[[nav:birds-eye]] → **Assets & liabilities** with **Cash Flow** on, **Transfers** expanded. Both
**College saving** rows sit here — in neither the Assets column nor the Liabilities column. Saving
for college is not an asset the family will receive and not a debt they owe. It is their own money
moving from one account to another, so it sits beside the balance rather than inside it.*

### So why bother?

If it does not change net worth, why model it — or do it? Because of the **wrapper**. Money inside a
529 grows without the annual tax drag it would carry in a taxable account, and [[term:qualified]] withdrawals
come out federal-income-tax free. Same dollars, different tax treatment, and the gap compounds
quietly over a decade. It is a real effect and a modest one at this household's scale — the wrapper
is worth something, not everything.

### What it actually buys: covered years

The bigger difference is not the tax break at all. It is whether the account still has money in it
when the bill arrives.

Follow **Bart College 529** through his four college years (**2032–2035**). On [[nav:forecaster]],
open [[nav:bs-table]] and move the selected year from **2031** to **2035**. The account is listed by
name, and its balance falls each year as tuition is paid out of it.

With \$500 a month behind it, Bart's account still has money in it at the end of his second year
and **runs dry during his third** (2034). The rest of his college is paid from ordinary household
assets. Without the monthly saving, the same account would run dry during his **second** year.
Lisa's account follows the same pattern two years later. It lasts into her third year (2036) with
the saving and only into her second without it. In this plan, the saving pays for about **one more
year of college** for each child.

That is the practical case for a 529: a funded account absorbs a large, dated, unavoidable expense
instead of that expense landing on the rest of the plan.

## Owner, beneficiary, and control

| Role | Who it usually is | What it means |
|------|-------------------|---------------|
| **Owner** (account holder) | Often a **parent** | Controls investments, contributions, and **who the beneficiary is** |
| **Beneficiary** | The student the account is **for** | The person whose education the money is intended to pay |

You *can* structure accounts so a child is closer to the control of the money (for example some
custodial setups). That can take **parents out of the decision loop** once the child can act on the
account — a family and legal choice, not only a tax one.

**Why parents often stay as owner**

- Keep the ability to **change the beneficiary** among eligible family members if one child does not
  need the full balance.
- Keep **investment and withdrawal** decisions with the people funding the plan.

The sample’s 529s sit on the **household** balance sheet with the parents’ other accounts — the
usual “parent owns, child is beneficiary” story for teaching.

## Financial aid: what FAFSA says now

Older advice said “student-owned 529s crush aid.” **Current federal FAFSA treatment differs:**

| Situation (dependent student on the FAFSA) | Typical FAFSA asset treatment |
|--------------------------------------------|-------------------------------|
| **Parent-owned 529** for the student | Reported as a **parent** asset |
| **Dependent student–owned 529** | Also reported as a **parent** asset (not as a high-rate student asset) |
| **UGMA/UTMA** and many other **student** assets | Still **student** assets, assessed at a **much higher** rate than parent assets |
| **Independent** student who owns a 529 | Reported as a **student** asset |

Parent assets are assessed at a **low maximum rate** (commonly cited around **5.6%** of countable
assets in the federal formula — exact brackets and allowances change). Student assets are assessed
far more aggressively (commonly cited around **20%**).

**Takeaways for this lesson**

1. For a **dependent** student, parent-owned vs student-owned **529** is **not** the old “5.6% vs
   20%” story on the FAFSA — both 529 shapes generally land as **parent** assets.
2. **Control and beneficiary flexibility** still favor clear parent ownership for most families.
3. Putting college money in a **taxable [[term:brokerage]] in the student’s name**, or in **[[term:ugma-utma|UGMA/UTMA]]**, can
   still hit aid harder than a 529.
4. **Institutional forms** (for example CSS Profile) and **state** rules can differ — verify with the
   school and current [StudentAid.gov](https://studentaid.gov/) guidance.
5. This app does **not** compute [[term:sai]] or package aid; treat aid as a **planning constraint**, not a
   chart on this canvas.

## One plan can serve the next kid

<aside class="lesson-did-you-know">
<p class="lesson-did-you-know-label">Did you know?</p>
<div class="lesson-did-you-know-body">

A 529 is not tied to the child it was opened for. The owner can change the **beneficiary** to
another member of that child's family, and the switch itself generally costs no income tax and no
penalty. The family reaches further than most people guess. Brothers and sisters count, and so do
first cousins and even the child's own parents.

</div>
</aside>

So if you pre-fund **Bart** and he needs less school, or none, what is left can often be redirected
toward **Lisa** or another eligible relative. The money stays in the plan and keeps its tax
treatment, and nobody has to take a non-qualified withdrawal.

**On this sample:** two separate 529 accounts and two college templates make the **per-child** story
visible. In real life you might use one account and change the beneficiary over time, or keep
parallel accounts — process and plan menus differ by state program.

**Where to look:** open each college template and note the **Student** person and the **funding**
link to that child’s 529 account; open the account and note its **Beneficiary** names the same
child. Point a college template at a sibling's 529 and the Issues panel warns that the student and
the beneficiary disagree.

## Leftover 529 → Roth IRA: a capped valve

Under **[[term:secure|SECURE]] 2.0**, unused 529 money can sometimes move into a **Roth IRA for the beneficiary**,
not as unlimited free Roth funding. The valve is deliberately small. The account has to be old, the
money has to have been in it for years, each year's move is held to the Roth contribution limit, and
the whole lifetime total is capped at **$35,000**.

<details class="lesson-reference" open>
<summary class="lesson-reference-label">Reference — the rollover conditions</summary>
<div class="lesson-reference-body">

| Condition | Typical rule of thumb |
|-----------|------------------------|
| **Account age** | 529 must have been maintained for the beneficiary for **at least 15 years** |
| **Seasoning of money** | Amounts (and earnings) from contributions in the **last five years** are generally **ineligible** |
| **Annual limit** | Rollover counts against the beneficiary’s **annual Roth IRA contribution limit** for that year |
| **Lifetime cap** | **$35,000** lifetime per beneficiary (aggregate from 529s into Roth IRAs) |
| **Whose Roth** | Roth must be owned by the **529 beneficiary** |
| **Earned income** | Beneficiary still needs **compensation** (and other usual Roth contribution rules that apply) |

These are rules of thumb. Verify current IRS and plan rules before acting.

</div>
</details>

So a 529 is **primarily** an education vehicle with a **capped** retirement-side relief valve — useful
against “what if they don’t go,” not a substitute for maxing workplace retirement accounts first.

[Roth conversion in the window](/learn/roth_conversion_window).

## Fees and diversification across two kids

Some **state 529 menus** charge fees in ways that make **each investment option** in an account
costly (details vary widely by program — **always read your plan’s fee schedule**).

In this section, **growth** means stock funds, which can rise more over time but swing harder
along the way. **Ballast** means steadier holdings such as bond or stable-value funds. The word
comes from sailing, where ballast is the weight low in a ship that keeps it steady.

**Idea:** suppose you want roughly **60% growth / 40% ballast** for two young children and the plan
charges a noticeable fee **per fund option held**. Putting **60/40 in each child’s account** can
mean **four** fee-bearing options. Putting **mostly growth in one child’s account** and **mostly
ballast in the other’s** can keep a similar **household** risk mix with **fewer** options — if the
plan’s fee design rewards that.

| Approach | Household mix | Fee risk (when fees are per option) |
|----------|---------------|-------------------------------------|
| 60/40 in Bart’s 529 **and** 60/40 in Lisa’s | Same overall tilt | More options → potentially **higher** fees |
| ~100% growth in Bart’s + ~100% ballast in Lisa’s | Similar overall tilt | Fewer options → potentially **lower** fees |

This sample does **not** model plan fees; both 529 holdings are simple equity blends for teaching
structure. The fee point is a **real-world checklist item**, not a chart claim.
