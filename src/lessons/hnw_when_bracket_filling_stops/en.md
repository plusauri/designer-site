# When bracket-filling stops working

Most advice about a [[term:roth-conversion|Roth conversion]] describes the same picture. A household stops working, its income drops, and for a few years before Social Security and required distributions begin, the low tax [[term:bracket|brackets]] sit empty. Converting enough each year to fill them moves money into a Roth at a low rate. That picture assumes the household's income really does drop. A large taxable portfolio pays interest and dividends every year whether anyone works or not, and at $20 million even 3% is $600,000 a year. This lesson follows one [[term:hnw]] couple through three plans to show where bracket-filling stops working, what municipal bonds change and what they cost, and the reasons a conversion can still make sense at the top of the brackets.

## Meet Helen and Arturo

Helen is 66 and Arturo is 64. They sold their business, retired, and now have about $27 million. They spend about $420,000 a year, and both will claim Social Security at 70. The plan has no state selected, so every tax figure in this lesson is federal. [Where to retire on $20 million](/learn/hnw_where_to_retire_state_taxes) brings the state in.

| Account | Balance | What it holds |
|---|---:|---|
| Joint [[term:brokerage]] | $18.5 million | A US stock index fund ($7 million), an international stock fund ($1.5 million), and an intermediate Treasury fund ($10 million) |
| Helen's [[term:traditional]] IRA | $5.5 million | A US stock index fund |
| Helen's [[term:roth]] IRA | $400,000 | A US stock index fund |
| Cash | $600,000 | A money market fund |
| Home | $2.5 million | Paid off |

They spend from the taxable account first and leave the [[term:ira|IRA]] alone, the order most planners suggest. Each plan carries a Roth conversion policy for every forecast year from 2027 through 2034, the years before Helen's first required distribution in 2035.

- [Treasuries, fill to 24%](#canvas-dossier:vance_fill_24) asks the plan to convert enough each year to fill [[term:ordinary-income|ordinary income]] to the top of the 24% bracket.
- [Munis, fill to 24%](#canvas-dossier:vance_munis_fill_24) is the same plan with the $10 million bond sleeve in a national municipal bond fund instead of Treasuries.
- [Fixed $500k a year](#canvas-dossier:vance_fixed_conversion) keeps the Treasuries and converts a fixed $500,000 a year instead of filling a bracket.

## Income they cannot turn off

A taxable portfolio pays three kinds of income without being asked. A bond fund pays interest, which is ordinary income. A stock fund pays dividends, which are mostly [[term:qualified-dividend|qualified]] and taxed at lower rates. A fund that sells holdings at a gain passes the gain out as a [[term:capital-gain-distribution|capital-gain distribution]]. None of it waits for the household to decide it needs the money.

For Helen and Arturo the bond sleeve is the large one. Ten million dollars in a Treasury fund paying about 4.4% is about $440,000 of interest a year, and the cash adds more. Open the federal statement for 2027 on [Treasuries, fill to 24%](#canvas-dossier:vance_fill_24).

[[img:hnw-floor-statement-2027]]

*The 2027 federal statement. Portfolio ordinary income is about $498,000. There is no IRA withdrawal at all, yet ordinary taxable income is about $465,000 after the standard deduction.*

The top of the 24% bracket for a married couple is about $414,000 of [[term:taxable-income|taxable income]] in 2027. Their interest alone is past it, before any Social Security and eight years before any required distribution. Every extra dollar of ordinary income they could add this year is taxed at 32%, and their interest also owes the 3.8% [[term:niit|net investment income tax]].

## The policy finds no room

The Roth conversion policy on this plan is the one most guides describe. It converts just enough each year to fill ordinary income up to the top of the 24% bracket.

The bracket is already full, so the policy has nothing to fill. In the Brackets view each year is a column of income stacked through the brackets. Every column from 2027 to 2034 reaches into the 32% bracket from portfolio income alone, and no year has a conversion layer.

[[img:hnw-brackets-treasuries]]

*The Brackets view for Treasuries, fill to 24%. Portfolio income fills each column into the 32% bracket and the policy converts nothing. From 2035 the required distributions on the untouched IRA push every year into the 37% bracket.*

This is the policy working correctly. A fill-to-bracket rule only has something to do when there is an empty bracket below the household's income, and a portfolio this size fills those brackets itself. Raising the target does not rescue it either. The 37% bracket has no top, so "fill to 37%" has no amount to aim at, and a household at this level chooses a conversion amount on purpose instead of filling a bracket.

The cost of doing nothing arrives in 2035. The IRA has grown untouched for eight years, and its first required distribution is about $385,000. Stacked on the interest, that puts every year from then on in the 37% bracket. [Forced traditional RMDs](/learn/banks_forced_rmd) shows the same pressure on a smaller household.

## Lowering the floor with munis

A [[term:municipal-bond|municipal bond]] pays interest that is excluded from federal taxable income. Swapping the Treasury fund for a municipal fund lowers the floor the conversion has to sit on. Switch to [Munis, fill to 24%](#canvas-dossier:vance_munis_fill_24), where nothing has changed except the bond sleeve.

[[jump:municipal-bond]]

[[img:hnw-munis-statement-2027]]

*The 2027 federal statement on Munis, fill to 24%. Portfolio ordinary income falls to about $24,000, the cash fund's interest. The conversion adds about $423,000 of traditional retirement income, and ordinary taxable income lands exactly at the top of the 24% bracket.*

The same policy now converts about $414,000 in 2027 and a similar amount every year through 2034. The conversion room did not come from earning less. It came from income that the federal return no longer counts as taxable.

[[img:hnw-brackets-munis]]

*The Brackets view for Munis, fill to 24%. From 2027 to 2034 each column is almost entirely Roth conversion, stopping at the top of the 24% bracket. The IRA is smaller by 2035, so the required distributions that follow stay inside the 24% bracket.*

Two things about munis stay true even here. The interest still counts in [[term:magi|MAGI]], so the 2027 statement shows MAGI about $315,000 above [[term:agi|AGI]], and Medicare premiums are figured on MAGI. At this income both plans already pay the top Medicare [[term:surcharge|surcharge]], so the added MAGI costs nothing more. [When tax-exempt interest still shows up on the bill](/learn/taxes_muni_interest_still_on_the_bill) covers the MAGI and state-tax side in detail.

Munis also pay less. The municipal fund here [[term:yield|yields]] 3%, against about 4.4% for the Treasury fund. Whether the swap pays depends on the bracket. At a marginal rate of 32% plus the 3.8% investment income tax, a 3% [[term:tax-free|tax-free]] yield is worth about as much as a 4.7% taxable one. Helen and Arturo are in that bracket or higher, so the muni sleeve keeps more after tax even before the conversion room it opens. A household in the 12% bracket would usually do better with the taxable bond.

## No free lunch: credit risk

A municipal bond is a loan to a state, a city, or an authority that runs a project such as a toll road, a hospital or a water system. Some are paid from the issuer's general taxes. Others are paid only from what the project earns. Either way, the bond is only as good as the borrower's ability to pay. The chance that it pays late, pays less, or stops paying is [[term:credit-risk|credit risk]].

Treasuries are treated as free of credit risk, because the federal government borrows in the currency it issues. Municipal bonds are not. Defaults on rated municipal bonds have been rare, far rarer than on corporate bonds of the same rating, but they happen. Detroit's bankruptcy in 2013 cut payments to some of its bondholders, and Puerto Rico restructured most of its debt after 2017. A household that buys the bonds of its own state for the state tax break also ties a large share of its money to one state's finances.

The forecast cannot show this risk. The muni fund in this plan carries price swings like any bond fund, but no plan in Tutilis models a default as an event. When [Munis, fill to 24%](#canvas-dossier:vance_munis_fill_24) ends ahead of the Treasury plan, part of what it is showing is the absence of a risk the model does not draw.

<aside class="lesson-saying" data-saying="free-lunch">

The muni sleeve keeps more after tax and opens room for conversions. The price is credit risk the Treasury fund does not carry, and a spread across many issuers is what keeps it small.

</aside>

## Converting at the top rate

Bracket-filling is one reason to convert, and at this level of wealth it is usually unavailable. Other reasons get stronger as wealth rises.

- **Required distributions stack on the floor.** A large IRA left alone grows, and its required distributions are added on top of the portfolio income. A dollar converted at 35% now may otherwise come out at 37% later.
- **A survivor files alone.** When one spouse dies, the other files as single, and the single brackets are about half as wide. The same income is taxed at higher rates for the rest of the survivor's life.
- **Heirs pay the tax on what is left.** Most heirs must empty an [[term:inherited-traditional-ira|inherited traditional IRA]] within ten years, on top of their own earnings. [Inheriting an IRA: the 10-year rule](/learn/inherited_ira_ten_year_rule) shows how that lands. A Roth passes to them tax-free.
- **Above the estate exemption, conversion tax leaves the estate.** The [[term:estate-tax|federal estate tax]] takes up to 40% of an estate above the exemption. Income tax paid on a conversion is money that is no longer in the estate, and a traditional IRA gets no [[term:step-up-in-basis|step-up in basis]] at death, while the taxable account does.

[Fixed $500k a year](#canvas-dossier:vance_fixed_conversion) keeps the Treasuries and converts $500,000 every year from 2027 through 2034. With the floor already in the 32% bracket, that $500,000 runs through the 35% bracket into the 37% bracket. The household pays more tax in those eight years. In return, the IRA is about half the size by 2035, and the first required distribution is about $182,000 instead of about $385,000. The later years stay in the 35% bracket instead of the 37% bracket.

## What the heirs keep

Comparing these plans by ending net worth misleads, because net worth counts a dollar in a traditional IRA the same as a dollar in a Roth. The traditional dollar still owes income tax, and the Roth dollar owes none. Tutilis's outcome card adds a row for this: the ending net worth after the income tax heirs would still owe on traditional balances, at 24% unless you choose another rate.

Read that row on each plan, with the simulation set to 1,000 paths so the comparison is steady. At the [[term:median|median]], [Fixed $500k a year](#canvas-dossier:vance_fixed_conversion) leaves the heirs about $1 million more than [Treasuries, fill to 24%](#canvas-dossier:vance_fill_24), on an estate of about $70 million. Heirs of an estate this size are likely to pay more than 24%, and at a 37% heir rate the fixed plan's lead grows.

The weak paths tell the other half. At the 10th [[term:percentile|percentile]] the fixed plan ends a little behind. The conversion tax is paid up front, and in a poor market the Roth has less growth with which to earn it back. A conversion at the top rate is a bet that the money will grow and that later rates will be at least as high, and on most paths that bet pays modestly.

The difference is small beside the estate, and that is a fair description of the trade at this level. A conversion at 37% is close to even, and it wins at the margins: smaller required distributions, fewer years at the top rate for the survivor, and less taxable income for the heirs. The estate-tax argument from the last section adds to it. These plans have estate tax switched on, so the outcome card also has an **After estate tax** column. At the median, the fixed plan's estate pays about $280,000 less federal estate tax than the fill-to-24% plan's, because the conversion tax has already left the estate. The card keeps the two adjustments apart: the after-estate column does not also take off the income tax the heirs still owe on the traditional IRA, and the heirs' row does not take off the estate tax. Read together, both favor the conversion.

[Munis, fill to 24%](#canvas-dossier:vance_munis_fill_24) ends well ahead of both, because it pays far less tax every year. Read that lead together with the credit-risk section above.

## What the plan leaves out

- **The estate tax and the heirs' income tax are not combined.** The outcome card shows each on its own. Heirs who inherit a traditional IRA from a taxable estate can also deduct the estate tax paid on it from their income tax, which this does not model.
- **[[term:filing-status|Filing status]] does not change at a death.** The plan files jointly for its whole length. To see the survivor's narrower brackets, build a second plan that files as single from the year you expect the first death.
- **The heirs' rate is one flat number.** Real heirs spread an inherited IRA over ten years at their own rates, which this does not model.
- **No default is ever modeled.** The municipal fund's credit risk is described, not simulated.
- **No state is selected.** State income tax on a conversion, and state tax on municipal interest from other states, both change the answer. [Where to retire on $20 million](/learn/hnw_where_to_retire_state_taxes) takes up the state.

A conversion at this size is usually planned with a tax professional and an estate attorney, alongside trusts, gifts and charitable plans this forecast does not represent. The plan gives that conversation numbers.

## What to take away

A large taxable portfolio pays an income floor of its own, and at this level the floor can fill the brackets that conversion advice is built around. A fill-the-bracket policy then converts nothing, and that is the correct answer to the question it was asked. Municipal bonds lower the floor and bring the room back, at the price of credit risk. Above the floor, a conversion is an amount chosen on purpose, and it is judged by later rates, the survivor's brackets, the heirs and the estate rather than by the bracket it fills.
