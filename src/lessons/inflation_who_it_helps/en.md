# Who inflation helps

[[term:inflation|Inflation]] is often called pernicious, and people write about the ravages of inflation. A rise in prices does make a paycheck and a pile of saved cash buy less, and that harm is real. A debtor can benefit from the same rise. A debtor owes a fixed amount of cash in the future, and those dollars are worth less when they are paid back.

A rise in prices helps someone who is repaying a fixed loan with a paycheck that rises too, and it squeezes someone whose check rises more slowly than the grocery bill. A fall in prices turns the loan the other way when pay falls with prices. It leaves an indexed check alone when that check has a floor at zero.

Deflation is a fall in the price level. It is the directional opposite of inflation. Inside a household the reversal is only as clean as the contract. A fixed dollar payment flips when pay moves with prices. Cash flips the other way: a stack of dollars buys less when prices rise, and more when they fall. A check that is indexed, and that this plan holds at zero when you ask for a fall, is helped by the rise and helped again by the fall.

## Where rises and falls are thought to come from

A plan can show you the household after prices have moved. The argument about why they moved is older than any plan, and it is worth having in mind before you move a slider.

Inflation is a rise in the price level. The same basket of goods costs more dollars than it did. People usually tell three stories about why that happens.

One story is that people try to buy more than is being produced. Sellers can raise prices and still find a buyer. That is the story often told about 2021 and 2022 in the United States. The twelve-month change in [[term:cpi|CPI-U]] was 9.1 percent in June 2022.

Another story is that making things costs more. Wages, fuel, or a broken supply line raise the cost of producing, and prices follow. The oil shocks of the late 1970s are told this way. CPI-U was up 14.8 percent over the twelve months ending March 1980.

A third story is that people raise prices because they expect prices to rise. Workers ask for raises because they expect the basket to cost more. Firms raise prices because they expect wages and supplies to cost more. The expectation keeps itself going. [[term:hyperinflation|Hyperinflation]] is that loop running without a brake. Philip Cagan, writing in 1956, drew the line at a month when prices rise 50 percent or more. Germany in 1923, Hungary in 1946, Zimbabwe in 2008, and Venezuela in the 2010s passed it.

Deflation is those same mechanisms pointed the other way. Spending falls short of what is being produced, the cost of making things falls, or people wait to buy because they expect goods to be cheaper later. In the United States the consumer price index fell in each year from 1930 through 1933. The annual average was down 2.7 percent in 1930, 8.9 percent in 1931, 10.3 percent in 1932, and 5.2 percent in 1933. The steepest twelve-month drop was 10.8 percent, from the autumn of 1931 to the autumn of 1932.

Stay on Nominal. Open [[nav:transforms]], unfold **Modifications**, and select **Living expenses**. Slide **Spending growth** up. The grocery line on the cash-flow chart climbs. That is this plan's picture of a faster rise, whichever of the three stories you have in mind. Slide it below zero. The control runs to about three times the 2.8 percent catalog rate on either side, so the grocery bill can sit near 8 percent below zero, close to the 1931 annual drop and short of 1932. The grocery line falls. That is this plan's picture of deflation. Hover a year on [[nav:cf-chart]]. The tip's **Home mortgage** line stays about $17,000, because the loan's schedule does not follow the grocery slider. The sections below ask who that payment helps, and who a fall hurts.

## A fixed payment gets lighter

A mortgage payment that stays the same number of dollars becomes a smaller share of a paycheck that keeps rising.

People describe inflation as pernicious, and they write about the ravages of inflation, because a rise in prices makes wages and saved cash buy less. That harm is real. The same rise helps a debtor. A debtor is someone who owes cash in the future. The dollars they pay back buy less than the dollars they borrowed, so the debt stings less.

Some people benefit from inflation. Those people are debtors. A debtor owes cash in the future, and inflation means they pay that debt back in dollars that are worth less. The payment stings less. Maya's mortgage is that debt on this plan. The payment stays about $17,000 while her wages rise.

</div>
</aside>

That benefit belongs to someone who already owes a fixed stack of dollars. It is a thin reason to take on a new loan. Anyone whose paycheck lags the grocery bill still buys less each year, and the lighter debt sits beside that harm. The payment is cash, due on a schedule, in years when prices may rise, stall, or fall. A lender who expects prices to rise usually charges for that expectation in the interest rate, so part of the lighter future dollar is charged up front. If the paycheck fails to rise, or if prices and pay both fall, that same payment takes a larger share of income. [Prices fall, the loan stays](#prices-fall-the-loan-stays) is that turn on this plan. What the money bought matters as well. Maya's loan pays for a house she lives in. A loan taken out to spend, against a paycheck that may not keep up, is a different arrangement.

Leave the mortgage on its schedule. The payment follows the amortization schedule. [[term:hover|Hover]] **Home mortgage** on [[nav:cf-table]] and read the **Payment** row: about $17,000 every year the loan runs. The [[term:principal]] and interest rows under it trade places. In 2027 that payment is about 13 percent of Maya's wages. Her wages rise about 3.5 percent a year, so by 2047, her last full year of pay, the paycheck is about twice what it was in 2027. The payment is still about $17,000. That is about 7 percent of the paycheck, close to half the share it was at the start.

[[jump:cash-flow|output__forecaster__forecaster.forecast-detail__cf-table]]

On [[nav:cf-chart]], hover a year. The tip lists **Home mortgage** as that same total, with the two legs under it. Switch the chart to **Today's $** and hover a later year. The payment is drawn as fewer of today's dollars. This plan restates future dollars at 3.5 percent a year, so a flat checkbook payment looks lighter in today's groceries on top of becoming a smaller share of pay. The balance she owes is still a stack of dollars. What fell is the weight of the payment against her paycheck, and against the grocery basket.

Maya's pension rises, and the grocery bill rises faster.

The pension starts in 2048, the year her wages stop, and its [[term:cola|COLA]] is 1 percent a year. Living expenses on this plan rise about 2.8 percent a year. A check that gains 1 percent buys a smaller grocery basket each year the gap stays open. ## Prices fall, the loan stays

Deflation is a fall in the price level, and when Maya's pay falls with prices the fixed mortgage payment becomes a larger share of that pay.

This trial is a Nominal reading. Today's $ on this plan keeps discounting every future dollar at positive 3.5 percent, so that view keeps making the payment look lighter no matter what you do to the grocery slider. Leave Today's $ for the comparison in the first section. Stay on Nominal here.

Select **Living expenses** and slide **Spending growth** below zero. The control runs to about three times the 2.8 percent catalog rate on either side, so the grocery bill can be set near 8 percent below zero. Then select **Maya's wages** and slide **Growth** below zero as well.

[[img:who-wages-modifiable|control]]

The grocery line and the paycheck both bend down. The mortgage payment stays on its schedule, about $17,000, so it is a larger share of the smaller paycheck. The person whom inflation helped, because her pay rose against a fixed payment, is the person a falling paycheck hurts. The loan's dollar amount held still. Her pay fell, so the payment's share of that pay rose.

Now put the wage slider back where it opened, and leave groceries below zero. The paycheck holds. Groceries get cheaper. The payment is the same stack of dollars against a paycheck that stayed put, so the loan keeps the weight it had. A contract that moves with prices flips. A paycheck that stays sticky while only prices fall leaves the borrower holding a cheaper grocery basket and the same payment.

## Why deflation is dreaded

Economists dread deflation because a fall in prices can feed on itself, even though cheaper goods sound like a gift.

Lower prices help a household whose paycheck holds still. Groceries cost less, and the same wages buy more. The dread begins when the fall continues and incomes fall with it.

People wait to buy when they expect the same goods to cost less next month. Shops sell less. Employers cut hours, wages, or jobs, and paychecks shrink. Debts promised in a fixed number of dollars then take a larger share of the smaller pay. Households that are squeezed spend less, and some sell what they own to keep the payments current. Waiting and selling push prices down again. Each step gives the next step a reason to keep going. That loop is a deflationary spiral.

One turn of the loop is already on this plan. Stay on Nominal. Select **Living expenses** and **Maya's wages**, and slide both growth rates below zero. The grocery line and the paycheck bend down. The mortgage payment stays about $17,000, so it is a larger share of the smaller paycheck. A household in that spot spends less and has a harder time keeping the next payment current. This plan's picture is that heavier payment. The shops selling less, and the paychecks shrinking again, are the turns after it.

The drop in United States consumer prices from 1930 through 1933, in [Where rises and falls are thought to come from](#where-rises-and-falls-are-thought-to-come-from), is what the loop looks like when it runs for several years.

## An indexed check holds

Social Security on this plan rises while prices rise, and the check holds still when you set prices to fall.

The benefit starts in 2048 at a [[term:cola|COLA]] of 2.5 percent a year, a little slower than the grocery bill at about 2.8 percent. While prices rise, Maya is helped: the check grows, and the mortgage she is still paying is a fixed stack of dollars. She is helped a little less than she would be if the check matched the grocery rate exactly. She is still helped.

Select **Maya's Social Security**.

The **COLA** slider opens at 2.5 percent and runs from 0 to 4.5 percent. Set it to zero, and set **Spending growth** on living expenses below zero, the way the previous section did. The check holds. The grocery bill falls. She was helped by the rise, and she is helped again by the fall, because the check stayed put while the basket got cheaper.

## A holding keeps its own rate

The TIPS fund keeps the catalog return on its own row while you move the grocery bill.

Select **Brokerage: TIPS fund** on the Modifiables card.<!-- term-skip: brokerage — the Transforms row name --> The card opens on the tab that holds that row.

[[img:who-tips-modifiable|control]]

[[jump:return|modifiable__transforms__transforms.modifications__transforms.modifiables__modifiables.holdings__balance-sheet.holding]]

The row opens near 5.5 percent a year, with its own ups and downs. Slide living expenses wherever you like. The fund's rate stays the one on its row. People buy [[term:tips|TIPS]] because a Treasury Inflation-Protected Security adjusts its principal with the consumer price index, up when prices rise and down when they fall, and pays back at least the original principal at maturity. The forecast grows this holding at the catalog return on its row. Moving the grocery bill leaves that return where it was, which is the comparison the plan can show you.

A stack of dollars in cash buys more of the grocery basket when prices fall, and less when they rise. That flip belongs to the cash itself. The demonstration on this plan is the TIPS row staying put while the grocery line moves.
