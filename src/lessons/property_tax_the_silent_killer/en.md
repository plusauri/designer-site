# Property tax, the silent killer

Most people who compare states compare income tax rates, because that is the number printed on every "best states to retire" list. Property tax gets less attention, and it is often the bigger bill. It is a percentage of what your house is worth, charged every year, and it grows as the house grows. Your income tax goes down when your paycheck stops. Your property tax does not. This lesson follows one Illinois household with an expensive house and uses the **Region** comparison to show what that bill costs over a lifetime.

## A bill that never retires

A property tax is set by the town, the county, the school district and other local bodies, and it is charged on the assessed value of the house. The **effective rate** is the tax actually paid divided by the market value of the home. It is the easiest way to compare places, because it already folds in how each state assesses and how many local taxes stack up.

Three features make the property tax harder on a long plan than its rate suggests.

- **It is charged on a growing number.** When the house gains value, the bill gains with it, even though the house has not paid you anything.
- **It has no off switch.** Income tax falls when wages end. Illinois, for example, does not tax retirement income from pensions, [[term:ira|IRAs]] or Social Security at all, but it still sends the property tax bill every year.
- **It crowds out saving.** Every dollar paid is a dollar that did not compound for the rest of the plan, so a steady gap of a few thousand dollars a year becomes a very large gap by the end.

Tutilis charges each house its property tax every year from the rate on the house's own **Property tax** setting, applied to the house's value in that year.

## The worst offenders

The states with the highest effective property tax rates are mostly in the Northeast and the Midwest. The rates below are the statewide averages that Tutilis uses on Region, estimated for tax year 2024 as the tax on a [[term:median|median]] owner-occupied home divided by its market value.

| State | Effective rate | Tax on an $850,000 house |
|---|---:|---:|
| New Jersey | 2.23% | about $19,000 |
| Illinois | 2.07% | about $17,600 |
| Connecticut | 1.79% | about $15,200 |
| Vermont | 1.68% | about $14,300 |
| New Hampshire | 1.61% | about $13,700 |
| Texas | 1.47% | about $12,500 |
| Colorado | 0.50% | about $4,300 |
| Tennessee | 0.45% | about $3,800 |
| Hawaii | 0.29% | about $2,500 |

Published rankings use slightly different methods, and New Jersey and Illinois trade first place between them, but the same states sit at the top every year. Local rates vary widely inside each state, and the suburban counties around Chicago run above the Illinois average. The lesson uses the statewide rate for both states so that the comparison is fair.

Illinois has a second problem. The jobs are concentrated around Chicago, and so are the expensive houses. A family that moves there for work often buys a house far above the state's median value and then pays the state's high rate on the whole of it.

## Meet the household

Marcus is 45 and Elena is 43. Together they earn $290,000, and both plan to retire at 65. They bought an $850,000 house in a Chicago suburb and still owe $560,000 on the mortgage. They save into a [[term:401k|401(k)]] and a [[term:roth|Roth]] IRA and hold about $250,000 in a [[term:brokerage|brokerage]] account and cash. Their living costs are $105,000 a year while they work and $85,000 a year once they retire. Both will claim Social Security.

In their first year the house is taxed at about $18,000. Their Illinois income tax, at a flat 4.95%, is about $15,000. The house costs them more in tax than their two salaries do.

[[img:property-tax-statements-illinois-2027]]

Look at the same statements in 2050, after both have retired. Illinois taxes almost none of their income now, because it exempts retirement income. The house has kept growing, and its tax bill has roughly doubled to about $38,000 a year. By this point the property tax is more than twenty times the state income tax.

[[img:property-tax-statements-illinois-2050]]

## Illinois against Colorado

A fair test needs a state that taxes income about the same way, so that the property tax is the main thing that changes. Colorado fits. Its income tax is a flat 4.4%, a little below Illinois's 4.95%, and its living costs outside housing are within a few percent of Illinois's. Its property tax rate is 0.50%, about a quarter of the Illinois rate.

The lesson carries a second plan, **Colorado**, that is the same household with the same house, the same wages and the same spending, taxed as a Colorado resident. In the first year its property tax is about $4,400 and its state income tax is about $12,000. Both are lower, but the property tax difference is more than four times the income tax difference.

Over the whole plan the difference is dramatic. On the expected path, the Illinois plan ends in 2075 with about $7.6 million and the Colorado plan with about $13.5 million. Both figures are in 2075 dollars. The [[term:monte-carlo|Monte Carlo]] view says the same thing. Across a thousand paths, the Colorado plan succeeds more often, and every point on its Key Forecasts sits well above the Illinois one.

## Reading the Region table

You do not need a second plan to ask this question. **Region** runs your own plan as if you lived somewhere else. Open Region on the [Illinois](#canvas-dossier:property_tax_illinois) plan, open **Region Selection**, and tap Colorado, Indiana, Texas and Tennessee on the map. Each state becomes a row in the comparison table below the map, beside Illinois, which is marked as home.

Each row shows the cost-of-living and wage multipliers for that state, its property tax rate, its first-year state income tax, the plan's final net worth if you lived there, and the difference from home. Region builds each row the same way. It switches the plan's state of residence, replaces the property tax rate on every house with the new state's average, and scales living costs by the **COLO ×** multiplier. The house's value does not change.

[[img:property-tax-region-defaults]]

With Region's defaults, every one of the four states finishes well ahead of Illinois. The defaults include a cost-of-living adjustment, though, so part of each row's gain comes from cheaper groceries and services rather than from taxes.

## Isolating the house

The **COLO ×** and **Wage ×** headers are checkboxes. Clear **COLO ×**, and leave **Wage ×** off, which is how it starts. Now every row keeps the Illinois household's exact wages and spending, and the only things that differ are the state's income tax and its property tax.

[[img:property-tax-region-taxes-only]]

Colorado is still millions of dollars ahead. Texas is the interesting row. It has no state income tax at all, yet it finishes behind Colorado, because its property tax rate of 1.47% is almost three times Colorado's. A state with no income tax is not a low-tax state for a family whose biggest asset is a house.

You can split the Colorado gap into its two parts. The **Prop. tax** cell on each row can be edited. Type Illinois's rate, 2.07, into the Colorado row. The row now differs from home only in its income tax, and most of Colorado's lead disappears. The small lead that remains is what Colorado's lower income tax is worth. Nearly all of the difference was the house.

To see one move in full, start again with only Colorado selected. Tapping it on the map highlights the state, adds its row, and shows its tax and cost profile on the card beside the map.

[[img:property-tax-region-map-colorado]]

With the Colorado row selected, **Simulation Summary** runs both versions of the plan across many futures and sets their net worth distributions side by side. The [Colorado](#canvas-dossier:property_tax_colorado) plan in this lesson is exactly that row with **COLO ×** and **Wage ×** off, so its distribution is the right-hand side of that comparison.

## The unfair comparison

The comparison that first shows most people this effect is Illinois against Tennessee, and in the table above Tennessee wins by the widest margin. That comparison is not fair, because Tennessee wins on everything. It has no income tax on wages, one of the lowest property tax rates in the country and lower living costs.

It also has lower wages. Turn on **Wage ×** as well as **COLO ×**. Region now scales the household's pay by each state's wage level relative to Illinois, which is the right assumption when a move means taking a new local job.

[[img:property-tax-region-wage-on]]

Tennessee's lead shrinks sharply, and Colorado, whose wages run above Illinois's, moves ahead of it. Keep wages off when a move keeps the same job, such as remote work or a transfer. Turn them on when the move means finding work at local pay.

## What the forecast leaves out

The forecast does not itemize [[term:deduction|deductions]], so it does not subtract property tax on the federal return. For a household like this one that is a real simplification. Federal law lets itemizers deduct state and local taxes, including property tax, up to a cap that is temporarily higher through 2029. While Marcus and Elena work and itemize, part of the Illinois property tax comes back to them as a smaller federal bill, so the forecast somewhat overstates the gap in those years. After 2030, and in retirement, most of that deduction is gone.

| 2029 | $41,624 |

The cap rises by 1% a year through 2029 and falls back to $10,000 in 2030. Above about $500,000 of income it shrinks by 30% of the excess, but never below $10,000. Married people filing separately get half.

The statewide averages also hide a lot. Rates within a state vary by town and school district, some states cap how fast an assessment can rise, and many offer homestead or senior exemptions. A house of the same size would also not sell for the same price in another state. Treat Region's rows as a clean experiment that holds everything else still. Before you choose a house, look up the actual rate for that address.

## What to take away

- Property tax is a percentage of a growing asset, charged every year for life. In a high-rate state an expensive house can cost more in tax than the state's income tax does, and it keeps costing after retirement ends the income tax.
- To see what a state's property tax costs, compare it with a state that taxes income about the same way. Illinois against Colorado does that. Illinois against Tennessee changes everything at once.
- On Region, clear **COLO ×** and leave **Wage ×** off to see the taxes alone. Edit a row's **Prop. tax** cell to split the property tax from the income tax.
- "No income tax" is not the same as "low tax". A high property tax can outweigh it.
