# A budget checklist for a first paycheck

Riley is 23, a year out of college, and earning $65,000 at a first real job. Rent gets paid, the car gets paid, the [[term:401k|401(k)]] takes 3% because that was the box on the onboarding form, and whatever is left at the end of the month is gone by the next one. Riley has never written the expenses down, and so has no answer to the only budgeting question that matters: **how much is being saved each month, and is it enough?**

This lesson is a checklist for answering that. It walks through every category a first paycheck goes to, asks the questions that find the amounts, and puts the totals on a plan so the tool can do the arithmetic. It then measures each section against a few rules of thumb, and shows what happens, this year and forty years from now, when the sections that are over their range are trimmed and the difference is given somewhere to go.

Two plans are open on the [[term:canvas]]: [As it is](#canvas-dossier:budget_checklist_as_is), Riley's budget as it stands, and [Trimmed to the ranges](#canvas-dossier:budget_checklist_trimmed), the same budget after the trims. Every figure below says which plan it comes from. The first forecast year is **2027**, so set the [[term:forecaster]] there once and it carries across the switch.

## The checklist

Go section by section and write a monthly number beside every line, even when the number is zero. A zero you wrote is a decision. A line you skipped is a guess. Each section opens with the questions that find the amounts, and closes with what Riley wrote down and how each line sorts into **Need**, **Want**, **Savings** or **Unexpected**. Those four buckets come back later, when the budget is measured.

### Housing

Where do you live, and what does it cost to keep the lights on and the fridge full? What do you pay to protect what is in the apartment? What does the phone cost, and the internet? How much goes to groceries in an ordinary week, and to the things that are not food but come from the same store? Did you buy a couch this year, and will you buy something like it next year?

| Line | Riley, per month | Sort |
|---|---:|---|
| Rent or mortgage | $1,625 | Need |
| Renter's or homeowner's insurance | $15 | Need |
| Utilities (electricity, gas, water) | $110 | Need |
| Internet | $60 | Need |
| Phone bill | $65 | Need |
| Groceries | $380 | Need |
| Household supplies (cleaning products, toiletries) | $40 | Need |
| Furniture and home essentials | $30 | Want |
| Repairs and maintenance | $0 | Unexpected |
| **Housing** | **$2,325** | |

Rent at $1,625 is exactly 30% of Riley's gross pay, which is a number we will come back to.

### Transportation

How do you get to work, and what does that cost per month rather than per tank? Is there a car payment, and for how many more years? What is the insurance, and when did you last shop it? What do you set aside for oil changes and tires, or do those arrive as surprises? Do you pay to park? Do you take rideshares, and how often is that a choice rather than a necessity?

| Line | Riley, per month | Sort |
|---|---:|---|
| Car payment | $430 | Need |
| Car insurance | $150 | Need |
| Gas | $130 | Need |
| Maintenance (oil changes, tires, repairs) | $50 | Need |
| Parking | $45 | Need |
| Public transit passes | $0 | Need |
| Rideshare budget | $45 | Want |
| **Transportation** | **$850** | |

### Personal and lifestyle

What do you spend on clothes and shoes across a year, divided by twelve? Haircuts? Is there a gym, and do you go? What do the hobbies cost? How many streaming services are you paying for, and could you name them all without checking? What did you spend eating out and going out last month, honestly? Do you set money aside for a trip, or does the trip go on a card? What do birthdays and the holidays cost?

| Line | Riley, per month | Sort |
|---|---:|---|
| Clothing and shoes | $80 | Need |
| Haircuts and grooming | $40 | Need |
| Gym membership or fitness classes | $50 | Want |
| Hobbies | $50 | Want |
| Streaming services | $45 | Want |
| Dining out and entertainment | $430 | Want |
| Travel fund | $100 | Want |
| Gifts (holidays, birthdays) | $50 | Want |
| **Personal and lifestyle** | **$845** | |

Clothes and haircuts are sorted as needs, because a first job expects you to show up presentable and that costs something every month. The part above presentable is a want, and the line between them is yours to draw. The sort matters later, when the buckets are measured, so make it deliberately rather than by default.

Dining out and entertainment at $430 a month is $5,160 a year, which is just under 8% of Riley's gross pay. That is the other number we will come back to.

### Work and professional

Does the commute cost something the transportation section did not already count? Does the job need clothes you would not otherwise buy? Are there courses or certifications you are paying for, or that the employer would pay for if you asked? Do you go to events that cost money to attend?

| Line | Riley, per month | Sort |
|---|---:|---|
| Work-related commuting costs | $0 (counted under gas and parking) | Need |
| Professional clothing | $25 | Need |
| Courses and certifications | $0 | Want |
| Networking events | $15 | Want |
| **Work and professional** | **$40** | |

### Savings and financial health

This is the section most people leave blank, and it is the one the lesson exists for. What goes into the 401(k) each month, and what does the employer add? Is there an emergency fund, and how many months of expenses would it cover? Is there a separate pot for the next big purchase, and for the long-term ones? Is anything invested outside the retirement account?

| Line | Riley, per month | Sort |
|---|---:|---|
| Emergency fund (3 to 6 months of expenses) | $0, and about $2,000 in checking | Savings |
| Retirement contributions (401(k), [[term:ira\|IRA]]) | $163, a 3% deferral, plus $81 from the employer | Savings |
| Short-term savings (vacations, big purchases) | $0 (the travel fund above is the only one) | Savings |
| Long-term savings (house, future goals) | $0 | Savings |
| Investing | $0 | Savings |
| **Savings and financial health** | **$163, plus the match** | |

<p class="lesson-did-you-know-label">Did you know?</p>
<div class="lesson-did-you-know-body">

An employer match is part of your pay, and you collect it only by saving. Riley's employer adds fifty cents for every dollar Riley puts in, up to 6% of pay. At a 3% deferral Riley collects a 1.5% match and leaves the other 1.5% of pay unclaimed, every year.

</div>
</aside>

The emergency fund is thin, too. $2,000 in checking is about two weeks of Riley's expenses, and the usual target is three months.

### Administrative and other

Is tax withheld from the paycheck, or will there be a bill in April? Is the bank charging you anything, and why? Is there a pet? And what did last year's parking ticket, cracked phone screen and flat tire add up to, because something like them will happen again this year?

| Line | Riley, per month | Sort |
|---|---:|---|

| Pet expenses | $0 | Need |
| "Life happens" fund (tickets, repairs, surprises) | $100 | Unexpected |
| **Administrative and other** | **$110** | |

Riley's checklist comes to **$4,390 a month** before the 401(k), or **$52,680 a year**, which is 81% of gross pay. What is left after that has to cover the tax bill and the retirement contribution, and we are about to find out whether it does.

## Putting it on the plan

A plan does not want forty line items. It wants each section as one number a year, which is the monthly total times twelve, and it wants savings as contributions on the accounts they go into rather than as expenses. That is how both plans in this lesson were built:

- Each expense section is one **living expense** line named for the section, with its monthly total times twelve in 2026 money. Housing is $2,325 × 12 = $27,900, Transportation is $850 × 12 = $10,200, and so on.
- Insurance and health is a **healthcare** line rather than a living expense, so the tool can switch it to Medicare at 65 and so it shows up under its own heading.
- The 401(k) is an account with two contributions, Riley's deferral and the employer's match, and the match is marked as employer-funded so it counts as income rather than as something Riley paid.
- Salary is $65,000 until retirement at 67, Social Security is estimated from a statement and claimed at full retirement age, and there is no student loan. If you carry one, it goes on the plan as a loan and appears as its own line.

Riley's own deferral is about $2,000, the match adds about $1,000, and the plan parks the few hundred dollars that had no name in Checking. In real life that money does not sit in checking; it gets spent, because money without a name always does. The plan is being generous.

Now sort the checklist into the four buckets. Riley's take-home pay, after tax and the 401(k) deferral, is about $4,300 a month.

| Bucket | Riley, per month | Share of take-home | The 50/30/20 target |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Needs | $3,440 | 80% | 50% |
| Wants | $850 | 20% | 30% |
| Unexpected | $100 | 2% | (inside needs) |
| Savings | $163 | 4% | 20% |

The 50/30/20 rule is a target, and it is one line long: half of take-home pay to needs, three-tenths to wants, a fifth to savings. Riley's wants are inside their range. Needs are four-fifths of take-home, which is normal on a first salary in a city with real rents. Savings are 4% against a target of 20%, and that gap is the shortfall the lesson is looking for. The buckets say where it can come from: a little from wants, and more from the one need that is bigger than all the others.

## Against the ranges

Rules of thumb are not laws, and every one of them has exceptions by city and by circumstance. They are useful because they say **which sections deserve a hard look**. Here are the ranges this lesson uses, measured against Riley's checklist as a share of gross pay. A section inside its range is fine. A section above it is where to look first.

| Section | Range | Riley, as it is | Verdict |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Rent or mortgage | 25% to 30% of gross pay | 30.0% | At the line |
| Housing, all in | 35% to 40% of gross pay, counting utilities, phone, internet and groceries; this band varies most by city | 42.9% | Watch |
| Transportation | 10% to 15% of gross pay, car payment included | 15.7% | **Over** |
| Dining out and entertainment | 3% to 5% of gross pay | 7.9% | **Over** |
| Retirement saving | 15% of gross pay or more, counting the employer match | 4.5% | **Under** |
| Emergency fund | 3 to 6 months of expenses | About half a month | **Under** |

Each highlighted row comes with a reason, because the reason is what makes the number worth acting on.

**Transportation is over** by a little, and the car payment is why. A car payment is the one line in this section that a budget cannot trim; it ends when the loan ends. The lever this year is small, which is shopping the insurance and cutting the rideshares. The bigger lever is the next car, which should cost less.

**Dining out and entertainment is over** by three points of pay, which is about $2,000 a year. It is the most flexible line on the whole checklist, and the rule of thumb is 5% because that is roughly where a single person on one salary can eat out regularly without it being the thing the budget is built around.

**Retirement saving is under** by ten points of pay, and the first three of those are free. The employer matches half of the first 6%, so raising the deferral from 3% to 6% costs Riley about $160 a month before tax and collects about $80 a month that is currently left on the table. Nothing else on this page has that return.

**The emergency fund is under**, which is the reason the "life happens" line exists and the reason it is not enough. A hundred dollars a month covers the parking ticket. It does not cover a month without a paycheck, and three months of Riley's expenses is about $13,000.

Rent is at the line rather than over it, and the housing row is marked *watch* rather than *over* because the all-in band is the loosest of these and depends most on where you live. But housing is the section where the room is, if Riley wants it. A roommate does not change the rule; it changes the rent.

## Trimmed to the ranges

The [Trimmed to the ranges](#canvas-dossier:budget_checklist_trimmed) plan is the same checklist with six lines changed and the difference given three places to go. Nothing else moves: same salary, same car, same phone, same gym.

| Line | As it is | Trimmed | Why |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Rent | $1,625 | $1,300 | A roommate; 24% of gross instead of 30% |
| Utilities | $110 | $70 | Split two ways |
| Internet | $60 | $30 | Split two ways |
| Car insurance | $150 | $130 | Shopped |
| Rideshare | $45 | $20 | Mostly the car |
| Streaming | $45 | $25 | Two services instead of four |
| Dining out and entertainment | $430 | $270 | 5% of gross pay |
| **Freed** | | **$620 a month** | |

And where it goes:

| Savings line | As it is | Trimmed | What it is |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| 401(k) deferral | 3%, $163 a month | 6%, $325 a month | Collects the full match, which doubles to $163 |
| Emergency fund | $0 | $300 a month | Into a savings account with a **target balance** of $12,000; the plan stops the top-up when it gets there |
| [[term:roth\|Roth]] IRA | $0 | $100 a month | [[term:after-tax\|After-tax]] money that grows and comes out untaxed |
| **Saved** | **$163 plus $81 match** | **$725 plus $163 match** | |

The savings add to $562 a month against $620 freed, and the extra 3% of deferral comes out before tax. From the second year that lowers the income tax, so the take-home falls by less than the deferral rises. The rest is slack, and slack is fine. Both plans grow through the working years, because salary rises a little faster than expenses do and the plan invests the difference. But the trimmed plan reaches retirement with roughly twice the [[term:net-worth|net worth]], and the shape after 67 is different in kind. As it is, the balance peaks at retirement, is drawn down every year after, and runs out at about 89, which is the red at the right edge of the first chart. Trimmed, the balance never falls, because Social Security plus the growth on what was saved covers the spending. The whole difference is $620 a month, [[term:compounding]] for forty years, with the employer's half of the match riding along for free.

## What to take away

- Write every line down, including the zeros. The checklist's job is to find the lines you never see, and the tax line alone is a sixth of Riley's pay.
- Total each section as monthly times twelve and put it on the plan as one line. The tool then does the arithmetic, including the tax and the leftover, every year for the rest of your life.
- Sort into needs, wants, savings and unexpected, and measure against the rules of thumb. The point of a range is not the verdict; it is that a section over its range is where to look first.
- The first money to move is the money that is already free. A deferral below the employer match is a pay cut you chose.
- The room in a first-job budget is usually in housing and in dining out, and it is worth about twice the net worth at retirement in this plan. How much of that room to take is a lifestyle decision, and it is easier to make once you can see what it buys.
