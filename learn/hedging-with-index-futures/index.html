Hedging a stock position with index futures

The situation Dana and Lee Whitcomb, 63 and 61, retire next year with about $13 million, most of it in an S&P 500 index fund with a large unrealized gain. Index puts cost them about 2.4% a year. A broker suggests shorting $5 million of S&P 500 futures for the six years around retirement instead: no premium, half the market's swings.

Dana and Lee Whitcomb are 63 and 61 and retire next year with about $13 million. About $9.8 million of it sits in one S&P 500 index fund, and the $8 million of that in their taxable brokerageAn ordinary taxable investment account — no special retirement wrapper, no contribution limit, and no withdrawal age rules. The trade-off is that dividends and realized gains are taxed as they happen rather than deferred. Often called the taxable bucket next to traditional and Roth.Full entry → account carries a large unrealized gain. The puts lesson tells their story in full.

The three plans are the same household with one thing changed.

  • Short futures shorts a fixed $5 million of S&P 500 index futures in the brokerage account, about half of the index fund, and rolls the position every year from 2027 through 2032.
  • No hedge holds the portfolio as it is.
  • Index puts buys one-year puts 10% below the market on the whole $9.8 million for the same six years, as in the puts lesson.

The future is sized in dollars on purpose. A futures position is a number of contracts, and it does not grow when the holding does, so the hedge in Short futures covers a smaller share of the fund as the fund grows. The account editor's Hedges section sizes a hedge either way, as a share of the holding or as a fixed number of dollars.

A short future on an index pays what the index loses and costs what it gains. Its price is set so that the holding and the future together earn the cash rate. A buyer of the future skips paying for the stock and earns interest on the money instead, and gives up the dividends the stock would have paid, so the future's price builds in the cash rate less the dividend yieldThe income an investment pays out — interest or dividends — as a percentage of its price. It is not total return: a holding can yield 4% and still lose value over the year. Chasing yield alone is how a portfolio quietly takes on risk it was not paid for.Full entry →. The holding keeps its dividends, and the future hands back the rest of the holding's return in exchange for the cash rate.

In the forecast, each dollar the future covers settles every year at the cash rate minus the index fund's total return for that year. The plan's assumption for large US stocks is a mean return of about 7.6%, and its assumption for cash is about 3.8%, so a covered dollar gives up about 3.7% a year on average. On $5 million that is about $190,000 a year. It is never charged as a premium. It arrives as a large loss in a good year and a large gain in a bad one, and the 3.7% is only their average.

Per dollar covered, that average cost is higher than the puts' premium of about 2.4% a year, even though the puts are bought at a markup. The puts pay for protection in the bad years and keep every good year. The future gives up the good years in exchange for the same protection, and the good years are where most of a stock fund's return comes from.

For the math lovers — why the hedged holding earns the cash rate

With an index at \(S\), a cash rate \(c\) and a dividend yield \(q\), a one-year future is priced at

\[ F = S\,(1 + c - q). \]

The short side receives \(F - S_T\) when it settles. Per dollar of index, that is \(c - q - r_p\), where \(r_p\) is the index's price return for the year. A holding's total return is \(r = r_p + q\), so the future pays

\[ c - r, \]

and the holding plus the future earns \(r + (c - r) = c\). On average the future pays \(c - \mu\), where \(\mu\) is the holding's expected return. With \(\mu = 7.56\%\) and \(c = 3.83\%\), that is about \(-3.7\%\) a year. This is the cost-of-carry rule for index futures that Hull sets out in Options, Futures, and Other Derivatives.

The plan can replay the 2008 financial crisis from 2027, so that 2028 is the deep year, and every plan sees the same returns. In 2028 the future in Short futures pays about $2.2 million into the brokerage account, and the plan invests most of it.

The cost comes in the rebound. In 2029 the replayed market rises sharply, and the future loses about $2.6 million that year. The plan has no premium saved to cover it, so it sells about $3.7 million of its holdings to pay the loss and the year's spending and taxes.

Across the thousand random paths that follow the replay, the medianThe middle outcome — half above, half below — as against the average, which a few enormous results can drag upward. Over long horizons the two come apart badly, and the median is the one that answers "what does a typical path look like?"Full entry → Short futures plan ends with about $19 million. The median No hedge plan ends with about $24 million and the median Index puts plan with about $35 million. The puts paid in the crash and kept the rebound. The future paid in the crash and gave the rebound back, and it gave back more than it had paid.

The great crash of 1929 fell for three years before it turned, and the plan can replay it from 2027 too. Here the future earns its keep. Without a hedge, about four paths in five fail. With Short futures, about three in five succeed, because the future pays in each of the three falling years.

The rebound still costs it. In 2030 the replayed market rises steeply, the future loses about $4.7 million, and the plan sells about $6.1 million of its holdings to pay for it. The median plan ends with about $900,000. Index puts covers twice as much of the fund, keeps the rebound, and every one of its paths succeeds.

There's no free lunch. TNFL

The future has no price tag, and it is still the most expensive hedge here on average, because what it pays with is the gains.

Regulated futures on a broad-based index are Section 1256 contracts, like index options. Each year's gain or loss is taxed that year, 60% as long-term and 40% as short-term. Tutilis taxes every short future this way, and a hedge in an IRAIndividual Retirement Account — a retirement wrapper you open yourself rather than through an employer. It comes in traditional (pre-tax going in) and Roth (taxed going in) flavors, so naming the flavor matters more than the acronym does.Full entry → settles inside the account, untaxed. A year's net loss offsets that year's other capital gains and then up to $3,000 of ordinary incomeIncome taxed at the regular graduated rates — wages, interest, pension payments, and withdrawals from traditional retirement accounts. The point of the label is the contrast: long-term capital gains and qualified dividends get their own, usually lower, rate schedule.Full entry →, and the plan keeps no carryforward, so in a strong year part of the future's loss goes unused.

A short future also raises a question puts do not. The Constructive saleA hedge the tax law treats as a sale of the holding it hedges (IRC §1259), so the holding's gain is taxed in that year even though nothing was sold. It applies to a hedge that removes substantially all of a holding's risk of loss and chance of gain, such as a short sale or a short future on the same or substantially identical property. A collar can qualify when its floor and cap sit close together; there is no bright-line test.Full entry → rule, IRC §1259, treats a hedge that removes substantially all of a holding's risk and reward as a sale of the holding. The gain the hedge was meant to defer is then taxed in the year the hedge is opened. The statute names a short sale, and a futures or forward contract to deliver the same or substantially identical property. Whether an S&P 500 index fund and an S&P 500 future are substantially identical is a question about the particular fund and contract. That is why the account editor shows a warning under every short future in a taxable account.

A CollarA protective put paid for by selling a call on the same holding. The call gives away any return above its cap, so the holding keeps returns between the floor and the cap. A collar can cost little or nothing in cash; its cost is the best years it gives up.Full entry → can be caught too. In 1997 Congress said Treasury regulations would set the line for collars, and none have been issued, so there is no bright-line test. Practitioners treat a collar whose floor and cap sit closer together than about 15% to 20% as exposed. Tutilis warns when a collar's floor and cap are less than 20 points apart, which is the cautious end of that range. A collar with a floor 10% below the market and a cap 20% above it is 30 points wide.

The warning is only a warning. The forecast does not tax a constructive sale, so a plan that would trigger one looks better in the forecast than it would on a tax return.

A short future answers a narrow question: how to take most of the risk out of a large stock position for a few years without selling it and without paying a premium. It suits a household that would otherwise pay a large tax to sell, that wants the protection more than the gains for a stretch of years, and that has the cash or bonds to meet a large loss in a strong year.

Holding less stock is the simplest insurance, and for most households it is the first thing to consider. In an IRA or a RothA retirement account where you pay income tax on the money going in, and qualified withdrawals later come out tax-free — the mirror image of traditional. Named after the senator behind the law, so the word tells you nothing on its own. Plans care because Roth dollars do not add to taxable income later, which keeps them clear of income-driven cliffs.Full entry → account it costs no tax at all to change, and even in a taxable account a household can move toward a lower stock share by spending from the stock first and buying bonds with new savings. A cash reserve, a sound allocation, tax placement and ordinary insurance on lives and property come before any hedge.

Futures are a specialist's tool, and a household can use them either way. Some work with an advisor who runs a hedging program, and others trade them in their own brokerage account. Tutilis models both the same way: a hedge on an account, sized in dollars or as a share, with its tax treatment.

  • Daily settlement. A real future settles every day, so a rising market asks for cash during the year, and a broker can close a position that is short of margin. The forecast settles once a year.
  • Tracking. The forecast's future tracks the insured fund exactly. A real index future and a real fund drift apart a little, and a future on an index hedging a single stock or a narrow fund drifts apart a lot.
  • The carry is an average. The future earns the plan's average cash rate in every year, including replayed years when real rates were very different.
  • Rolling costs. Commissions and the price of rolling from one contract to the next are small for index futures. A yearly cost can be entered on the hedge if you have a figure.
  • Loss carryforward and carryback. A Section 1256 loss can be carried back three years against Section 1256 gains, and a capital loss carries forward. The plan uses neither, which understates the future's tax benefit.
  • The constructive sale. The forecast warns about it and does not tax it.

  • A short index future costs no premium and gives up the holding's gains and losses alike for about the cash rate.
  • On average it costs the extra return stocks are expected to earn over cash, which is more per dollar than the puts' premium.
  • It protects best in a long crash and worst in a crash that recovers quickly, because it gives back the rebound.
  • A short future on the same or substantially identical holding may be taxed as a sale under Section 1259, and so may a tight collar.
  • Holding less stock is the simplest insurance. Sell for more than basis and the difference is a taxable gain; the money you originally put in is not taxed again.
