Federal ordinary incomeIncome taxed at the regular graduated rates — wages, interest, pension payments, and withdrawals from traditional retirement accounts. The point of the label is the contrast: long-term capital gains and qualified dividends get their own, usually lower, rate schedule.Full entry → is taxed in brackets — the first slice of taxable incomeWhat the rates are finally applied to — income after adjustments and deductions, not what you earned. It sits at the bottom of the page: gross income, then AGI, then this. Most people's taxable income is meaningfully smaller than their pay, which is why a bracket alone never tells you the tax.Full entry → at 10%, the next at 12%, then 22%, and up. The low brackets are the cheap buckets, and every year you get a fresh set. Room you do not use does not roll over.

That creates a question no single number answers: how full are my cheap buckets each year, and who is filling them — me, or the rules? Two very different kinds of income compete for the same space. Required minimum distributions (RMDs) are forced out of traditionalA retirement account where the tax is paid on the way out — not a description of an old-fashioned account. Money went in before income tax, grew untaxed, and is taxed as ordinary income when you take it out — traditional 401(k)s and IRAs work this way. The contrast is Roth (taxed going in, tax-free coming out) and taxable (brokerage, taxed as you go).Full entry → accounts by statute. A Roth conversionDeliberately moving money from a traditional account into a Roth one and paying the income tax on it now, so it grows and comes out tax-free later. The usual reason is a low-income year — between retiring and claiming benefits, say — where the conversion is taxed at a lower rate than the future withdrawal would be. It also shrinks later RMDs.Full entry → is wholly your choice.

And it is not a one-off decision you model by hand. AssumptionsA main-rail area. The rules the forecast runs under — growth, taxes, movements, success definition, locality, currency. The common misreading is that entering a higher bracket taxes all your income at that rate — it does not. Only the dollars inside that band pay the higher rate, so a raise never leaves you with less after tax.Full entry → to fill, plus the range of years it runs — and the engine then executes it every year of the forecast, sizing each year's conversion against that year's actual income and that year's bracket edges. First, know which chart you are looking at

Whether a conversion policy is enabled changes what the picture is telling you, so it is worth settling before reading a single column. The card states it in plain text directly under the chart — including the mode and the year range — so you never have to go back to Assumptions to find out.

AssumptionsWhat the columns showThe question it answers
No conversion policyBase income and forced RMDs only — no teal segment. Everything left over is hatched."How much cheap-bracket room am I leaving unused, and when do RMDs start consuming it?" — a diagnosis.
Conversion policy onA third, teal segment: the dollars the engine actually converted that year under your policy."Is my policy filling the bucket it promised, and how much is left for it after forced income takes its share?" — a readout of a decision already made.

Both readings are useful, and they are not the same lesson. With no policy, the hatched cap is opportunity. With a policy on, the hatched cap is whether the policy did its job — and the teal segment is the part of the column you can still change by editing an assumption.

One thing the status line gives you that the chart cannot: the configured window, which is not the same as the years conversions actually happen. A policy can be active for years in which nothing converts — because the traditional balance ran dry, or forced income already filled the target. Seeing "active 2026–2055" above a stretch of columns with no teal segment is information, not a bug.

Turn on the BracketsA Forecast Detail tax-lens chip. Ordinary-income tax ladder for the selected year — how full the brackets are and who filled them. chip. Each year is a single stacked column measured against that year's ordinary ladder, drawn behind it as dashed steps.

The stack is ordered by how much choice you had, bottom to top:

   ░░░  unfilled room (hatched)          ← cheap-bucket space left this year
   ▒▒▒  Roth conversion   (you chose)    ─── 12% edge
   ▓▓▓  RMD              (rules forced)  ┃ ← outline = statutory requirement
   ███  base income      (already yours) ─── 10% edge
   ────────────────────────────────────────
     2027   2031   2032   …   2048

Two conventions carry most of the meaning:

  • Color means source, never rate. The rate is already encoded by height — where the column top sits against the step lines. Coloring by bracket would say the same thing twice; coloring by source says the thing nothing else does.
  • Solid means it happened; hatched means it is schedule. The hatched cap is not income. It is published statute — room the law leaves open — so it gets the same treatment as every other "machine knowledge" mark in the Forecaster.

Two things are worth noticing before RMDs ever appear.

The conversion segment grows every year. Not because the policy got more aggressive — it is the same "fill to 12%" instruction — but because the base segment is shrinking. The pension is flat in nominalA face-value figure, with no adjustment for inflation. A balance that doubles in nominal terms over 25 years may buy little more than it does today. The counterpart is real, which is inflation-adjusted and closer to what the money will actually buy — always check which one a chart shows.Full entry → dollars while the standard deductionThe flat amount most households subtract instead of itemizing individual deductions. You take the larger of the two, so it acts as a floor: below it, an extra deductible expense saves nothing at all, which surprises people who gave to charity expecting a break.Full entry → inflates, so less of it survives to be taxed each year. The room that opens up gets handed to the conversion automatically.

The hatched cap is essentially zero. That is what a filled bucket looks like. Compare it to any year on a plan with no policy, where the hatch is most of the column.

Philip was born in 1959, so under SECUREThe law (and its sequel) that moved several retirement-account rules: the age RMDs start, catch-up contributions, and how quickly an inherited account must be emptied. Its practical effect on a plan is that dates you may remember from a decade ago are no longer the dates.Full entry → 2.0 his RMDs begin at 73 — calendar year 2032. This is the sentence the lens exists to make visible: RMDs and conversions are not two separate stories. They are two claims on one bucket, and the forced one has priority.

With no policy, the window years show a column that is mostly hatch — $71,471 of unused 12% room in 2027 alone. Nothing bad happens that year. The bill arrives in 2037, when the untouched traditional balance has compounded to the point that the RMD by itself pushes past the 12% edge. From that year to the end of the plan, Philip is in the 22% bracket on income he never chose to realizeA gain that has been locked in by selling — and, in a taxable account, the moment it becomes taxable. Until then it is unrealized: the holding is worth more than you paid, but nothing has happened yet and no tax is due. The unused hatched room is not free. It is a cheap bucket you declined to fill, and the rules will eventually fill an expensive one in its place.

…but do not read that as "converting wins"

On this same path, honestly:

  • Total taxes paid are higher with the policy on: $682,350 vs $632,419.
  • Terminal net worth is lower with the policy on: $8.03M vs $8.33M.

Converting pays tax earlier, out of the taxable brokerage, and those dollars stop compoundingGrowth on top of previous growth — this year's return is earned on last year's gains as well as the original money. It is why a plan curve bends upward instead of climbing in a straight line, and why the early years matter more than their size suggests.Full entry →. That is a real cost, and this chart does not net it out for you.

The terminal comparison is also not apples to apples: net worth counts a pre-taxMoney that went in before income tax was taken — the traditional bucket. Not the same as untaxed: the tax was deferred, not forgiven, and it comes due as ordinary income on the way out. A pre-tax balance is worth less to spend than the same number in a Roth.Full entry → traditional dollar at face value. The un-converted path still holds roughly $2.3M of traditional money at the end (implied by its 2054 requirement of $262,303 and the age-95 Uniform Lifetime divisor of 8.9), against about $0.7M on the converted path — and every one of those extra dollars carries an unpaid tax bill at 22% or more. Adjust for that and the gap closes and reverses.

For the math lovers — adjusting net worth for the unpaid tax

A required withdrawal is the prior year-end traditional balance divided by the Uniform Lifetime divisor, so the balance behind the un-converted path's 2054 requirement is

\[ B \approx 262{,}303 \times 8.9 \approx \$2.33\text{M} \]

The converted path holds about $0.7M, so the un-converted path carries about $1.6M more pre-taxMoney that went in before income tax was taken — the traditional bucket. Not the same as untaxed: the tax was deferred, not forgiven, and it comes due as ordinary income on the way out. A pre-tax balance is worth less to spend than the same number in a Roth.Full entry → money. At a 22% rate, the tax still owed on that difference is

\[ 0.22 \times \$1.6\text{M} \approx \$0.36\text{M} \]

The raw gap in net worth is $8.33M − $8.03M = $0.30M. The unpaid tax is larger than the gap, so once it is counted, the policy-on path comes out ahead.

The Brackets lens answers "who is filling my brackets, and at what rate?" It does not answer "should I convert?" For that, read success, taxes paid, and terminal together, as Roth conversion in the window sets out.

Inside the indigo RMD band there is a second mark — an outline showing what the rules actually required that year. The filled band is what got taxed.

They come apart when base income stops covering the standard deduction, because the leftover deduction is then absorbed out of the forced dollars instead.

On this plan they come apart only at the very end — and that is worth knowing before you go looking. Base income covers the deduction for most of the horizon: first the pension, then, once it is gone, the interest and dividends the taxable holdings keep paying. So for year after year the outline sits flush on the fill:

A 1% gap on a full-height column is two or three pixels. Do not expect to see daylight there. The number is in the tooltip and the table; the drawing only separates visibly once far more of the deduction spills into the forced dollars.

Where it does show. Keep going to the last columns ('52–'54). Base income is gone for good by then, the account is being run down, and the whole of the standard deduction is being absorbed out of a shrinking requirement — so the outline lifts off and widens every year after, from a few percent when it opens to a clearly visible slice by the final year, when close to a fifth of what the rules require is no longer being taxed at all. That is the picture this mark exists to draw, and the reason it only appears on a plan that runs its account down with nothing else left to tax.

So read the flush outline through the middle of the plan as the other half of the lesson: your own income is still covering the deduction, so every forced dollar is landing in a bracket. The year the outline lifts off is the year that stops being true.

Above the chart, Room to chooses what the hatched cap is measured against:

Room to:  [ Current bracket ]  [ 10% ]  [ 12% ]  [ 22% ]  [ 24% ]  [ 32% ]  [ 35% ]

Current bracket measures to the top of whatever class this year's income landed in — "how much before the next rate?" Picking a rate measures to the top of that class instead — "how much room to the 22% line?" — which is the question you ask when deciding how far to convert. A plan carrying a fill-to-bracket policy starts on its own rate; every other plan starts on Current bracket.

The cap is not a parallel calculation. It is the same figure the conversion strategy fills to, read from the engine, so the room you see and the room a policy targets cannot drift apart.

On this plan, 12% and Current bracket look identical — that is the readout

Select 12% here and then Current bracket, and nothing moves. That is not a broken control. A fill-to-bracket policy converts until income sits exactly on the top of its target class, and that edge is simultaneously the top of the class the income lands in. Both ceilings are the same number, both caps are zero, and neither draws a hatch.

Two settings agreeing is the pass condition — the policy filled its bucket precisely, with nothing spilled into the next rate up. The interesting move is to pick a different rate:

PickWhat you learn on this plan
