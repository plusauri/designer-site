On the right now: Stayed the course

Stay the course

The situation Robin and Jae Marsh are 52 and 50 and five years from retiring when a crash like 2008 arrives. One version of them keeps contributing and rebalancing back to their mix. The other moves their 401(k)s and Roth IRAs to cash after the fall and buys back in three years later, once things feel safe. What does the second version cost, and is there any crash it would have dodged?

"Stay the course" is the advice John BogleInvestors who follow the ideas of John C. Bogle, who founded Vanguard and in 1976 launched the first index fund sold to individual investors: own broad index funds, keep costs low, choose a simple mix and hold it through good years and bad. The community gathers on the forum and wiki at bogleheads.org.Full entry → gave for market falls, and it is easy to agree with on a calm day. It is harder in a year like 2008, when a balanced portfolio loses a fifth of its value and every headline says it could get worse. This lesson puts that crash into a saving couple's plan five years before they retire, and compares two versions of them: one that keeps contributing and rebalancing, and one that moves its retirement accounts to cash after the fall and buys back in once things feel safe. Then it slides the crash to other years, to ask whether selling on a schedule ever works.

Robin and Jae Marsh are the couple from Costs you keep paying. They are 52 and 50, they save about $45,000 a year, and they hold about $1.1 million in three index funds: roughly 61% US stocks, 16% international stocks, 22% bonds and a little cash. Robin retires in 2036.

  • Stayed the course holds that mix every year. When a fall leaves stocks below their share, it rebalancesSelling some of what grew and buying what lagged, to bring the mix back to the target you chose. Without it a portfolio quietly drifts toward whatever has been winning, and gets riskier than intended precisely when it feels safest.Full entry → inside the 401(k)sAn employer-sponsored retirement account, named after the section of tax law that created it — so the name says nothing about what it does. Contributions are usually payroll-deducted, often matched by the employer, and come in traditional (pre-tax) and Roth flavors. A 403(b) is the same idea for schools and nonprofits, a 457 for government workers.Full entry → and RothA retirement account where you pay income tax on the money going in, and qualified withdrawals later come out tax-free — the mirror image of traditional. Named after the senator behind the law, so the word tells you nothing on its own. Plans care because Roth dollars do not add to taxable income later, which keeps them clear of income-driven cliffs.Full entry → IRAs, selling some bonds to buy stocks.
  • Sold after the fall is the same plan until the fall. Then it moves the 401(k)s and IRAs to cash for three years, and in 2035 it goes back to the original mix.

The second plan sells inside the retirement accounts, which is where most savers' money is and where a sale owes no tax. The brokerage account stays invested in both. The difference between the plans is the decision, not a tax bill.

Choose the Global Financial Crisis of 2007 and start it in 2031. Its first year is 2007, the market's last good year, so the large fall lands in 2032, and the recovery years of 2009 and 2010 follow. Market crashA rare, sharp fall across markets — 2008 and March 2020 are the recent ones — lasting months to a couple of years and arriving perhaps once a decade. Not the everyday case, and not a prediction: it matters in planning because these are the moments correlations rise toward +1, so holdings that usually differ fall together and diversification helps least exactly when it is needed most.Full entry → episodes replay real years in order, for every simulated path. Here is the plan that stays the course, with the crash in place.

Stayed the course, with the 2007 crisis starting in 2031 (the red years on the axis). Net worth falls sharply in 2032 and is back above its pre-crash level by 2034. Robin retires in 2036 with about $2.8 million.

Two things work for the Marshes while prices are low. Their contributions keep buying, and each dollar buys more shares than it did before the fall. And the rebalancing sells bonds, which held up, to buy stocks, which fell. Both are a purchase at a discount, and both are repaid in the recovery years. On this plan the crash leaves the Marshes slightly better off at the medianThe middle outcome — half above, half below — as against the average, which a few enormous results can drag upward. Over long horizons the two come apart badly, and the median is the one that answers "what does a typical path look like?"Full entry → than a plan with no crash at all, because the episode ends with years of strong recovery that they were fully invested for.

Switch to Sold after the fall. The fall is the same. What follows is not.

The asset mix of Sold after the fall. From 2032 to 2034 about 70% of the portfolio is cash: the retirement accounts were sold after the fall, while the brokerage stayed invested. In 2035 the mix returns to its targets.

Sold after the fall, with the same crash. The fall in 2032 is the same, but the recovery years pass the cash by. Robin retires in 2036 with about $2.1 million, about $600,000 less than the plan that stayed, and the gap keeps widening for the rest of their lives.

The cost did not come in the year they sold. In 2031 the two plans were worth exactly the same, and the fall in 2032 hit both. The cost came in the years they were out: the recovery that followed the bottom happened without them, and when they bought back in, they bought at higher prices than they sold at. A temporary fall became a permanent loss.

Over 1,000 simulated futures with the same crash, the difference shows on the outcome cards.

Stayed the course: a median ending net worth of about $7.3 million, and success on about 90% of paths.

Sold after the fall, on the same axis: a median of about $3.1 million, and success on about 74% of paths.

The plan that sold has its dates written in: cash from 2032 to 2034, whatever the market does. Slide the crash and see what that rule does when the fall comes at another time.

With the crash in 2029, the fall lands in 2030 and the plan sells two years later, into the recovery, and misses the rest of it. With the crash in 2033, the crash happens to arrive while the plan is already in cash, and it does slightly better. That one row is market timing working, and it works only because the crash landed inside the one window when the plan was out. Nobody gets to choose that window in advance. A decision made during a fall is made after the fall, which is the row that costs the most.

The plan that stayed the course did nothing clever. It held a written target and rebalanced back to it every year. That is the whole of an investment policy statement, a plan many do-it-yourself investors write before they need it: a target mix, when to rebalance, and what they will do in a crash. In Tutilis the target is the plan's glide path, under AssumptionsA main-rail area. The rules the forecast runs under — growth, taxes, movements, success definition, locality, currency. A glide pathA planned shift in allocation over time — classically toward bonds as retirement approaches, so risk comes down as the years available to recover from a loss come down with it. The word that matters is *planned*: a portfolio drifting toward whatever has been winning is not on a glide path, it is unrebalanced.Full entry → with one target held every year is a constant mix; one that steps down toward retirement is the usual shape for a household that wants less risk later. Glide paths through retirement compares those shapes.

  • The crash is one historical episode. Other falls have been shallower, deeper, quicker or slower to recover. The Sequence-of-returns risk modifiable offers several.
  • The sale is inside the retirement accounts only. Selling in a taxable accountAn ordinary taxable investment account — no special retirement wrapper, no contribution limit, and no withdrawal age rules. The trade-off is that dividends and realized gains are taxed as they happen rather than deferred. Often called the taxable bucket next to traditional and Roth.Full entry → would also realizeA gain that has been locked in by selling — and, in a taxable account, the moment it becomes taxable. Until then it is unrealized: the holding is worth more than you paid, but nothing has happened yet and no tax is due. The same word cuts the other way: a loss is only realized once you sell, which is why a downturn you ride through costs nothing you did not sell.Full entry → gains and owe tax on them.
  • Cash earns the money market rate, and the plan buys back at the year's price. Buying back in happens on a particular day, often after the best days of a recovery have already passed.
  • People are not plans. The hardest part of staying the course is the year itself, which no forecast can make easier. Writing the rule down beforehand is the closest thing to it.

A crash that arrives while you are still saving is a sale you get to buy into, as long as you keep buying and rebalancing. Selling after the fall turns a temporary loss into a permanent one, and the cost arrives in the recovery years you sit out. A rule to sell on a schedule wins only when the next crash happens to land while you are out of the market, which is a window nobody can pick.
