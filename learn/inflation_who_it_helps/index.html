A rise in prices helps someone who is repaying a fixed loan with a paycheck that rises too, and it squeezes someone whose check rises more slowly than the grocery bill. A fixed dollar payment flips when pay moves with prices. Cash flips the other way: a stack of dollars buys less when prices rise, and more when they fall. A check that is indexed, and that this plan holds at zero when you ask for a fall, is helped by the rise and helped again by the fall.

A plan can show you the household after prices have moved. The argument about why they moved is older than any plan, and it is worth having in mind before you move a slider.

Inflation is a rise in the price level. The same basket of goods costs more dollars than it did. People usually tell three stories about why that happens.

One story is that people try to buy more than is being produced. Sellers can raise prices and still find a buyer. That is the story often told about 2021 and 2022 in the United States. The twelve-month change in CPI-UConsumer Price Index — a measure of how the price of a fixed basket of goods changes. CPI-U is the index for all urban consumers, and it is the basket this product's ordinary-spending growth is read from. Social Security's cost-of-living adjustment uses a related index, CPI-W, covering urban wage earners. A plan's inflation number is a long-run assumption, and it is separate from next year's print.Full entry → was 9.1 percent in June 2022.

Another story is that making things costs more. Wages, fuel, or a broken supply line raise the cost of producing, and prices follow. The oil shocks of the late 1970s are told this way. CPI-U was up 14.8 percent over the twelve months ending March 1980.

A third story is that people raise prices because they expect prices to rise. Workers ask for raises because they expect the basket to cost more. Firms raise prices because they expect wages and supplies to cost more. The expectation keeps itself going. HyperinflationA rise in prices so fast that money stops working as a way to hold value. Philip Cagan's 1956 line is a month when prices rise 50 percent or more. Germany in 1923, Hungary in 1946, Zimbabwe in 2008, and Venezuela in the 2010s passed it. A grocery slider on a household plan is a stress for one bill, and it stops far below a month like that.Full entry → is that loop running without a brake. Philip Cagan, writing in 1956, drew the line at a month when prices rise 50 percent or more. Germany in 1923, Hungary in 1946, Zimbabwe in 2008, and Venezuela in the 2010s passed it.

Deflation is those same mechanisms pointed the other way. Spending falls short of what is being produced, the cost of making things falls, or people wait to buy because they expect goods to be cheaper later. In the United States the consumer price index fell in each year from 1930 through 1933. The annual average was down 2.7 percent in 1930, 8.9 percent in 1931, 10.3 percent in 1932, and 5.2 percent in 1933. The steepest twelve-month drop was 10.8 percent, from the autumn of 1931 to the autumn of 1932.

People describe inflation as pernicious, and they write about the ravages of inflation, because a rise in prices makes wages and saved cash buy less. That harm is real. The same rise helps a debtor. A debtor is someone who owes cash in the future. The dollars they pay back buy less than the dollars they borrowed, so the debt stings less.

Did you know?

Some people benefit from inflation. Those people are debtors. A debtor owes cash in the future, and inflation means they pay that debt back in dollars that are worth less. The payment stings less. Maya's mortgage is that debt on this plan. The payment stays about $17,000 while her wages rise.

That benefit belongs to someone who already owes a fixed stack of dollars. It is a thin reason to take on a new loan. Anyone whose paycheck lags the grocery bill still buys less each year, and the lighter debt sits beside that harm. The payment is cash, due on a schedule, in years when prices may rise, stall, or fall. A lender who expects prices to rise usually charges for that expectation in the interest rate, so part of the lighter future dollar is charged up front. If the paycheck fails to rise, or if prices and pay both fall, that same payment takes a larger share of income. Prices fall, the loan stays is that turn on this plan. What the money bought matters as well. Maya's loan pays for a house she lives in. A loan taken out to spend, against a paycheck that may not keep up, is a different arrangement.

Deflation is a fall in the price level, and when Maya's pay falls with prices the fixed mortgage payment becomes a larger share of that pay.

Her pay fell, so the payment's share of that pay rose.

Now put the wage slider back where it opened, and leave groceries below zero. The paycheck holds. Groceries get cheaper. The payment is the same stack of dollars against a paycheck that stayed put, so the loan keeps the weight it had. A contract that moves with prices flips. A paycheck that stays sticky while only prices fall leaves the borrower holding a cheaper grocery basket and the same payment.

Economists dread deflation because a fall in prices can feed on itself, even though cheaper goods sound like a gift.

Lower prices help a household whose paycheck holds still. Groceries cost less, and the same wages buy more. The dread begins when the fall continues and incomes fall with it.

People wait to buy when they expect the same goods to cost less next month. Shops sell less. Employers cut hours, wages, or jobs, and paychecks shrink. Debts promised in a fixed number of dollars then take a larger share of the smaller pay. Households that are squeezed spend less, and some sell what they own to keep the payments current. Waiting and selling push prices down again. Each step gives the next step a reason to keep going. That loop is a deflationary spiral.

One turn of the loop is already on this plan. Stay on Nominal. Select Living expenses and Maya's wages, and slide both growth rates below zero. The grocery line and the paycheck bend down. The mortgage payment stays about $17,000, so it is a larger share of the smaller paycheck. A household in that spot spends less and has a harder time keeping the next payment current. This plan's picture is that heavier payment. The shops selling less, and the paychecks shrinking again, are the turns after it.

Did you know?

Social Security paid a 0 percent COLA in 2010, in 2011, and in 2016. In each of those years the price index the program uses finished the measuring period no higher than its previous peak, and the law holds the benefit still. The benefit stayed at its previous dollar amount. The slider on this plan stops at the same floor.

A pension at 1 percent, or slid to zero, is a milder version of the same floor. It can lag the grocery bill for decades. Ask the what-if for a fall in prices and the pension holds at that floor.

Works cited — the publications behind the figures

  • Congressional Research Service, Social Security: Cost-of-Living Adjustments (94-803). The COLA is based on CPI-W, and the benefit stays at its previous dollar amount when that index is flat or lower.
  • TreasuryDirect, Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS). Principal follows the consumer price index, and a TIPS pays at least its original principal at maturity.
  • U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Consumer Price Index news release for June 2022. CPI-U rose 9.1 percent over the twelve months ending June 2022.
  • U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Consumer prices up 8.5 percent for the year ended March 2022. The table of earlier twelve-month changes includes March 1980 at 14.8 percent.
  • U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, One hundred years of price change, Monthly Labor Review, April 2014. The largest twelve-month decrease in 1929–1941 was 10.8 percent, September 1931 to September 1932, and the same drop the next month.
  • Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis, Consumer Price Index, 1913–. Annual average percent change: 1930 −2.7, 1931 −8.9, 1932 −10.3, 1933 −5.2. The series is the BLS index.
  • Philip Cagan, "The Monetary Dynamics of Hyperinflation," in Milton Friedman, editor, Studies in the Quantity Theory of Money, University of Chicago Press, 1956. Hyperinflation begins in the month prices rise by more than 50 percent.
