A retired household lives on cash, and the portfolio produces cash in several different ways. A bond fund pays interest. A stock fund pays dividends. Selling shares turns part of the account into money in the bank. An IRAIndividual Retirement Account — a retirement wrapper you open yourself rather than through an employer. It comes in traditional (pre-tax going in) and Roth (taxed going in) flavors, so naming the flavor matters more than the acronym does.Full entry → withdrawal does the same thing from a different kind of account. Every one of these is cash you can spend, and the tax code treats nearly every one of them differently. This lesson follows one retired couple through three versions of the same plan to show what each kind of income costs, which ones you get to time, why a sale of long-held shares is often the cheapest cash a retiree has, and which account each kind of income belongs in.

Ruth is 65 and Victor is 64, and both have stopped working. They live in Florida, which has no state income tax, so every tax figure in this lesson is federal. They spend about $96,000 a year. Ruth will claim Social Security at her full retirement age in 2028 and Victor at his in 2029, and until then the portfolio pays for everything.

They have about $1.4 million, which is comfortable without being rich. It sits in four places.

The brokerage account has been building for decades, so most of its stock fund is gain. The fund is worth $322,000 and the couple paid $150,000 for it. That $150,000 is the cost basisWhat you paid for an investment — the starting point tax measures your gain against. Sell for more than basis and the difference is a taxable gain; the money you originally put in is not taxed again. It is the reason two accounts with identical balances can cost very different amounts to spend from.Full entry →, and it will matter a great deal.

The lesson opens three plans. Each one changes a single thing, so the difference between any two is caused by that one thing.

  • Taxable account first holds the same balanced mix of stocks and bonds, about 64/36, in every account, the way a target-date fund would. When spending runs short it sells from the taxable accounts first, then the IRA, and the Roth last.
  • Every account at once is the same household with the same accounts. It sells from every account in proportion to its size.
  • Bonds in the IRA keeps the same dollars in each account and the same overall mix, but moves the bonds into the IRA and the stocks into the brokerage and the Roth. It sells from every account in proportion, like the second plan.

Two columns in that table do most of the work in retirement. The tax column says that a dollar of interest and a dollar of long-term gain are taxed very differently. The last column says that some of these arrive on someone else's schedule. The next two sections take those columns one at a time.

Interest and dividends arrive whether the household needs them or not. A bond fund pays its interest every month, and a stock fund pays its dividends every quarter. Once paid, they are taxed in that year, and reinvesting them does not change that, as The dividend you never see shows.

Open the tax statement on Taxable account first for 2027. The line Portfolio ordinary is $10,447. That is the interest from the bond fund in the brokerage and from the money market fund in savings. Ruth and Victor did nothing to cause it. It would have arrived in a year of high income just the same, stacked on top of whatever else they had.

The IRA sits between the two. Withdrawals are voluntary until required minimum distributions, or RMDsRequired Minimum Distribution — once you reach an age set by statute, the law makes you withdraw a minimum amount each year from traditional retirement accounts, whether or not you need the money. The withdrawal is taxable income, so RMDs can push a household into a higher bracket or over a surcharge threshold. Roth accounts are generally not subject to them.Full entry →, begin. For Ruth that is 2036, and after that the law decides a minimum every year. On this plan Ruth's RMD is $49,134 in its first year and $107,501 by 2053, and the household has to take it whether they need it or not.

The uncontrolled income is not bad income. It still pays bills, and the usual advice is to spend it before selling anything. The point is that it uses up room on the tax return before you have made any decision, so it should be counted first when you plan the rest of the year.

When you sell shares in a taxable account, most of what comes back is often your own money returning to you. Only the part above the cost basis is a realized gainA gain that has been locked in by selling — and, in a taxable account, the moment it becomes taxable. Until then it is unrealized: the holding is worth more than you paid, but nothing has happened yet and no tax is due. The same word cuts the other way: a loss is only realized once you sell, which is why a downturn you ride through costs nothing you did not sell.Full entry →, and only the gain is taxed. When the shares were held more than a year, that gain is a long-term capital gain, taxed at the lowest rates in the code.

The IRA withdrawal of $43,958 puts all $43,958 on the return. The brokerage sale of $79,438 put only $32,338 there, and none of that was taxed. This is the sense in which a sale of long-held shares is the cheapest cash a retiree has. Part of each dollar is basis, which is never taxed again, and the rest is taxed at rates that start at zero.

For the math lovers — what a sale puts on the return

Write the sale proceeds as \(S\), the shares' value as \(V\) and their cost basis as \(B\). With average-cost basis, each dollar sold carries the same share of basis as the whole holding, so the gain realized is

\[ G = S \times \left(1 - \frac{B}{V}\right) \]

The brokerage stock fund started at \(V = \$322{,}000\) and \(B = \$150{,}000\), so a little more than half of every dollar sold is gain. An IRA withdrawal is the case \(B = 0\) taxed at ordinary rates: the whole of \(S\) is income. A money market fund is the opposite case, \(B = V\), so selling it realizes nothing at all.

Did you know?

A married couple with little other income can realize tens of thousands of dollars of long-term gains a year and owe nothing on them. Some retirees use those years to sell and immediately rebuy winning shares on purpose. That raises their cost basis for free, so the gain they would have paid tax on later disappears. Planners call it gain harvesting.

Most retirement guides, and the large fund companies, give the same rule of thumb for which money to spend first.

  1. Spend what arrives on its own. Interest, dividends, Social Security, a pension and, later, RMDs come whether you want them or not and are taxed either way, so they should pay the bills before anything is sold.
  2. Then sell from the taxable account. Much of each dollar is basis, and the gain is taxed at preferential rates.
  3. Then withdraw from tax-deferred accounts. Every dollar is ordinary income, so these are drawn after the cheaper sources.
  4. Leave the Roth for last. It grows tax-free, it has no required withdrawals for the original owner, and it passes to heirs tax-free.

Taxable account first follows this order. Every account at once ignores it and takes the same share from everything. Tutilis calls these two choices Tax-account order and Pro-rata.

The conventional order does exactly what it promises in the early years. On Taxable account first, Ruth and Victor owe no federal tax at all from 2027 through 2035. Every one of those years, they live on interest, dividends, Social Security and brokerage sales, and the sales land in the 0% band. In 2036 Ruth's first RMD arrives and the bill is $6,303.

The cost shows up later. Because the IRA is never touched, it keeps growing, and when RMDs begin they are larger. By 2045 the RMD on Taxable account first is $77,891, against $51,947 on Every account at once, and by 2053 it is $107,501 against $63,143. The Brackets view shows the shape.

Over the whole plan, the two orders pay similar amounts. Taxable account first pays about $181,000 of federal tax and Every account at once pays about $189,000, a difference of roughly 4% that can turn the other way with a different horizon or market. What differs reliably is when the tax is paid. The conventional order pays nothing for a decade and then pays all of it, mostly at a time when the household can no longer choose.

This is the main criticism researchers make of the rule of thumb. Cook, Meyer and Reichenstein showed in 2015 that a retiree can usually do better than either plan here. The improved approach still lives mostly on the taxable account in the early years, but it also takes just enough from the IRA, or converts just enough to a Roth, to fill the empty 10% and 12% bracketsA band of income taxed at a given rate. The common misreading is that entering a higher bracket taxes all your income at that rate — it does not. Only the dollars inside that band pay the higher rate, so a raise never leaves you with less after tax.Full entry → each year. Filling the cheap buckets shows that technique on its own plan, and Roth conversion in the window shows how to set it up.

The ending balances also need care. In 2053 Taxable account first shows a net worth of about $1.92 million and Every account at once about $1.87 million, but $1.12 million of the first figure sits in the traditional IRA, and every dollar of that is still owed tax. Comparing the totals without noticing which accounts they are in flatters the plan that deferred the most.

The order you draw accounts in is a yearly decision. Which account holds which investment is a decision made once, and it lasts for decades. Planners call it asset location, and the usual guidance follows directly from the tax table.

Going deeper — why this comparison draws from every account at once

You might expect the location plan to use the conventional order, since a household that places its bonds carefully usually spends the taxable account first as well. The trouble is that Tutilis's Tax-account order sells each holding in the taxable accounts in proportion to its size, and it does not rebalance inside the IRA afterward. With all the stocks in the brokerage, selling the brokerage first sells the stocks first, and year after year the household drifts from about 64% stocks toward bonds. Its ending net worth then falls because of the drift, which has nothing to do with taxes.

A real household would avoid that by selling bonds and buying stocks inside the IRA, which costs no tax. Because the forecast does not rebalanceSelling some of what grew and buying what lagged, to bring the mix back to the target you chose. Without it a portfolio quietly drifts toward whatever has been winning, and gets riskier than intended precisely when it feels safest.Full entry → that way, this lesson compares location under Pro-rata, which keeps every holding's share of the portfolio steady, so the only difference between the two plans is where the bonds sit.

A forecast is a model, and this one simplifies a few things that matter for capital gains.

  • Every sale is long-term. Tutilis does not track how long each lot has been held, so it never produces a short-term gain. For a retiree selling shares bought years ago, that is accurate.
  • Basis is averaged. Tutilis uses one average cost for each holding. A real brokerage lets you pick which lots to sell, and choosing the highest-cost lots first can make each sale cheaper than the average shows.
  • There is no gain-harvesting setting. The forecast does not sell and rebuy shares to use up the 0% band on purpose, so the gain harvesting described above is not in these numbers.
  • Preferential income is stacked one kind at a time. When a year has both qualified dividends and long-term gains reaching past the 0% band, Tutilis measures each against ordinary income on its own, which can understate the tax a little. Ruth and Victor stay inside the band in the years this lesson reads.
  • State tax depends on the state. Florida has no income tax. Most states tax capital gains as ordinary income, so in another state the sale would still be cheap federally but not free.
  • The comparison is one path. Every figure is the forecast's middle path. In a bad market the totals change, though the differences in tax character do not.

  • Interest, dividends, capital gains and IRA withdrawals are all cash, and each is taxed differently. Interest and IRA withdrawals are ordinary income. Qualified dividends and long-term gains get rates that start at 0%.
  • Interest, dividends and RMDs arrive on someone else's schedule. Sales and voluntary IRA withdrawals arrive on yours, so plan them around what has already arrived.
  • A sale from a long-held taxable account returns basis untaxed and taxes only the gain at preferential rates. For many retirees it is the cheapest cash available.
  • The conventional order is to spend what arrives first, then sell from taxable accounts, then draw tax-deferred accounts, and leave the Roth for last. It makes the early years cheap, but it can leave low brackets empty and RMDs large, so fill those brackets on purpose.
  • Put interest-paying bonds in tax-deferred accounts, broad stock funds in the taxable account, and the fastest-growing holdings in the Roth. When you compare plans, count the tax still owed inside each account as well as the total.
