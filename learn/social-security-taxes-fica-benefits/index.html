Social Security shows up in two completely different places in a US plan. This lesson names both, shows where each appears on the federal tax statement, and walks you through reading them on a live sample.

ConceptWhat it isWhere you see it
Payroll Social Security (FICA)6.2% employee tax on earned income (wages), wage-base cappedFederal tax statement → Social Security payroll (FICA)
Social Security benefitsYour retirement check — only part may be taxableFederal tax statement → Social Security benefits section

Payroll FICAFederal Insurance Contributions Act — the payroll tax withheld from a paycheck for Social Security and Medicare. An employee pays half and the employer pays the other half. It is separate from income tax, which is why a plan can show income tax and payroll tax as different lines. Someone who works for themselves pays both halves instead (SECA).Full entry → is not “tax on your benefit.” Benefit taxation is not the 6.2% line. Same product word, two IRS systems — keep them separate when you read the statement.

The words Social Security name two separate federal tax systems, and the tax statement shows each one in its own place.

1. Payroll Social Security (FICA) — tax on work

While Alex has Working Income, the federal statement assesses:

  • Social Security payroll (FICA) — ~6.2% of earned income up to the wage base
  • Medicare — ~1.45% of earned income

These are payroll taxes on wages, not a levy on the retirement benefit. BasisWhat you paid for an investment — the starting point tax measures your gain against. Sell for more than basis and the difference is a taxable gain; the money you originally put in is not taxed again. It is the reason two accounts with identical balances can cost very different amounts to spend from.Full entry → is earned income. When wages stop, these lines go to $0 even if Social Security benefits continue.

Think of FICA as the “comes out of the paycheck” Social Security tax — the engine shows it as its own assessed line so it never looks like benefit inclusion.

2. Taxation of Social Security benefits — tax on retirement income

After claim, benefits appear as cash inflow. Federally, benefits are not automatically 100% ordinary income. The IRS uses a provisional-income test (combined incomeHow much of a Social Security benefit counts as taxable income — under US rules none, up to half, or up to 85% of the benefit, depending on other income. Worth separating from the payroll tax that funded it: FICA taxes the wages going in, this taxes the check coming out, and a plan showing both is not double-counting anything.Full entry →): roughly other AGIAdjusted Gross Income — total income minus a specific set of adjustments, and the number many other tax rules build on. It is a midpoint, not your taxable income: deductions still come off after it.Full entry → + half the benefits. That places the household in one of three inclusion bands:

BandRough ideaInclusion of benefits
Below the base thresholdModest other income0% taxable
Middle bandMore other incomeUp to 50%
Upper bandHigher other income / larger planUp to 85%

Thresholds are fixed statutory dollars (they do not inflate with our tax-bracket growth curve). Over long forecasts, that means more of a growing nominalA face-value figure, with no adjustment for inflation. A balance that doubles in nominal terms over 25 years may buy little more than it does today. The counterpart is real, which is inflation-adjusted and closer to what the money will actually buy — always check which one a chart shows.Full entry → benefit can become taxable — that is intentional honesty, not a bug.

Did you know?

The income thresholds that decide whether Social Security is taxed have never been adjusted for inflationA rise in the price level. The same basket of goods costs more dollars than it did. A household plan carries a growth rate on each bill, because one published inflation number is one basket. The reporting rate that restates future dollars into today's dollars is a separate assumption.Full entry →. Congress set them at $25,000 for a single filer and $32,000 for a married couple in 1983, when they reached only a small share of retirees. Today about half of the families who receive Social Security pay some income tax on it.

What the statement must show (audit rows):

RowMeaning
Benefits receivedFull annual benefit (cash reality)
Taxable Social SecurityAmount included in ordinary income after 0/50/85%
Adjusted gross incomeSparse federal AGI used for this worksheet (and future cliffs)
Ordinary income (assessed)Uses the taxable inclusion — not the full check

If Taxable Social Security is less than Benefits received, the plan is not overstating federal ordinary tax on benefits the way a naïve “tax 100% of SS” model would.

3. State treatment (bonus — not the federal inclusion)

This sample files Ohio. Many states exclude Social Security from the state base entirely. On the state tax statement you may see Social Security excluded under Adjustments — that is state law, separate from the federal 0/50/85% inclusion. Do not mix the two tables.

Work these steps on the plan on the right. The first reads a working year, the second reads a year after the claim, and the third is an optional contrast sample.

A. Working year — payroll only

  1. Scrub Year to a pre-retirement year with Working Income (e.g. early in the plan).
  2. Open Taxes → US federal.
  3. Confirm Social Security payroll (FICA) has a non-zero basis (earned income) and tax.
  4. Confirm there is no “Social Security benefits” section yet (benefits not received).
  5. Medicare should also track earned income.

Prefer “FICA is on wages” over any single dollar amount.

B. After claim — benefits evidence

  1. Scrub Year to a year after Social Security has started (e.g. 2036 on this sample — post-FRA claim; Alex is past retirement).
  2. On Taxes → US federal, read totals-first groups (no extra section titles — the parent is the group name):
    • Taxable Social Security → children: benefits received, inclusion % (e.g. 85% — inclusion rate, not a tax rate).
    • Adjusted gross income → children: non-zero AGI ingredients (taxable SS, traditionalA retirement account where the tax is paid on the way out — not a description of an old-fashioned account. Money went in before income tax, grew untaxed, and is taxed as ordinary income when you take it out — traditional 401(k)s and IRAs work this way. The contrast is Roth (taxed going in, tax-free coming out) and taxable (brokerage, taxed as you go).Full entry →, LTCGLong-Term Capital Gain — profit on something held longer than a year, taxed on its own lower schedule instead of at ordinary rates. The holding period is the whole trick: the same sale a day earlier is taxed as ordinary income.Full entry → when present, …). This is the household AGI.
    • Ordinary taxable incomeWhat the rates are finally applied to — income after adjustments and deductions, not what you earned. It sits at the bottom of the page: gross income, then AGI, then this. Most people's taxable income is meaningfully smaller than their pay, which is why a bracket alone never tells you the tax.Full entry → → children: ordinary ingredients + standard deductionThe flat amount most households subtract instead of itemizing individual deductions. You take the larger of the two, so it acts as a floor: below it, an extra deductible expense saves nothing at all, which surprises people who gave to charity expecting a break.Full entry → (no LTCG).
    • Total tax → children: ordinary / LTCG / FICA / Medicare lines (basis + assessed).
  3. Match Traditional retirement to Money Movements sales marked (tax-deferredTax postponed, not avoided. Growth compounds without a yearly tax drag, and the bill arrives when the money comes out — which is why a large tax-deferred balance and a required withdrawal age are the same conversation.Full entry →).
  4. Social Security payroll (FICA) should be $0 if there is no earned income that year.

C. Optional contrast — Social Security Only

Open Social Security Only (social_security_only) in a second tab: person + SS template, no portfolio wages. On modest benefit-only years, federal Taxable Social Security often sits at $0 (below the provisional-income base). That is the “we no longer tax the whole check by default” proof in its purest form.

Prefer “inclusion depends on other income” over memorizing the statutory threshold table.

SSA statement (or estimator) lines are often already age-adjusted for a claim ageWhen you start taking Social Security. Claim before full retirement age and the monthly check is permanently smaller; wait past it and it is permanently larger. It is one of the few decisions in a plan that is both free to change beforehand and impossible to change after.Full entry → and implicitly assume you keep earning toward that claim. If the dossier retires much earlier, the pasted monthly amount can be optimistic relative to a stop-work-early path.
