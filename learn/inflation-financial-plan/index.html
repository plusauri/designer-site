Prices on your plan

The situation Maya has a paycheck, a grocery bill, a child starting college, and a fixed-rate mortgage. Each one already grows at its own rate. The question is how to read those rates, and how to stress the one bill that worries you without saving a new plan.

InflationA rise in the price level. The same basket of goods costs more dollars than it did. A household plan carries a growth rate on each bill, because one published inflation number is one basket. The reporting rate that restates future dollars into today's dollars is a separate assumption.Full entry → is a rise in the prices of the things a household buys. Prices rise when people try to buy more than is being produced, when making things costs more, or when people raise prices because they expect prices to rise. The Reyes household starts from a historic default for every growth rate on the plan. Those defaults are reasonable readings of the past. The future can differ from that history, and each rate on this plan is one you can read and one you can change.

Each bill on this plan grows at its own rate. The cash-flow chart draws the dollars of one year, and the rate itself sits on the item's card. The rate is easier to read on the card than on the chart. Look at the bars above the axis. Those are the incomes, and they get bigger from year to year, with a slight curve along their tops. The bars below the axis grow downward, and their tops form a slight curve as well. A bigger bar is the growth, and the percent for that growth is the number on the card. The slight curve is compoundingGrowth on top of previous growth — this year's return is earned on last year's gains as well as the original money. It is why a plan curve bends upward instead of climbing in a straight line, and why the early years matter more than their size suggests.Full entry →. Each year's growth is added to the growth that came before.

Here is 2028, with Sam in college. The bars are that year's dollars. Cost growth on that card reads N(4.5%, 2%, μg=4.5%). That is the catalog rate for college prices. The 4.5 percent is the average, the 2 percent is the volatility, and μg is the rate the bill compounds at, about 4.5 percent a year across the four years it is on the plan, starting in 2027. College prices have run ahead of the grocery basket for a long time, and the catalog keeps that gap. The Cost growth slider on Sam's college in TransformsA main-rail area. Living expenses rise about 2.8 percent a year. The card for Living expenses names that row Spending growth, and it reads N(2.8%, 1.5%, μg=2.8%). That is the long-run CPI-UConsumer Price Index — a measure of how the price of a fixed basket of goods changes. CPI-U is the index for all urban consumers, and it is the basket this product's ordinary-spending growth is read from. Social Security's cost-of-living adjustment uses a related index, CPI-W, covering urban wage earners. A plan's inflation number is a long-run assumption, and it is separate from next year's print.Full entry → rate the catalog uses for a household's ordinary spending. Maya's wages rise about 3.5 percent a year until she retires. The card for Maya's wages names that row Growth, and it reads N(3.5%, 1.5%, μg=3.5%). From 2027 to her last working year the paycheck roughly doubles, which is what about 3.5 percent a year does over those twenty years. The Growth slider on Maya's wages opens on that rate.

There is no single inflation number in the model, and there is no single inflation number in the prices a household pays. Costs tend to rise together over time, and they rise at different rates.

The mortgage payment is the same number of dollars every year, and those dollars buy less of the grocery basket as prices rise.

The Display menu in the top bar has two ways to read a dollar, under Purchasing power. Nominal shows each amount in the money of its own year. Today's $ restates every future amount into today's purchasing power. The choice is display basisWhich dollars the numbers are shown in — nominal face value, or inflation adjusted to a chosen year's purchasing power. It changes the display and never the forecast: the same run reads very differently in the two, which is why every chart says which one it is drawn in.Full entry →. It changes the picture and leaves the plan alone. Real vs nominal returns is the longer visit to the same pair of readings.

Stay on Nominal and open CF ChartA Forecast Detail chip. CF = cash flows. Money in above the line, money out below, by category over the years. The net line, drawn in thick black, is net cash flow — often the thing you came for.. Hover a year. The tip lists Home mortgage as one payment, about $17,000, with the principal and the interest on the lines under it. That total is the same in 2027 and in a later year such as 2035. The Payment row on the mortgage card is the same number, written once for the whole loan.

Switch to Today's $. Hover a later year. The same payment is drawn as fewer of today's dollars. This plan restates future dollars at 3.5 percent a year, the reporting rate in its assumptions. A dollar promised twenty years from now is drawn as less than a dollar today, so a payment that is flat in the checkbook looks lighter in today's groceries.

That is the seed of the next lesson. The loan's dollar amount held still. Maya's paycheck rose. The payment's weight fell. Prices can help the person who owes a fixed stack of dollars.

A household with three children heading toward college does the same thing on its own plan: select the college line, move that one slider, and read the chart. The other bills keep the rates they had.

A hard year for prices is something you can lay over the grocery bill, and the slider shows how far this plan will take that trial.

Select Living expenses on the same Modifiables card.

Spending growth opens near 2.8 percent. The slider runs to about three times that rate on either side of where it opened, so this grocery bill can be set near 8 percent, or near 8 percent below zero. Slide it toward the top and watch the living-expense line on Nominal. Everything else holds. That is a household asking what a faster spell of prices would do to its own grocery bill.

Did you know?

The twelve-month change in CPI-U was 9.1 percent in June 2022, and 14.8 percent in March 1980. The top of this slider, near 8 percent, sits in the neighborhood of that 2022 peak for a single year, and it stops short of 1980. HyperinflationA rise in prices so fast that money stops working as a way to hold value. Philip Cagan's 1956 line is a month when prices rise 50 percent or more. Germany in 1923, Hungary in 1946, Zimbabwe in 2008, and Venezuela in the 2010s passed it. A grocery slider on a household plan is a stress for one bill, and it stops far below a month like that.Full entry →, in Philip Cagan's 1956 definition, begins in a month when prices rise 50 percent or more. Germany in 1923, Hungary in 1946, Zimbabwe in 2008, and Venezuela in the 2010s passed that line. The slider is a stress for Maya's grocery bill. A month like those sits far above it. Who inflation helps writes out the presumed causes of a rise and of a fall, and the drop in United States consumer prices from 1930 through 1933.

The other direction of the same slider is a fall in prices. Who inflation helps uses it, together with Maya's paycheck, the pension, and Social Security, to ask who gains when prices rise and who gains when they fall.
