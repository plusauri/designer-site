Ines is retired in the United States and spends in dollars. Her income is a pension from the years she worked in Lisbon, paid in euros. Most of her savings sit in a ladder of British government bonds, in pounds, and the rest is a savings account in Tokyo, in yen. A modest US brokerageAn ordinary taxable investment account — no special retirement wrapper, no contribution limit, and no withdrawal age rules. The trade-off is that dividends and realized gains are taxed as they happen rather than deferred. Often called the taxable bucket next to traditional and Roth.Full entry → account is the only thing she owns that is priced in the currency she lives in.

This is a household whose retirement is a bet on three exchange rates, whether or not anyone thought of it that way. This lesson is about what a forecast can say about that bet, and about the one thing this forecast can say that a fixed-rate forecast cannot: how much of her spread is the currencies themselves.

Open Assumptions → Currency on the first plan. Under the rate table there is a two-way choice, Exchange rates: Fixed | Modeled, and it is set to Fixed. That setting is the whole opt-in. A plan that has never touched it converts every year of every path at the rates in the table, exactly as it did before the model existed, and its forecast does not change by a cent.

The same panel on the modeled plan. Exchange rates is Modeled, and each rate becomes a path of its own.

The model changes nothing about how a plan is written, either. The pension is still recorded in euros, the bond ladder in pounds, the savings in yen. What Modeled changes is the rate the forecast uses when it converts those amounts to dollars in a given year of a given path. With Fixed, that rate is the same number every time. With Modeled, it is a path of its own.

Before asking whether the rates matter, look at how much of the plan they touch. The forecast reports exposureHow much of the portfolio moves with a given kind of risk — equity exposure, currency exposure. More exposure means more of the upside and more of the swings; a glide path is a schedule for changing it over time.Full entry → whether or not the rates are modeled, because it is a fact of the plan rather than of the model.

About two thirds of her assets are pounds, a fifth are yen, and a sixth are dollars. The dollar slice grows a little as the US equities compound and the rest is drawn down, but the shape holds to the horizon. Her income is starker still: every euro of it is the Lisbon pension, and every dollar she spends has to be bought with one of the three foreign currencies first.

That is the shape of her currency risk. The assets that fund her retirement are foreign; the retirement they fund is in dollars. Nothing about it is unusual for someone who has lived and worked in more than one country, and nothing about it shows up in a forecast that holds the rates still.

The model does not draw an exchange rate out of thin air, and it does not simply give each currency a return and a volatilityHow much an investment's returns bounce around from year to year, not whether it goes up or down overall. Written σ (sigma) in the product. Two holdings can have the same average return and feel completely different to live through — and high volatility drags the growth you actually compound below that average.Full entry → the way a fund gets one. It builds each year's change from two pieces that economists have argued about for a century and mostly agree on over long horizons.

The first piece is drift from inflation. If prices in one country rise faster than prices in another, that country's currency tends to lose value against the other's by about the difference. This is relative purchasing power parity, and over decades it is the most reliable thing anyone can say about where a currency is headed. In the model, every region's inflation is stated relative to the plan's own reporting inflation for the United States. A region with a zero spread is believed to inflate at the same pace as the US, and its currency has no drift. This is also why a high-inflation currency cannot stay strong by accident: the drift is tied to the inflation the rest of the forecast already uses.

The second piece is the real exchange rate, which is the rate after both countries' price levels are taken out. It is where the genuine currency risk lives. Real rates swing, sometimes by a quarter or more, but they do not swing forever. They tend to come back toward a long-run level over a few years. The model states this as a half-life: the number of years it takes for half of a deviation to close, four years for the major floating currencies. Each year the real rate takes a random step and then leans back toward its long-run level, so a forty-year path cannot wander off to a valuation no currency has ever held.

The nominalA face-value figure, with no adjustment for inflation. A balance that doubles in nominal terms over 25 years may buy little more than it does today. The counterpart is real, which is inflation-adjusted and closer to what the money will actually buy — always check which one a chart shows.Full entry → rate you see is the product of the two. It is not given a volatility of its own, which is deliberate: if the nominal rate and the real rate were sampled separately they could tell contradictory stories. Every number the model uses, per currency, is listed under Assumptions → Currency and printed by assumptions.fx-model. Most of them are shared-world assumptions, like the rate at which tax brackets are indexed, rather than something each household is asked to type. The one exception is the inflation spread, which is a belief a household can reasonably hold for itself, and the next section shows where to state it.

For the math lovers — the drift and the half-life

Drift. With US inflation \(\pi_{US}\) and the other region's inflation \(\pi_c\), the expected yearly change in the rate, in logs, is

\[ \ln\frac{1+\pi_{US}}{1+\pi_c} \]

so over many years the expected rate is simply the ratio of the two price levels.

The real rate. Write the real rate's log distance from its long-run level as \(x_t\). Each year it keeps a share \(\rho\) of that distance and takes a random step:

\[ x_{t+1} = \rho\,x_t + \sigma_q\,\varepsilon_{t+1}, \qquad \rho = 0.5^{1/h} \]

A deviation shrinks to \(\rho^h = 0.5\) of itself after \(h\) years, which is what a half-life means. With a four-year half-life, \(\rho = 0.5^{1/4} \approx 0.84\).

The nominal rate you see is the real rate carried along by the price-level ratio, so it gets no volatility of its own.

The deterministic forecast, the GMFGMF ✦ (also FID 0 / GMF) is the product north star — the deterministic geometric mean forecast path you steer by, not a random Monte Carlo draw and not “most likely.” Prefer it for a stable year-by-year read of the plan. Switch away when you want to explore other randomized scenarios or paths in the multi-path cloud. The ✦ mark matches the live FID control (compass / north star, not a rating star).Full entry →, draws no random steps. On the second plan its exchange-rate path is flat: a pound is worth the same in dollars in 2053 as in 2027. That is not the model declining to work. It is the model saying that, with no stated inflation differential and today's rate taken as fair value, the expected rate is today's rate. The spread you will see in a moment comes entirely from the paths around that expectation.

The third plan states one belief: Britain inflates 1.5 points a year faster than the United States. Nothing else is different. The belief lives in the rate table on Assumptions → Currency, in the Inflation vs US column, which is editable once Exchange rates is Modeled. The pound's row reads 1.5% there, and every other row reads zero. In the parameter table below it, the pound's source changes from the system default to the plan itself. Now the GMF path of the pound tilts. Each year the pound loses about 1.4% of its dollar value, which is exactly the ratio of the two inflation rates, and by 2053 it sits almost a third below where it started. The euro and the yen, with no stated spread, stay flat beside it.

Because two thirds of what Ines owns is pounds, that one belief takes the expected terminal net worth from a little under a million dollars to a little over half of one. Nothing in the plan changed but a single assumption about two countries' inflation, and the forecast moved by more than most edits to the plan itself would move it. You can read the path off forecast.fx-path for the expected forecast, and it is the cleanest way to check what a stated spread does before trusting a Monte CarloThe method behind a multi-path run: draw many randomized return paths, run the plan down each one, and read the spread of what happened. Named after the casino, because the engine is repeated random sampling rather than a formula — which is why two runs of one plan differ a little, and why a success rate is a proportion of sampled futures rather than a promise.Full entry → built on it.

Every stochastic path draws its own rates. On the second plan, forecast 7 has the pound about 8% below today in the mid-2030s, a quarter above it through the late 2040s, and back to about 15% above at the horizon. The euro spends the same decades a quarter strong and returns to today by 2053. The yen ends a fifth stronger than it started. The three do not move independently: the model correlates real-rate surprises across currencies, so a year in which the dollar strengthens tends to strengthen it against all three, and it correlates each region's inflation surprises with its own currency's real rate.

That is what currency risk looks like inside one path: rates that wander a long way, come back, and wander again, tied together loosely by a common dollar factor. No path is a prediction. The point is that every path is economically possible, which a random walk on the nominal rate would not manage over a retirement.

The question Ines actually asked is how much of her spread is the currencies themselves, and a fixed-rate forecast cannot answer it. To answer it, the forecast runs the same Monte Carlo twice, once with the rates modeled and once with them held at today's, and compares the ending net worth. The Currency risk table in the FX quick view does this on demand; forecast.currency-risk prints the same comparison.

With the rates held still, the cloud of outcomes is what her bond ladder, her savings rate and her equities make it. With the rates modeled, the cloud is wider on both sides: a higher ninetieth percentileA position along outcomes sorted from worst to best. The 10th percentile (P10) is the outcome only one in ten falls below; the 50th is the median; the 90th (P90) is the one only one in ten beats. Forecasts report P10 and P90 to show the downside and upside without leaning on the single best or worst run.Full entry →, because a strong pound or euro is as possible as a weak one, and a lower tenth percentile, because a weak one is too. The standard deviation of her ending net worth is a fifth higher, the floor at the tenth percentile is tens of thousands of dollars lower, and the success rate is a couple of points lower. The last row of the table puts one number on it: about three tenths of the variance in her ending net worth comes from the exchange rates. The rest is the assets.

For the math lovers — how a fifth more spread becomes three tenths of the variance

Variance is the square of the standard deviation. If the modeled rates make the standard deviation \(1.2\) times the fixed-rate one, the variance is \(1.2^2 = 1.44\) times as large, and the share that the exchange rates add is

\[ 1 - \frac{\sigma_{fixed}^2}{\sigma_{modeled}^2} = 1 - \frac{1}{1.44} \approx 0.31 \]

Read that number the way it is meant. It is not a forecast of which way the currencies will go; the expected path is flat. It is a measure of how much of her uncertainty she is carrying in the currencies rather than in the markets, which is what would tell her whether hedging, or holding more of her savings in dollars, is worth its cost. A household with the same foreign share held in equities would find the currencies a smaller part of a larger spread; a household holding foreign bonds and cash, as she does, finds them a large part of a modest one.

Two things make this measurement possible here and not in a spreadsheet with a rate column. The rates live inside the simulation, so every path carries its own rate history alongside its own returns, rather than one static rate multiplied through afterward. And the plan can be run with the rates frozen, so the attribution is a comparison of two honest forecasts rather than a guess about what the currencies contributed.

The currency model does not change how any item grows. The bond ladder still earns what a bond ladder earns, in pounds; the model only changes the rate used to state that pound value in dollars. It does not change US inflation, which stays the plan's own reporting inflation and is the same curve every today's-dollars figure in the app is discounted along. And it does not yet correlate a currency's moves with the returns of the assets held in it. Those are the next steps, and the model is built so they slot in without changing what a plan says.

  • Exchange rates can be part of the simulation, but only for a plan that opts in. A plan that never touches the switch forecasts exactly as before.
  • A moving rate is two things: a drift from the inflation differential between regions, and a real rate that wanders and comes back. The nominal rate follows from both, so no currency can stay strong by accident or wander to an impossible level.
  • The expected path is flat until you state a differential. State one, and the drift is the ratio of the two inflation rates, year after year, and a household that is mostly in that currency moves with it.
  • The forecast can say how much of a household's spread is its currencies by running the same Monte Carlo with the rates modeled and with them held still. For a retiree living on foreign income and foreign bonds, that share is large, and it is the number that says whether the currency risk is worth doing something about.
