Most of the time, major asset classes move together — not against each other. CorrelationHow closely two investments tend to move together, from −1 (opposite) through 0 (unrelated) to +1 (lockstep). Written ρ (rho). It is what decides whether holding several things actually spreads risk: assets that all fall at once are less diversified than a count of holdings suggests.Full entry → (ρThe Greek letter the product uses for correlation — how closely two holdings move together, from −1 (opposite) through 0 (unrelated) to +1 (lockstep). Positive ρ between equities is the normal case rather than the exception, which is why assuming zero quietly understates how much of a portfolio can fall at once. The product lets you run the plan with system ρ applied or not.Full entry →) is that together-ness. DiversificationSpreading money across investments that do not all move together, so a bad year for one is not a bad year for everything. Owning many holdings is not the same thing — if they are highly correlated they behave like one large position wearing several names.Full entry → is not “own more tickers”; it is own risks that do not always move as one. When ρ is positive (the usual case), a mix still has shared risk in the math. Pretending holdings are independent hides that risk.

The product has a switch for exactly this. ρ included (the default) counts the co-movement; ρ not included drops it and treats the holdings as independent. This lesson is about what that switch does to a plan whose holdings really do move together.

About that last row. A stress or crash period is a rare, sharp fall across markets — 2008 and March 2020 are the recent ones — lasting months to a couple of years and arriving perhaps once a decade. It is not the everyday case, and the row is not a prediction. It is there because those are the moments diversification is for, and they are also when it does the least: holdings that usually differ tend to fall together. Planning for them is a matter of sizing, not worry — Crisis honesty below says what the product does and does not do about it.

Read the table one way: the label on a holding does not tell you whether it diversifies. Only ρ does, and only against the thing you already own.

Where ρ comes from. The product resolves ρ from its built-in table of category pairs (US equity × US high yield, and so on), plus any run overrides. A dossier can also store its own dossier_correlation_matrix, but the live Risk / forecast path does not read that field yet. This sample is wired so the categories themselves carry positive ρ.

ρ is the correlation from the overview — how much two holdings move together, from −1 (opposite) through 0 (unrelated) to +1 (lockstep). Three ranges matter:

  • ρ near +1: the mix is almost as jumpy as its ingredients.
  • ρ positive but < 1 (this sample, and most real portfolios): some diversification compared with +1, but the two still share risk — leave that shared part out and the portfolio looks too safe.
  • ρ near 0 or negative: a large free calm-down — special cases (e.g. some stock / Treasury eras), not the everyday default.

No formula needed for the idea. Think of portfolio risk as two piles:

  1. Each holding's own risk, scaled down by how much of the portfolio it is. Half your money in a 16%-volatility holding does not bring 16 points of risk to the mix — it brings much less, because the other half is doing something else.
  2. A together-ness pile: extra risk that exists only because the two holdings tend to fall on the same days. This is the only place ρ appears. Positive ρ makes the pile bigger; ρ of zero makes it vanish; negative ρ turns it into a subtraction.

For the math lovers — the formula

The two piles are the two-asset portfolio variance, with weights \(w\), volatilities \(\sigma\), and correlation \(\rho\):

\[ \sigma_p^2 = w_1^2\,\sigma_1^2 + w_2^2\,\sigma_2^2 + 2\,w_1 w_2\,\sigma_1\sigma_2\,\rho_{12} \]

TermRole
\(w_1^2\sigma_1^2\), \(w_2^2\sigma_2^2\)The own-risk piles — each holding's variance, scaled by its weight squared
\(2 w_1 w_2 \sigma_1 \sigma_2 \rho_{12}\)The together-ness pile — the only place ρ appears

With \(w_1 = w_2 = 0.5\), \(\sigma_1 = 16\), \(\sigma_2 = 10\), \(\rho_{12} = 0.69\):

\[ \sigma_p^2 = 64 + 25 + 2 \cdot 0.25 \cdot 16 \cdot 10 \cdot 0.69 \approx 89 + 55 = 144 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \sigma_p \approx 12.0\% \]

Setting \(\rho_{12} = 0\) (ρ not included) drops the last term: \(\sigma_p = \sqrt{89} \approx 9.4\%\). At \(\rho_{12} = 1\) the expression is a perfect square, \((w_1\sigma_1 + w_2\sigma_2)^2\), so portfolio σ is the weighted average, 13% — no diversification at all.

ρ included vs ρ not included

The product's switch is labeled ρ included and starts on (green): the together-ness pile is counted. Switch it off and the label reads ρ not included: the forecast treats the holdings as independent and the pile is dropped.

True ρEffect of ρ not included
Positive (this sample)Removes a risk-adding pile → too calm; ★ often slides left
NegativeRemoves a benefit → looks riskier; ★ slides right

Planning rule: a portfolio whose holdings move together must keep ρ included for an honest risk figure.

Real ρ is time-varying, and crashes push many pairs toward +1 — co-movement intensifies exactly when it hurts most. The product runs a static category table by default: a clear planning lever, not a full crisis engine. It does not auto-spike in a panic, so a plan that already looks tight with today's ρ is tighter than it reads in one. That is an honesty limit of the current model, stated rather than hidden.

There's no free lunch. TNFL

That is why this is the lesson's last word rather than a footnote: the risk you hide does not leave; it stops being counted.

  • Lead with positive ρ — the everyday default for a portfolio with more than one kind of risk.
  • US equity × high yield is a real positive pair; Treasuries are a different story.
  • ρ not included with true ρ > 0 understates risk.
  • Crises can push ρ toward +1.
  • Path: Risk → Current Risk → Analytic Risk; Holdings; toggle ρ included (label left of switch).
