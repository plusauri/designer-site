The corner everyone wants is high return and low risk — up and to the left. Markets rarely hand that out freely. Most real books sit on a trade-off: more assumed return usually comes with more assumed bounce.

Honesty: μ and σ are assumptions (often researched defaults you can override), not promises and not a forecast of next year’s path. The chart teaches the trade-off, not a prediction.

Two things in that second picture are worth a sentence, because both look like mistakes and neither is. The dashed average line sits above the middle path, and it stays above it — that is not the chart double-counting. A few very good outcomes pull an average up in a way they cannot pull a middle outcome up, and the more the holdings bounce, the wider that gap gets. The middle path is the one to plan against. And the cone keeps widening forever rather than settling down, because each year's uncertainty stacks on the last; the plan is not getting riskier, the question is simply reaching further ahead.

For the math lovers — why the average sits high, and why the cone widens

The gap has a name. CompoundingGrowth on top of previous growth — this year's return is earned on last year's gains as well as the original money. The cone widens as √t. If each year's return is an independent draw, variances add across years while the expected return only accumulates linearly — so the spread grows with the square root of the horizon, σ√t, not with the horizon itself. Four times as long is twice as wide, not four times. That is the shape you are looking at, and it is why a long horizon widens the cone without meaning the holdings changed.

What the three chips put on the chart

Five chips sit above the scatter. Two of them change how dots are drawn — Compare to categories and Size by value, both covered in the walk above. The other three change what is on the chart at all: Real Assets, Cash Flows, Holdings. They are not three views of the same thing — each adds a different kind of item, drawn in its own color, and only some of them count toward the ★.

ChipAddsColor
Real Assetsthe homebrown
Holdingsthe three positions separately (instead of one account dot)blue
Cash Flowssalary (in) and living expense (out)green / rose

All three chips on. Every item type at once: the brown home, the three blue holdings spread from bond to concentrated stock, and — far left, where growth assumptions are calm — the green salary and rose living expense.

Real Assets moves the ★. The star is the blend of your holdings and, when Real Assets is on, your real assets as well. The home expects less return than the stock funds and does not rise and fall with them, so turning it on pulls the star down and to the left. Turning Holdings on only splits the account dot into its positions. The blend is the same money either way, so the star stays where it is.

Cash flows are on the chart but never in the blend. A flow's μ and σ are its growth assumption: how fast the salary rises and how fast the expense inflates. That is a different quantity from an equity's expected return, even though both land on these axes. Read the flow dots as how uncertain each stream's growth is. They do not say what anything earns. A stream of payments is also spent as it arrives, so it is not a balance that can be averaged in with your investments. What Cash Flows adds below the chart is one line: how many times your assets and your incomes, counted at present value, cover your planned spending. It is the same balance Bird's Eye weighs when its view includes cash flows.

The cone below follows the same chip. Below the scatter, Portfolio Range Over Time projects the starred portfolio forward as a widening cone. With Real Assets off, the cone is your holdings alone. With it on, the home joins and everything you own is projected. Those are two honest answers to two different questions: how risky is what I invest, and how risky is what I own. The chip chooses which question both charts answer.

Holdings on

Holdings chip on: each holding is its own dot (this sample: bond-like left, broad equity mid-right, concentrated stock far right). The ★ is the portfolio blend.

What this chart is not: it is not a sell list. A dot far to the right is not a mistake to correct, and nothing here ranks your holdings as keep-or-drop. Two reasons, and either alone is enough. First, the axes are assumptions — long-run μ and σ you or a researched default supplied — so a dot does not move because the market fell last quarter, and a chart that ignores recent prices cannot be telling you to act on them. Second, risk is not damage: a high-σ holding is doing its job if you are paid for the bounce and you can sit through it. What the picture is good for is noticing that you own something whose assumed risk you had not registered — which is a question to go ask, not an answer.

Name, don’t deep-dive: ρ included

Next to the portfolio ★ readout you will see ρThe Greek letter the product uses for correlation — how closely two holdings move together, from −1 (opposite) through 0 (unrelated) to +1 (lockstep). Positive ρ between equities is the normal case rather than the exception, which is why assuming zero quietly understates how much of a portfolio can fall at once. The product lets you run the plan with system ρ applied or not.Full entry → included (default, switch on) or ρ not included when toggled off. That is about how holdings move together, not one holding’s own σ. Full treatment: Diversification and correlation. Here: know it exists — and that turning ρ off is what many tools do by ignoring cross-holding correlationHow closely two investments tend to move together, from −1 (opposite) through 0 (unrelated) to +1 (lockstep). Written ρ (rho). It is what decides whether holding several things actually spreads risk: assets that all fall at once are less diversified than a count of holdings suggests.Full entry → entirely.

For typical positive equity ρ, ignoring those cross terms understates portfolio risk: σₚ falls toward √(Σ wᵢ²σᵢ²) and the ★ often slides left. Negative ρ works the other way (dropping diversificationSpreading money across investments that do not all move together, so a bad year for one is not a bad year for everything. Owning many holdings is not the same thing — if they are highly correlated they behave like one large position wearing several names.Full entry → can push risk up). The correlation card above is the cross terms the toggle is including or zeroing.

Why the ★ sits left of its ingredients. This is the one piece of arithmetic worth carrying out of this lesson. Whenever holdings are less than perfectly correlated — any ρ below 1 — portfolio σ comes in below the value-weighted average of the individual σs, while portfolio μ is exactly the value-weighted average of the individual μs. Return adds; risk does not. That gap is the entire mechanical content of diversificationSpreading money across investments that do not all move together, so a bad year for one is not a bad year for everything. Owning many holdings is not the same thing — if they are highly correlated they behave like one large position wearing several names.Full entry →, and you can watch it on the chart: the ★ sits to the left of where a straight blend of the dots would put it, and the lower the ρs, the further left it sits. Set every ρ to 1 and the gap closes — the ★ lands on the weighted average and the free lunch is gone.

This is variance arithmetic, not counsel: it says what the blend does, and says nothing about which holdings anyone should own.

  • Risk and return belong on one picture for the book you actually hold.
  • Path: Risk → Current Risk → open Analytic Risk; check Holdings.
  • The star is the portfolio; dots are the ingredients — and it sits left of their weighted average whenever ρ < 1. Blending averages the return and less than averages the risk; that gap is what diversification is.
  • Assumptions drive the plot — change μ/σ (or growth categories) and the picture moves. It is not a sell list, and it does not react to last quarter’s prices.
  • The ★ is always your holdings blend — the chips add dots, they do not move it. The cone below the scatter is the chart that follows the Real Assets chip.
  • Correlation is a second axis of honesty; handle it next.
