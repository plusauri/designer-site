Many public programs ask the same question about a household's income: how large is it next to the federal poverty line for a household of this size? The answer is a percentage, written %FPLThe federal poverty level: a yearly guideline, set by household size, that public programs measure income against. Written as a percentage, %FPL is income divided by the guideline for your household, so it moves when the household changes size as well as when income does. Marketplace premium credits, Medicaid and Medicare's Extra Help all test against it.Full entry →. The ACAAffordable Care Act — the law behind the individual health-insurance marketplace and its income-based subsidies. It matters to a plan mostly before Medicare age: retiring early means buying coverage here, and the subsidy is tested against income you partly control.Full entry → premium tax creditThe ACA subsidy that pays part of a marketplace health premium, sized by income. It is the reason a dollar of extra income can cost far more than its tax: crossing the wrong line trims or ends the subsidy for the whole year.Full entry →, Medicaid, and Medicare's Extra Help all read it. Because it is a ratio, it can change from either end. Income can rise, or the household can get smaller, and a smaller household has a lower poverty line. For the marketplace, the federal poverty level the ACA reads also sets the ACA subsidy cliff, the income at which its help stops.

This lesson follows one family through a year in which the second change happens alone. Kim and Lee's income keeps pace with prices and no faster, yet in 2032 they cross the cliffA threshold where one extra dollar of income triggers a step cost — a surcharge tier, a lost subsidy — instead of a smooth increase. Cliffs are why plans care about landing just under a number, and why a small year-end decision can be worth far more than its size.Full entry → where the marketplace premiumWhat you pay for coverage — a monthly insurance or Medicare cost — and not a bonus or a prize. Confusingly, the same word means "above face value" when applied to a bond. In a plan it is nearly always the insurance sense: a recurring outflow that rises with age and, past a threshold, with income.Full entry → credit ends. The second plan shows one way they could have stayed under it.

%FPL is household income divided by the poverty guideline for the household's size, as a percentage. Each year the Department of Health and Human Services publishes the guidelines. For 2026 the line is $15,960 for one person and $5,680 more for each additional person, so a household of four has a line of $33,000 and a household of two a line of $21,640. An income of $99,000 is 300% of the line for four and 457% of the line for two.

The marketplace measures income in a particular way. It uses MAGIModified Adjusted Gross Income — AGI with certain deductions added back. It matters far out of proportion to the small adjustment, because MAGI is the figure that triggers cliffs: Medicare surcharges, the investment surtax, and health-subsidy eligibility all test against a MAGI, and each rule uses a slightly different one.Full entry →, with untaxed Social Security and tax-exempt interest added back, and it divides by the previous year's guideline, because enrollment for a year opens before that year's guidelines are published. The household is the people on the tax return: the filers and the dependents they claim.

Under the rules in force for 2026, the credit is the premium of a benchmark silver plan minus an expected contribution, which rises from 2.10% of income at the bottom of the scale to 9.96% from 300% of the line. Above 400% of the line there is no credit at all. The credit does not taper away near the line. A household one dollar over it loses the whole year's credit.

The first jump costs the family little. Between 300% and 400% the expected contribution is the same 9.96% of income, so a higher ratio inside that band does not raise what they pay. The credit is smaller in 2029 because the plan's premium for three people is lower than the premium for four, and the credit covers part of whatever the benchmark costs.

The only part of the ratio the family controls is the income at the top of it. A traditionalA retirement account where the tax is paid on the way out — not a description of an old-fashioned account. Money went in before income tax, grew untaxed, and is taxed as ordinary income when you take it out — traditional 401(k)s and IRAs work this way. The contrast is Roth (taxed going in, tax-free coming out) and taxable (brokerage, taxed as you go).Full entry → 401(k)An employer-sponsored retirement account, named after the section of tax law that created it — so the name says nothing about what it does. Contributions are usually payroll-deducted, often matched by the employer, and come in traditional (pre-tax) and Roth flavors. A 403(b) is the same idea for schools and nonprofits, a 457 for government workers.Full entry → deferral is taken out of pay before tax, so it lowers MAGI dollar for dollar. The second plan, Deferral raised in 2032, is the same family raising each deferral from $4,000 to $15,000 a year from 2032, which moves $22,000 more of their pay into the 401(k)s each year.

That brings 2032's MAGI down to about $88,000, about 360% of the line. From then until Medicare the ratio stays between about 355% and 375%, and the credit comes back at about $10,000 in 2032, rising to about $15,500 by 2042, or about $138,000 over those eleven years.

The extra saving is not free. The family takes home less pay, but the credit and the income tax saved on the deferral together cover more than half of the $22,000. The rest is still theirs, in the 401(k)s.

The first year has a delay. The plan sizes each year's advance credit on the previous year's income, and 2031's income, measured against a household of two, is over the line. So in 2032 the plan pays no advance, and the family pays the full premium. The return for 2032 reconciles the credit on the year's real income, and about $10,000 comes back as a refund in 2033. A real family can avoid that wait by telling the marketplace at enrollment what income it expects, including the higher deferral. The plan cannot be told that.

The margin is worth watching. The Cliff radar marks the ACA row as nearby in each of those years, because income sits within $25,000 of the line. The family is about $10,400 under it in 2033, and the gap narrows to about $8,200 by 2042. A bonus, a sale in a taxable accountAn ordinary taxable investment account — no special retirement wrapper, no contribution limit, and no withdrawal age rules. The trade-off is that dividends and realized gains are taxed as they happen rather than deferred. Often called the taxable bucket next to traditional and Roth.Full entry →, or a Roth conversionDeliberately moving money from a traditional account into a Roth one and paying the income tax on it now, so it grows and comes out tax-free later. The usual reason is a low-income year — between retiring and claiming benefits, say — where the conversion is taxed at a lower rate than the future withdrawal would be. It also shrinks later RMDs.Full entry → could still push a year over it.

The plan computes the credit from its own people, income and premium. Several parts of the real rules are not in it.

  • Students. The plan counts dependents until 19 and cannot extend that to 24 for a full-time student. Counting too few people only raises %FPL, so the error never makes a household look more eligible for help than it is.
  • The benchmark premium. Unless a phase gives a benchmark of its own, the plan treats its premium as the benchmark silver plan. The dollars are an estimate of the credit and not a quote.
  • Medicaid, cost-sharing reductions, and state subsidies. None is modeled, nor is an employer offer of coverage that would rule the credit out.
  • Who is in the tax household. The plan's household is its people. Two owners who do not file jointly would really be two households, and the plan cannot tell whether a second owner is a spouse.
  • The law after 2026. The 400% line returned for 2026 when the larger credits of 2021 to 2025 expired. Congress could change it again, and the plan follows the rules in force for 2026 in every later year.
