A glide pathA planned shift in allocation over time — classically toward bonds as retirement approaches, so risk comes down as the years available to recover from a loss come down with it. The word that matters is *planned*: a portfolio drifting toward whatever has been winning is not on a glide path, it is unrebalanced.Full entry → is a target mix that changes with time. The one this lesson studies is the bond tent. It holds the least stock at the retirement date, when a crash does the most damage, and adds stock back as retirement goes on. The lesson opens three plans for Morgan:

  • Bond tent aims for 40% stocks at retirement in 2028, then rises to 70% by 2043. The box Rebalance inside tax-sheltered accounts is ticked, so the IRA trades to follow the path.
  • Constant 60/40 holds one mix for the whole plan, also rebalanced inside the IRA.
  • Steering only has the same bond tent with the box unticked. The path is written down, but the IRA never trades to follow it.

A glide path is a plan for trades you have to make yourself. When the bond tent shows a better result in this lesson, that result assumes that every year Morgan logs into the IRA and sells or buys funds to match the target. Tutilis cannot make those trades. If the trades are not made, the real portfolio behaves like the Steering only plan, and that plan does worse than either of the others in every crash this lesson tests. A glide path is a schedule of target mixes, one for each year of the plan. Target-date funds use one. A 2030 fund holds mostly stock when its owners are young and moves toward bonds as 2030 approaches, and the fund's managers make the trades. Most glide paths only move in one direction, toward bonds, and keep going after the target date.

The years around the retirement date are when a fall in the market does the most damage. Before retirement a paycheck covers spending, so a crash is something to wait out. After it, every year's spending is sold from the portfolio, and shares sold near the bottom of a crash miss the recovery. This is sequence-of-returns riskThe order the good and bad years arrive in. Two plans with identical average returns can end in very different places if one takes its losses early while money is being withdrawn — selling into a fall removes shares that are then not there for the recovery. It is why retirement risk is not summarized by an average, and why the years either side of the day you stop working carry more weight than their count suggests.Full entry →, and the lesson Timing a crash measures it.

That risk is largest in the first years of retirement and fades after them. By the time Morgan is 80, a crash still hurts, but there are fewer years left to fund and Social Security covers more of each one. Wade Pfau and Michael Kitces asked what follows from that. If the danger peaks at the retirement date, the least stock should be held there, and stock can rise again afterward. The shape that results, falling toward retirement and rising after it, is what Kitces calls a bond tent: the share of bonds rises to a peak at retirement and falls away on both sides.

To set a path on your own plan, open AssumptionsA main-rail area. The rules the forecast runs under — growth, taxes, movements, success definition, locality, currency. Read and write., set the Money movement strategy to Glide rebalance, and tick Set a target mix. The first column starts at your plan's current mix. Use Add year for each point of the path, type the year and the four shares, and tick Rebalance inside tax-sheltered accounts if your path lowers stock over time.

Without the box, a glide path steers only money that moves anyway. Each year's leftover cash buys whatever is below its target, and each year's shortfall is sold from whatever is above it. That is enough to follow a path that adds stock. Stocks usually grow faster than bonds, and while stock sits below its target each year's spending is sold from bonds, so both push the mix the way the path is going. It is not enough to follow a path that takes stock away.

A trade inside an IRA is not a withdrawal. No money leaves the account, so there is no tax to pay and no early-withdrawal penalty, and the cash-flow statement leaves the swap out. That is why Tutilis rebalances only inside tax-sheltered accounts: traditional and RothA retirement account where you pay income tax on the money going in, and qualified withdrawals later come out tax-free — the mirror image of traditional. Named after the senator behind the law, so the word tells you nothing on its own. Plans care because Roth dollars do not add to taxable income later, which keeps them clear of income-driven cliffs.Full entry → IRAs and 401(k)s, 529 plans and HSAs. Selling in the brokerage account to rebalance would realize capital gains, so the plan never does it. An account can only swap between funds it already holds, so the path is reached as far as the sheltered balances allow. Morgan's IRA holds two-thirds of the money, which is plenty.

The Allocations switch also works on the Movements table, which then lists each class's value, share, target and gap for the selected year.

The bond tent has two sides, and the box makes both of them happen. On the way down, from 2026 to 2028, the IRA sells stock and buys Treasuries each year until the portfolio is 40% stock on Morgan's retirement date. On the way up, from 2028 to 2043, the target adds two points of stock a year, and the trades run in the other direction.

Bond tent, 2035. The year's spending is sold mostly from Treasuries, and a small swap inside the IRA buys stock.

By 2035 the target is about 54% stock. Most of the year's spending comes from the Treasury funds, which are the class above target, so the spending itself moves the mix toward more stock. The IRA makes up what is left with a small swap. On the rising side the path is easy to follow, because withdrawals do most of the work. The falling side is where the trades are large.

When markets are kind, more stock ends richer. On the expected path with no crash, which Tutilis draws as the GMF, the steering-only plan ends with about $1.55 million, the constant 60/40 plan with about $1.42 million, and the bond tent with about $1.34 million. Holding 40% stock for the first years of retirement gives up some growth, and the tent ends about 5% below the constant plan.

The trade is the same one as in Getting safer as retirement approaches. The tent gives up a little in the average future to do much better in the bad ones. Over 1,000 paths with no crash it still succeeds slightly more often than the constant plan, about 87% against 86%, because the bad paths count against success and the good ones do not.

Every result in this lesson for the bond tent and the constant plan assumes the trades in Moves TableA Forecast Detail chip. Mov = Movements. Holding-level trades for the selected year — sold or invested, after, %, and Why. really happen. In 2027 that means Morgan sells about $322,000 of the S&P 500 fund in the IRA and buys the Treasury fund with it. In a crash year it means buying stock while the news is at its worst. Tutilis models those trades. It cannot make them, and neither can your plan.

If the trades are not made, the real portfolio is the Steering only plan. The path is still in the plan, and the real portfolio does not follow it. As the crash table shows, that is worse than never having a path at all and holding 60/40, because the portfolio enters retirement near 76% stock and then sells stock at the bottom to pay for each year.

A few things make the trades more likely to happen:

  • Put a date on them. Rebalance once a year on a fixed date, and use the Allocations table for that year as the list of trades.
  • Trade inside the IRA. The trades cost no tax there, which is why the plan makes them there. Selling in a brokerage account to rebalance can realize capital gains that the forecast does not assume.
  • Let a fund do it. A target-date or balanced fund rebalances on its own. A target-date fund's path usually keeps falling after the target date, so it is not a bond tent.
  • Check that the box matches your behavior. If you know you will not rebalance, untick the box, and the forecast will show you the steering-only result instead of a result you will not get.

Wade Pfau and Michael Kitces tested glide paths in "Reducing Retirement Risk with a Rising Equity Glide Path" (2014). They simulated retirements with paths that started with little stock and added more each year, and compared them with constant mixes and with paths that kept falling. Paths that started at about 30% stock and rose to about 60% did better than a constant 60%. They failed less often, and when they failed they failed by less. The falling paths that many advisors used did worse than both.

Their explanation is the one this lesson shows. The worst outcomes come from a crash early in retirement, so the least stock should be held then. Later, when a crash matters less, rising stock adds growth. Kitces later named the whole shape, from the years before retirement through the rise after it, the bond tent.

Morgan's tent is a gentler version of theirs, 40% rising to 70%, and the crash table above shows the same pattern on Morgan's plan. The research makes the same assumption as the forecast: the portfolio is rebalanced to the path every year.
