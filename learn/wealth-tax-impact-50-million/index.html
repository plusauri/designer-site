An income tax is charged on what you earn in a year. A wealth tax is charged on what you own. Each year the household adds up the value of everything it holds, subtracts its debts, subtracts an exemption, and pays a percentage of what is left. A 2% wealth tax on $100 million above the exemption is $2 million, whether the portfolio rose 20% that year or fell 20%.

Most Americans already pay one wealth tax without calling it that. A property tax is a yearly percentage of what a house is worth, and it is due whether or not the house earned anything. Property tax, the silent killer shows what that does to a household over a lifetime. A net worth tax applies the same idea to stocks, bonds, private businesses and everything else.

The difference that matters most for a plan is what the tax is measured against. Because a wealth tax takes a share of the whole pile every year, it is best understood as a share of the pile's return. A portfolio that earns 6% a year and pays 2% of its value in wealth tax gives up a third of its return before any income tax is charged. That is why a rate that sounds small can change a plan a great deal.

The math — a wealth tax as a tax on the return

Let W be the taxable wealth, r the return it earns in a year and τ the wealth tax rate. The wealth tax is τW. The return is rW. As a share of the return, the wealth tax is

τW ÷ rW = τ ÷ r

At τ = 2% and r = 6%, that is one third, an income tax of about 33% on the year's return. At r = 4% it is one half. In a year when the return is zero or negative, the share is undefined, because the tax is still due and there is nothing to take it from but the principalThe amount itself — the money lent, borrowed, or invested, as against the interest or growth on top of it. On a mortgage payment it is the part that actually reduces the debt; early on, most of the payment is not principal. (Not *principle*, and not a person.)Full entry →.

An exemption changes the arithmetic only for the part of the wealth below the line. For Nadia and Graham, the first $50 million is untaxed, so in 2027 their bill is about 1.2% of their whole net worth, which is still about a fifth of a 6% return. It is charged on top of the income tax already due on the same return.

There is no federal wealth tax in the United States today, and none of the bills below has passed a committee. These are the proposals a household like Nadia and Graham's hears about most often.

Warren's bill taxes "all property, real or personal, tangible or intangible, wherever situated," less debts. It does not exclude a home or a retirement account. The only carve-out is for personal belongings worth $50,000 or less each. It also includes a 40% exit tax on the net worth of anyone who gives up citizenship to avoid it. Its two thresholds are fixed dollar amounts written into the bill, a detail this lesson returns to in A line that does not move.

California's Proposition 40 is a one-time tax. It measures net worth on December 31, 2026, applies to anyone who was a resident on January 1, 2026, and can be paid over five years with an interest charge. Unlike Warren's bill, it leaves out real estate held directly, retirement accounts, and up to $5 million of other assets. California's Legislative Analyst's Office expects it to raise tens of billions of dollars over several years, and also expects the state to lose some income tax every year afterward from billionaires who move away.

Other states have debated wealth taxes without passing one. In January 2023, legislators in eight states introduced coordinated bills to tax the very rich, and several of them were wealth taxes, including California's AB 259 at 1% above $50 million. None passed. Washington considered two wealth tax bills in 2025 and passed neither. It taxes capital gains above a threshold, a tax its Supreme Court upheld in 2023 as a tax on the sale of assets rather than on the assets themselves.

Reference — taxes on the rich that are not wealth taxes

Several proposals and laws that are often mentioned alongside wealth taxes are taxes on income instead.

  • The Billionaire Minimum Income Tax appeared in the Biden administration's budgets from 2023 to 2025. It would have charged households worth more than $100 million at least 25% of their income, counting gains on assets they had not sold. Treasury scored the 2025 version at about $503 billion over ten years. It was never enacted.
  • The Billionaires Income Tax (Wyden, 2021 and 2023) would have taxed the yearly gain on billionaires' assets as if they had been sold. The Joint Committee on Taxation put it at $557 billion over ten years.
  • Massachusetts' millionaires' surtax adds 4% to income above about $1.08 million (2025). It raised about $3 billion in fiscal 2025.
  • Washington's 2026 income tax charges 9.9% on household income above $1 million starting in 2028. It is being challenged in court, and a repeal initiative is on the November 2026 ballot.

The federal government has never taxed net worth. The Constitution is the main reason. It requires a "direct" tax to be divided among the states by population, which a tax on wealth could never do fairly, because wealth is not spread among the states in proportion to people. In 1895, in Pollock v. Farmers' Loan & Trust Co., the Supreme Court held that a tax on income from property was a direct tax. The Sixteenth Amendment, ratified in 1913, let Congress tax incomes without dividing them among the states. It says nothing about wealth.

Whether a federal wealth tax would survive in court is still an open question. In Moore v. United States (2024), the Court upheld a one-time tax on American shareholders of foreign companies, but it took care to say what it was not deciding. Its opinion states that it does not address "taxes on holdings, wealth, or net worth" or "taxes on appreciation." Two justices who dissented would have held that the Sixteenth Amendment only allows a tax on income that has been realizedA gain that has been locked in by selling — and, in a taxable account, the moment it becomes taxable. Until then it is unrealized: the holding is worth more than you paid, but nothing has happened yet and no tax is due. The same word cuts the other way: a loss is only realized once you sell, which is why a downturn you ride through costs nothing you did not sell.Full entry →, for example by a sale. The question would be decided only if a wealth tax passed and someone challenged it.

States and cities face no such limit, and they tax wealth every day through property taxes. Some states have also taxed financial assets.

Did you know?

Florida, where Nadia and Graham live and which has no income tax, used to tax stocks, bonds and other financial assets every year. Its intangible personal property tax charged 50 cents per $1,000 of value above an exemption of $250,000 a person. The legislature repealed it in 2006, effective January 1, 2007.

Wealth taxes were once common in Europe and have mostly been repealed. According to the OECD, 12 of its member countries taxed net wealth in 1990, and only four did in 2017: France, Norway, Spain and Switzerland. Austria, Denmark, Germany, the Netherlands, Finland, Iceland, Luxembourg and Sweden all dropped theirs, and France replaced its general wealth tax with a tax on real estate alone in 2018. The OECD found that most of these taxes raised little money. The countries that repealed them pointed to their economic and administrative costs and to their failure to reduce inequality as much as intended.

CountryWhat happened
SwitzerlandHas taxed wealth for more than a century, through its cantons, with low exemptions that reach the middle class. It raises about 1% of GDP, the most in the OECD, but rates are low, mostly under 1%.
NorwayRaised its top rate to 1.1% in 2022. Dozens of wealthy residents moved, mostly to Switzerland. Whether the change gained or lost revenue overall is still argued over.
SpainHas a regional wealth tax, plus a national "solidarity" tax on fortunes above €3 million added in 2022. Its Constitutional Court upheld the national tax in 2023.
FranceReplaced its general wealth tax with a real estate wealth tax in 2018. A proposal for a 2% minimum tax on fortunes above €100 million, the "Zucman tax," failed in 2025.
GermanyThe Constitutional Court ruled in 1995 that valuing different assets in different ways was unconstitutional. The tax has not been collected since 1997.

Even where wealth taxes survive, they raise a small share of revenue. In 2016 they brought in between 0.2% of GDP (Spain) and 1.0% (Switzerland), and between 0.5% and 3.7% of all tax revenue. Supporters answer that a tax with a high exemption and few loopholes, applied to a whole economy as large as the US, would perform better than Europe's taxes did. In 2024, a report commissioned by Brazil's G20 presidency proposed a coordinated 2% minimum tax on the world's roughly 3,000 billionaires, on the grounds that coordination would leave them nowhere to go.

Did you know?

Germany's wealth tax was never repealed. In 1995 the Constitutional Court ruled that taxing real estate at old, low assessed values while taxing stocks and bonds at market value was unequal treatment, and it gave the legislature until the end of 1996 to fix it. The legislature never did, so the tax has simply not been collected since 1997.

How much a federal wealth tax would raise depends on two hard questions. The first is how well the government could value assets that do not trade, such as private companies, art and real estate. The second is how much the wealthy would do to avoid it. The estimates for Warren's plan differ mostly on the second.

Lawrence Summers and Natasha Sarin argued in 2019 that the Saez and Zucman estimate was far too high. They pointed to the estate tax, where appraisals, discounts and trusts shrink what is reported, and suggested a wealth tax might raise as little as 40% of the projection. Saez and Zucman answered that a well-designed tax could close those loopholes. The Congressional Budget Office has not scored any of the net worth tax bills.

Research on Europe's taxes measures how strongly the wealthy respond:

  • Sweden. Reported wealth responded only modestly to the tax, and the response came from avoidance and evasion rather than from saving less (Seim, 2017).
  • Denmark. The response was clear, and it was larger for the very wealthy (Jakobsen, Jakobsen, Kleven and Zucman, 2020).
  • Switzerland. A one-point cut in the wealth tax rate raised reported wealth by at least 43% after six years. About a quarter of that came from people moving between cantons (Brülhart and others, 2022).
  • Scandinavia. A one-point rise in the top rate reduced the number of wealthy taxpayers by about 2%, as some moved abroad, with small effects on the wider economy (Jakobsen, Kleven, Kolsrud, Landais and Muñoz, 2026 revision).

None of these studies says what a household should do. They do say that the revenue a wealth tax raises depends on design details, which are the same details that decide what it would cost any one household.

The wealth tax what-if is a stress test, and it is not a forecast of what Congress will pass. A real law would differ from it in several ways.

  • One bracket. The editor charges one rate above one exemption. Warren's 3% bracket above $1 billion, and any phase-in, are not modeled. On Nadia and Graham's best paths, the ones that end above $1 billion, the real bill would be larger than the picture shows.
  • Valuation. The plan values every holding at its modeled market value. A real tax would rely on appraisals, and a private business or a piece of art can be valued in more than one way. That is the single biggest practical problem the studies describe.
  • Behavior. The plan does not move, give money away early, change how it invests or renounce citizenship in response to the tax. Real households do some of these things, which is why the revenue estimates differ so much.
  • Liquidity. The plan pays the tax by selling holdings. A household whose wealth is mostly a private company or real estate might have to borrow or sell at a bad time. Warren's bill allows illiquid taxpayers to defer payment over five years, which the what-if does not model.
  • The courts. A federal wealth tax that passed would face a constitutional challenge, and the plan assumes it survives.

What the lesson does claim is narrower and more useful. A wealth tax is charged on what you own, so it is best read as a share of your return, and its cost compounds. For a household far above the line, the question it raises is how much is left rather than whether the money lasts. For a household below the line, the most important detail in any proposal is whether the line rises with inflation. Transforms can price any combination of these against a real plan in a few seconds.
