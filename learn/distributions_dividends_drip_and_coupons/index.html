Alex holds $200,000 in a dividend fund. The fund yields 3%, so it distributes about $6,000 in the first year and more as the fund grows. Alex has two choices, and most brokerages present them as a single checkbox: take the dividend as cash, or reinvest it.

The checkbox looks like it is about convenience. It is not. It changes what shows up on the cash flow statement, what the reconciliation has to account for, and — in the case of a bond — whether the household ends the year better off at all.

Desktop app only

The checkbox is the holding's Distributions choice, under fund or stock yields.

The Dividend Fund on Dividend as cash. Distributions is Spendable, so the dividend arrives as cash the household can spend.

The same fund on Dividend reinvested. Distributions is Reinvested, so the dividend buys more of the fund and never becomes cash.

With Dividend as cash, the dividend arrives. It shows up in the year's income beside the salary, and the household can spend it, save it, or let the plan reinvest it as ordinary surplus.

What is easy to miss is where the money came from. A fund's published return is a total return — the price appreciation and the yield together. When the fund pays a dividend, the share price drops by what was paid. The fund handed over value it was already holding on Alex's behalf. Nothing was created.

That is why a plan built on chasing yield is not a plan. A 6% yielding fund and a 3% yielding fund with the same total return leave the household in exactly the same place; the first one just hands back more of itself each year.

For the math lovers — why yield is a split of the return

Write the share price at the start of the year as \(P_0\), at the end as \(P_1\), and the dividend per share as \(D\). The total return is

\[ R = \frac{P_1 + D - P_0}{P_0} = \frac{P_1 - P_0}{P_0} + \frac{D}{P_0} \]

The first term is the price return and the second is the yield. The price falls by \(D\) when the dividend is paid, so for the same \(R\), every point of extra yield is a point less of price return.

Switch to Dividend reinvested. The salary is unchanged. Living expenses are unchanged. But the year's income falls by the whole dividend, because no dividend arrived.

Look at the reconciliation — the panel that proves the year's money adds up. Set the forecaster to 2030, which is the year every table below is read from. It grows a new source:

The dividend and the shares it bought are the same $7,326, entered on both sides. It is a source because something had to pay for those shares. It is not income — it never reached the household, so the cash flow statement is right not to list it. The reconciliation is where the two facts are allowed to coexist.

And notice the third line. Alex's spending did not change, but the salary alone no longer covers it, so the plan sells $8,729 of the fund to make up the difference — after buying $7,326 of the same fund with the dividend. Reinvesting a distribution you were going to need anyway is a round trip with a tax bill attached.

This is the single most useful thing to know about reinvestment, and it is invisible on a brokerage statement that only shows the share count going up.

Now switch to Coupon reinvested. Alex holds a $200,000 corporate bond at a 5% coupon instead of the fund, and the coupon is reinvested. The reconciliation for 2030 reads:

SideSegmentAmount
sourceReinvested Distributions$10,000
useInvestments$10,000

The shape is the same as the dividend, and the meaning is completely different. A bond coupon is paid by the issuer, out of the issuer's pocket, on top of the principalThe amount itself — the money lent, borrowed, or invested, as against the interest or growth on top of it. On a mortgage payment it is the part that actually reduces the debt; early on, most of the payment is not principal. (Not *principle*, and not a person.)Full entry →. The bond's price is not reduced by it, the way a fund's share price is. So when the coupon is reinvested, the position grows by $10,000 that did not previously exist anywhere in Alex's plan. That is real new net worth.

For a dividend, "reinvest" moves money from one pocket to the same pocket. For a coupon, "reinvest" is the only combination in the whole grid where the reinvestment itself adds something.

  • A dividend is not free money. The fund's price falls by what it pays. Yield is a split of the total return, not an addition to it.
  • Reinvesting does not make it disappear. In a taxable account you owe tax on a distribution you never received.
  • A bond coupon is the exception, and it is the exception because of who pays it. The issuer pays; the position is untouched; reinvesting it genuinely compounds.
  • If you are going to need the cash anyway, reinvesting it just means selling something else later.
