Will Social Security be there? Plan for the shortfall, not for zero

Maya is 58 and Theo is 56. Both plan to stop working in 2033, when Maya turns 65 and Theo turns 63, and both plan to claim Social Security at their full retirement ageWhen you start taking Social Security. Claim before full retirement age and the monthly check is permanently smaller; wait past it and it is permanently larger. It is one of the few decisions in a plan that is both free to change beforehand and impossible to change after.Full entry → of 67, Maya in 2035 and Theo in 2037. Their Social Security statements put Maya's benefit at $2,900 a month and Theo's at $2,100 in today's dollars. Together that is about $60,000 a year, and it is the largest single source of income they will have in retirement. They have $1.2 million saved and plan to spend $102,000 a year.

A friend has told them to leave Social Security out of the plan entirely, because "it won't be there." The friend is not alone. In a 2023 Nationwide survey, 45% of Gen Z adults and 39% of millennials said they expect to get none of the Social Security benefits they have earned.

The worry has a real source. The Social Security trust fund is projected to run out in the 2030s, and every year the Trustees publish the date. What the headlines usually leave out is what happens on that date, and it is not what Maya and Theo's friend thinks. This lesson looks at the official numbers, lays the projected cut over Maya and Theo's plan with a what-if in TransformsA main-rail area. The name is Transforms because you transform a copy of the plan — a what-if — without rewriting the original dossier. Compare original vs modified side by side., and compares the cost of planning for that cut with the cost of planning for nothing.

It opens two plans:

  • As scheduled is Maya and Theo's plan as it stands. It counts on every scheduled dollar of both benefits.
  • Ready for a shortfall is the same couple spending $96,000 a year instead of $102,000. Nothing else changes.

Social Security is mostly pay-as-you-go. The payroll tax that workers and employers pay today, the 12.4% FICAFederal Insurance Contributions Act — the payroll tax withheld from a paycheck for Social Security and Medicare. An employee pays half and the employer pays the other half. It is separate from income tax, which is why a plan can show income tax and payroll tax as different lines. Someone who works for themselves pays both halves instead (SECA).Full entry → Social Security tax on wages up to the annual cap, pays today's benefits. The trust funds hold the surplus that built up when payroll taxes brought in more than benefits cost, most of it after Congress raised taxes and trimmed benefits in 1983.

There are two funds. The Old-Age and Survivors Insurance fund pays retirement and survivor benefits, and the Disability Insurance fund pays disability benefits. Together they are called OASDIOld-Age, Survivors, and Disability Insurance — Social Security's formal name, and the label on the payroll-tax line of a paycheck. Seeing OASDI and Social Security in one plan is not two things; it is the tax and the benefit it funds.Full entry →. The reserve is now being drawn down because benefits cost more than the program takes in. When it reaches zero, benefits can only be paid from the taxes that keep arriving.

That last sentence is the whole lesson in miniature. A depleted trust fund is an empty reserve. The payroll tax on every paycheck in the country does not stop when the reserve does.

The Social Security Board of Trustees publishes a report every year. The 2026 report, released on June 9, 2026, projects:

FundReserves run outShare of scheduled benefits payable thenShare payable in 2100
Old-Age and Survivors Insurance (retirement)Late 203278%62%
OASI and DI combinedMid 203483%65%

The Congressional Budget Office makes its own projection, and it is somewhat darker. CBO's 2026 long-term projections have the retirement fund exhausted in fiscal year 2032 and the combined funds in fiscal year 2033, with a benefit reduction of 23% in 2034 that grows to 37% by 2100.

The two retirement-fund dates are closer than they look. The Trustees count calendar years and CBO counts fiscal years, which end in September, so the Trustees' "fourth quarter of 2032" falls in CBO's fiscal 2033.

The combined figure is the one most often quoted, but it assumes Congress lets the retirement fund borrow from the disability fund. Congress has shifted money between the two funds before, in 1994 and again in 2015, so the assumption is reasonable, and it is still an assumption.

A single depletion year sounds more certain than it is. It depends on how many people work, what they earn, how long retirees live, how many children are born and how fast prices rise. The Trustees test that uncertainty by running 5,000 simulations, each with its own path for those assumptions, and reporting how the depletion year spreads:

Probability the combined reserves are goneBy
50%2034
80% range2033 to 2037
95% range2032 to 2039

In other words, if Congress changes nothing, a shortfall arriving sometime in the 2030s is close to certain. What is uncertain is the year, and how large the cut would be. A plan cannot know either, which is exactly the kind of question a what-if is built for.

Reference — why the date moves from year to year

The 2026 report widened the 75-year shortfall from 3.82% of taxable payroll to 4.42%, mostly because the Trustees lowered their long-run fertility assumption from 1.90 to 1.75 children per woman. Fewer future workers means less future payroll tax.

Two recent laws also moved the numbers a little. The Social Security Fairness Act of January 2025 repealed the Windfall Elimination Provision and the Government Pension Offset, which raised benefits for some public-sector retirees and cost the program 0.14% of taxable payroll over 75 years. The 2025 reconciliation law added a deduction for older taxpayers, so less income tax is collected on benefits and less of that tax flows back to the trust funds. The Chief Actuary estimated its effect at 0.16% of payroll. The 2026 report still puts combined depletion in the third quarter of 2034, because faster near-term wage growth offset the loss.

The law answers this directly. The Antideficiency Act forbids a federal agency from paying out more than the law authorizes, and for Social Security the limit is what the trust fund holds. Once the reserve is empty, the program can pay only what incoming payroll taxes and the tax on benefits bring in. The Congressional Research Service describes two ways that could work: full checks paid late, or reduced checks paid on time. The Trustees' "83% payable" measures how much of each check the incoming money would cover.

History points the same way. The one time a trust fund actually ran dry, every check was still paid.

Did you know?

On November 5, 1982, Social Security's retirement fund ran out of money. The Treasury paid that month's checks with a $581 million loan from the disability fund. A national commission chaired by Alan Greenspan was already at work, and in April 1983 Congress raised the full retirement age to 67 in steps, taxed up to half of benefits for higher-income retirees and moved scheduled payroll tax increases earlier. Nobody's check stopped.

Congress can close the gap in the other direction too. The Social Security actuaries estimate how much of the 75-year shortfall each common proposal would close. Their latest estimates are measured against the 2025 projections, when the shortfall was a little smaller than it is now. Removing the cap on taxable wages without raising benefits closes about two thirds of it, which is the what-if Erin On The Wage Cap models. Raising the payroll tax from 12.4% to 16.4% closes all of it. A lower cost-of-living formula closes about a sixth, and raising the full retirement age to 69 closes about a third. Most packages combine several of these, and some of them would change benefits for today's workers as well.

A plan cannot know which of these Congress will choose. What it can do is ask whether it still works under current law if Congress does nothing, which is the cut.

The editor holds one share for every year after the cut starts. The official projections instead have the payable share sliding slowly downward over the century. A share of 65% from 2034 is a harsher test than any single year of the Trustees' path until about 2100, so it works as a floor for the later decades.

Now try the friend's advice. Set Share of benefit paid to 0% and start it in 2027:

The chance of success falls to about 28%. On the expected path, Maya and Theo run out of money in 2054, when Maya is 86.

To make a plan with no Social Security as safe as their plan with it, Maya and Theo would have to cut their spending from $102,000 a year to about $65,000. That is about $37,000 a year, or more than a third of everything they spend, for the rest of their lives, to insure against a check that stops completely.

That is the cost of "count on nothing." It sounds cautious, but it gives up a third of a retirement to prepare for an outcome that current law does not produce.

Now switch to Ready for a shortfall. This plan spends $96,000 a year, $6,000 less than the plan as scheduled. Lay the same cut over it, 83% from 2034:

With the cut, this plan succeeds about 91% of the time. That matches Maya and Theo's original plan with Social Security paid in full. A 6% trim in spending buys back everything the Trustees' cut takes away. If Congress fixes the program and the full benefit arrives, the trimmed plan simply succeeds about 98% of the time and leaves more behind.

Spending less is not the only lever. Working another year or two, or delaying the claim past 67, moves the plan in the same direction. Claim early vs live long shows what claim timing does to a household, and a shortfall is a good reason to run those what-ifs together. The approach does not change: pick the cut you want to be ready for, lay it over the plan, and adjust until the Modified side is a plan you would accept.

The shortfall what-if is a stress test, and it is not a forecast of what Congress will do. Every solution Congress has passed before has changed the program in its own way. A future law might protect current retirees and change benefits for younger workers, raise the full retirement age, change the cost-of-living formula, raise taxes, or combine all four. None of those looks like a flat cut applied to everyone, and any of them could affect Maya and Theo differently than the what-if does.

The what-if also applies one share from one year onward. The Trustees' projections have the payable share falling gradually, and CBO's fall faster, which is why the table above tests a lower share too.

What the lesson does claim is narrower and more useful. Under current law, the trust fund running out means a smaller check. A plan can be tested against that smaller check today, and being ready for it usually costs far less than giving the benefit up entirely.
