Required minimum distributions (RMDs) are not a residual preference — they are a statutory force. Once the owner is past RMDRequired Minimum Distribution — once you reach an age set by statute, the law makes you withdraw a minimum amount each year from traditional retirement accounts, whether or not you need the money. The withdrawal is taxable income, so RMDs can push a household into a higher bracket or over a surcharge threshold. Roth accounts are generally not subject to them.Full entry → age and holds a traditionalA retirement account where the tax is paid on the way out — not a description of an old-fashioned account. Money went in before income tax, grew untaxed, and is taxed as ordinary income when you take it out — traditional 401(k)s and IRAs work this way. The contrast is Roth (taxed going in, tax-free coming out) and taxable (brokerage, taxed as you go).Full entry → (pre-taxMoney that went in before income tax was taken — the traditional bucket. Not the same as untaxed: the tax was deferred, not forgiven, and it comes due as ordinary income on the way out. A pre-tax balance is worth less to spend than the same number in a Roth.Full entry →) retirement account, the plan must sell enough from that account each year (or pay a penalty model) whether or not your residual strategy would rather spend taxable cash first.

The other half: what the forced dollars cost you

This lesson shows that RMDs happen regardless of strategy. It does not show what they do to your tax bracketA band of income taxed at a given rate. The common misreading is that entering a higher bracket taxes all your income at that rate — it does not. Only the dollars inside that band pay the higher rate, so a raise never leaves you with less after tax.Full entry → — and that is where the money is. Forced distributions consume cheap-bracket room that a Roth conversionDeliberately moving money from a traditional account into a Roth one and paying the income tax on it now, so it grows and comes out tax-free later. The usual reason is a low-income year — between retiring and claiming benefits, say — where the conversion is taxed at a lower rate than the future withdrawal would be. It also shrinks later RMDs.Full entry → could otherwise have used, and they consume it first.

Did you know?

In a year an RMD is due, the first dollars out of a traditional IRA count toward that RMD, and RMD dollars cannot be converted to a Roth. Only money taken beyond the required amount can be converted. That rule is why the forced dollars always claim the cheap brackets first.

Filling the cheap buckets draws that competition directly: one stacked column per year, forced RMD below discretionary conversion, with the unfilled bracket room hatched on top. It is also where the statutory required amount is separated from the taxable band — the two diverge once base income stops covering the standard deductionThe flat amount most households subtract instead of itemizing individual deductions. You take the larger of the two, so it acts as a floor: below it, an extra deductible expense saves nothing at all, which surprises people who gave to charity expecting a break.Full entry →, and the gap is the deductionAn amount subtracted from income before the tax is computed, so it saves tax at your marginal rate rather than dollar for dollar. That is the difference from a credit, which comes off the tax itself: a $1,000 deduction is worth a few hundred dollars, a $1,000 credit is worth $1,000.Full entry → sheltering income the rules compelled.
