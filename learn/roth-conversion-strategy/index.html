A Roth conversionDeliberately moving money from a traditional account into a Roth one and paying the income tax on it now, so it grows and comes out tax-free later. The usual reason is a low-income year — between retiring and claiming benefits, say — where the conversion is taxed at a lower rate than the future withdrawal would be. It also shrinks later RMDs.Full entry → moves money from a traditionalA retirement account where the tax is paid on the way out — not a description of an old-fashioned account. Money went in before income tax, grew untaxed, and is taxed as ordinary income when you take it out — traditional 401(k)s and IRAs work this way. The contrast is Roth (taxed going in, tax-free coming out) and taxable (brokerage, taxed as you go).Full entry → (pre-taxMoney that went in before income tax was taken — the traditional bucket. Not the same as untaxed: the tax was deferred, not forgiven, and it comes due as ordinary income on the way out. A pre-tax balance is worth less to spend than the same number in a Roth.Full entry →) retirement account into a RothA retirement account where you pay income tax on the money going in, and qualified withdrawals later come out tax-free — the mirror image of traditional. Named after the senator behind the law, so the word tells you nothing on its own. Plans care because Roth dollars do not add to taxable income later, which keeps them clear of income-driven cliffs.Full entry → (after-taxMoney that has already had income tax taken out of it. In an account it means you own that basis outright and will not be taxed on it again — only on the growth, and how *that* is taxed depends on the wrapper it grew in.Full entry →) account. You pay ordinary incomeIncome taxed at the regular graduated rates — wages, interest, pension payments, and withdrawals from traditional retirement accounts. The point of the label is the contrast: long-term capital gains and qualified dividends get their own, usually lower, rate schedule.Full entry → tax on the amount converted now, so future growth and qualifiedA tax word meaning the conditions were met, not a judgment of quality. A qualified Roth withdrawal is tax-free; an unqualified one is not. Qualified dividends get the lower capital-gain rates; ordinary ones do not. Whenever you see it, the interesting question is *which* conditions.Full entry → withdrawals can come out federally tax-freeNo income tax due when the money comes out — the Roth promise, and the reason Roth dollars keep a withdrawal clear of income-driven thresholds. The word hides a condition: the withdrawal has to be qualified, which turns on age and how long the account has been open. It also hides a scope: municipal-bond interest is tax-free on the federal return yet still counts toward whether Social Security is taxed.Full entry →.

That trade is most attractive in low-income years — after paychecks stop and before required minimum distributions (RMDs) force large traditional withdrawals. Planners call that span the conversion window. This lesson shows how the plan states a conversion policy, how the forecast executes it without treating it like ordinary spending, and how the conversion shows up on tax, MAGIModified Adjusted Gross Income — AGI with certain deductions added back. It matters far out of proportion to the small adjustment, because MAGI is the figure that triggers cliffs: Medicare surcharges, the investment surtax, and health-subsidy eligibility all test against a MAGI, and each rule uses a slightly different one.Full entry →, and Movements

For the next ten years Alex has no paycheck, no Social Security, and no required withdrawals, so the 12% bracket is almost empty. That changes later. From 2036 Social Security arrives, and from 2041 the required minimum distributions come out of a traditional IRA that will have grown past $2 million if nothing is converted. Together they push Alex into the 22% bracket, and late in life into 24%. A conversion moves traditional dollars out while they can still be taxed at 12%, so fewer of them are left to be taxed at 22% or 24% later.

The plan shows the result directly. The same household with the policy turned off is its own sample (roth_conversion_off_sample), and the two can be compared in the Simulator.

The two plans end with about the same net worth, but they do not leave the same amount to spend. The plan without conversions still holds about $1 million more in its traditional IRA, and every dollar of it will be taxed as ordinary income when Alex or an heir takes it out. Net worth counts those dollars at full value, which is why it hides the difference. The lower tax bill is the benefit, and it is real money.

A conversion is not always worth it. If Alex's later income would land in the same bracket as the conversion, prepaying the tax gains nothing. The lesson's household was chosen because the gap is large: 12% now against 22% to 24% later.

Did you know?

Converting a traditional IRA to a Roth before age 59½ does not trigger the 10% early-withdrawal penalty. The IRS treats a conversion as a move between two of your own accounts, not a withdrawal you spend. The penalty can come back only if you are under 59½ and take the converted dollars out of the Roth within five years of converting them.

Filling a bracket is the way a planner usually sizes a conversion, because the bracket edge is the line that matters. A fixed amount is easier to read in a table but can spill into the next bracket in a year when other income is higher.

4. Look ahead to IRMAA (and the Cliffs strip)

IRMAA is a Medicare premium surchargeAn extra amount added on top of a normal charge once income passes a line — in planning, almost always the Medicare premium surcharge. It is not a tax rate, so it does not show up in a bracket; it arrives as a higher premium, in tiers, based on a tax return from two years earlier.Full entry → driven by MAGI from two years earlier — a healthcare cash outflow, not a federal tax-statement line. The honest version is simple: a conversion that lifts MAGI in year t can raise premiums in year t+2 once someone is on Medicare (planning anchor: age 65). Example: convert at 63 → IRMAA risk at 65.

On this sample the surcharge appears, and then it shrinks. Alex turns 65 in 2031. The conversions of 2029 to 2032 lift MAGI over the first IRMAA threshold, so the cash-flow table shows an IRMAA surcharge in 2031 to 2034. Over the whole plan, though, conversions lower the IRMAA Alex pays. Without them, the larger RMDs keep MAGI high for the rest of Alex's life, and the plan pays about twice as much in surcharges. Paying a small surcharge for four years buys a smaller one for twenty.

There's no free lunch. TNFL

A conversion moves tax into years you choose, but the converted dollars are income in those years. They raise MAGI, and a cliff the plan crosses can charge more than the tax the conversion was meant to save.

You already met forced RMDRequired Minimum Distribution — once you reach an age set by statute, the law makes you withdraw a minimum amount each year from traditional retirement accounts, whether or not you need the money. The withdrawal is taxable income, so RMDs can push a household into a higher bracket or over a surcharge threshold. Roth accounts are generally not subject to them.Full entry → with the RMD lesson.
