Compound growth is the slow bend that becomes a steep one. A few percentage points of mean return, held for decades, change the shape of a plan more than almost any one-year surprise.

Suppose you invest a lump of money and leave it alone. Each year the balance (not just the original principalThe amount itself — the money lent, borrowed, or invested, as against the interest or growth on top of it. On a mortgage payment it is the part that actually reduces the debt; early on, most of the payment is not principal. (Not *principle*, and not a person.)Full entry →) grows by roughly the same rate. That is compoundingGrowth on top of previous growth — this year's return is earned on last year's gains as well as the original money. It is why a plan curve bends upward instead of climbing in a straight line, and why the early years matter more than their size suggests.Full entry →: last year’s growth becomes part of this year’s base.

On a chart of dollars vs years, low growth looks almost linear for a long time, then quietly curves. Raise the rate and the curve does not merely get “a bit taller early on” — over a long horizon the gap between paths explodes. That is why people call compounding the “eighth wonder” in finance rhetoric: small rate differences, long time, large dollar difference.

Did you know?

Over forty years, a dollar that grows at 3% a year becomes about $3.26. At 7% the same dollar becomes about $15. The rate is a little more than twice as high, and the money at the end is more than four times as much.

Honesty: markets bounce. The smooth curve is the on-trend / typical path (what the product’s growth math often shows as a geometric-mean line). Real paths zigzag around it. The lesson here is the shape of compounding, not a promise of a smooth ride.

Try this — the whole point of the lesson

  1. Set a simple yearly amount (e.g. $1,000) and a multi-decade year range.
  2. Run at about 3% mean growth. Note the end figure.
  3. Change only the rate to about 6–7%. Watch how much larger the end is — read the number, not the curve. It is not a linear “double the rate, double the dollars” story over long horizons; compounding is nonlinear.
  4. Optionally try 0% (flat face value) vs a mild positive rate so the curve shape is obvious.

For the math lovers — the compounding formula and the rule of 72

A lump \(P\) growing at rate \(r\) for \(n\) years becomes

\[ FV = P\,(1+r)^n \]

The exponent is why the gap explodes. At \(n = 40\), \(1.03^{40} \approx 3.26\) while \(1.07^{40} \approx 14.97\).

The rule of 72. Money doubles when \((1+r)^n = 2\), so \(n = \ln 2 / \ln(1+r)\). For small rates \(\ln(1+r) \approx r\), which gives a doubling time of roughly \(72 / (\text{rate in \%})\) years. That is about 24 years at 3% and about 10 at 7%, so over forty years the 3% dollar doubles less than twice and the 7% dollar doubles almost four times.

Where it is in the app compounding

Where it shows in the forecast

Other planning tools often show all scenario paths as lines from the start year through to the final year — a full fan of journeys over time. That is a typical standard view: every year of every path, drawn end to end. It is honest about sequence of returnsThe order the good and bad years arrive in. Two plans with identical average returns can end in very different places if one takes its losses early while money is being withdrawn — selling into a fall removes shares that are then not there for the recovery. It is why retirement risk is not summarized by an average, and why the years either side of the day you stop working carry more weight than their count suggests.Full entry →, but easy to drown in ink.

This is that same idea, but only showing where net worth ends up on that last year. The product calls it a Net Worth Distribution — a distribution diagram (or histogram):

  • Instead of drawing every path year by year, it keeps only the ending value of each path on the last year.
  • Those endings form a cloud / histogram: how many paths land poor, typical, or rich at the horizon.
  • Quartiles, GMF ✦GMF ✦ (also FID 0 / GMF) is the product north star — the deterministic geometric mean forecast path you steer by, not a random Monte Carlo draw and not “most likely.” Prefer it for a stable year-by-year read of the plan. Switch away when you want to explore other randomized scenarios or paths in the multi-path cloud. The ✦ mark matches the live FID control (compass / north star, not a rating star).Full entry →, min/max, and success % sit next to that cloud so you can read the spread without tracing thousands of lines.

So: other tools often show the journey; this summary chart shows where the journeys finish. Both are compounding with volatilityHow much an investment's returns bounce around from year to year, not whether it goes up or down overall. Written σ (sigma) in the product. Two holdings can have the same average return and feel completely different to live through — and high volatility drags the growth you actually compound below that average.Full entry → and plan cash flows — the distribution is just the end-of-horizon cut.

IdeaPlain meaning
Compound growthGrowth applies to a growing base, not only the original deposit.
Rate × timeSmall rate gaps over long horizons dominate most one-year surprises.
One line vs manyA single smooth line is the easiest way to see the bend. It is not a forecast — real markets give you a range, and the plan shows you that range.
Paths vs distributionFull path fans show every year of every scenario. We prefer to show you where those scenarios end up, as a distribution — it is the same information without the ink.
