Alex's employer granted 1,200 restricted stock units and 2,000 stock options at the start of 2026, both in the company's stock, both vesting a quarter at a time over four years after a one-year cliff. None of it is Alex's yet. A plan that counted it as wealth today would be counting shares that vanish if Alex leaves; a plan that ignored it would miss four years of income. This lesson shows the middle: unvested shares are a memo, and each vest is booked in its year as the wages it is.

Today's lesson

By the end of this lesson you should be able to answer these questions. Each one links to the section that answers it.

  1. How are unvested stock units and options valued, and why are they not yet net worth? See Two grants, one stock.
  2. How is a vest taxed, and in which year? See A vest is wages.
  3. What happens to the shares once they vest? See Where the shares go.
  4. What does the plan look like after the last grant has vested? See Four years, then wages alone.
  5. What does this plan leave out about stock options and stock purchase plans? See What this plan is not showing.

Both grants name the same stock, which is also the brokerage's own US Equity Market position. That matters: the grants follow the stock's simulated price, so a forecast path where the market does well is a path where the vests are worth more too.

The RSU card values the unvested shares at today's price, $180,000, and says they are forfeited if Alex leaves, held into the Brokerage as they vest, and vest in four tranches of 300 from 2027 to 2030. That unvested value is a memo beside net worth, not part of it.

The option card is the same shape with a strike price. An option is worth the spread between the stock and the strike, $50 a share today, so 2,000 options are $100,000 unvested. These are sold as they vest, and they expire in 2036.

Open the CF TableA Forecast Detail chip. CF = cash flows for the selected year — incomes, expenses, taxes, and invest / sell / borrow totals. on 2027, the first year after the cliff.

Three income lines: Alex's salary, the RSU tranche, and the option tranche. Each vest arrives as wages, which is how it is taxed. Payroll tax and ordinary income tax apply to it in the year it vests, whatever Alex does with the shares afterward. The RSU line is 300 shares at that year's simulated price; the option line is 500 options times the spread over the $100 strike.

Did you know?

Vested shares are taxed as wages on the day they vest, whether you sell them or keep them. Keeping them is the same, for tax purposes, as being paid in cash and using it to buy your employer's stock that day. Only the change in price after the vest is a capital gain.

The two grants answer that question differently, and the Moves TableA Forecast Detail chip. Mov = Movements. Holding-level trades for the selected year — sold or invested, after, %, and Why. for the same year shows both answers.

The RSU vest appears as a linked investment into the brokerage's US Equity Market position: the wages arrived and were bought straight into the stock, with a cost basis equal to the vest value. From there the shares are an ordinary holding, growing with the stock and sold when the plan needs cash, with the gain from the vest price onward taxed as a capital gain.

The option vest has no linked line. It was sold at vest, so its spread is spendable cash, and it is invested wherever the plan's residual strategy puts surplus.

The CF ChartA Forecast Detail chip. CF = cash flows. Money in above the line, money out below, by category over the years. The net line, drawn in thick black, is net cash flow — often the thing you came for. shows the shape: four taller years while the grants vest, then the salary on its own from 2031. The grants are gone from the balance sheet's Equity compensation section once the last tranche has vested.

Desktop app only

Open Transforms and select Retirement & forecast end. Move Alex's retirement to 2029. The grants say unvested shares are forfeited when Alex leaves, so the 2029 and 2030 tranches disappear from the cash flows. Change either grant's If they leave to Vests at retirement and the remaining shares vest together in the retirement year instead.

Transforms → Modifiables on this plan, Global tab, with Retirement & forecast end selected. That row is where the retirement age moves.

Then open the option grant and set the strike to $200. The stock is at $150, so every tranche is under water at today's price and books nothing. On the paths where the stock climbs past $200 by 2027, the vest reappears.

Incentive stock options are modeled as if they were non-qualified: the spread is taxed as wages at vest. The alternative minimum tax and the capital-gains treatment of a qualifying disposition are not modeled, so a plan holding ISOs should read its vest-year tax as the conservative case. An employee stock purchase plan is not modeled at all.
