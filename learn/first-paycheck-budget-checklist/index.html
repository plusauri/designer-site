Riley is 23, a year out of college, and earning $65,000 at a first real job. Rent gets paid, the car gets paid, the 401(k)An employer-sponsored retirement account, named after the section of tax law that created it — so the name says nothing about what it does. Contributions are usually payroll-deducted, often matched by the employer, and come in traditional (pre-tax) and Roth flavors. A 403(b) is the same idea for schools and nonprofits, a 457 for government workers.Full entry → takes 3% because that was the box on the onboarding form, and whatever is left at the end of the month is gone by the next one. Riley has never written the expenses down, and so has no answer to the only budgeting question that matters: how much is being saved each month, and is it enough?

This lesson is a checklist for answering that. It walks through every category a first paycheck goes to, asks the questions that find the amounts, and puts the totals on a plan so the tool can do the arithmetic. It then measures each section against a few rules of thumb, and shows what happens, this year and forty years from now, when the sections that are over their range are trimmed and the difference is given somewhere to go.

Go section by section and write a monthly number beside every line, even when the number is zero. A zero you wrote is a decision. A line you skipped is a guess. Each section opens with the questions that find the amounts, and closes with what Riley wrote down and how each line sorts into Need, Want, Savings or Unexpected. Those four buckets come back later, when the budget is measured.

Riley's checklist comes to $4,390 a month before the 401(k), or $52,680 a year, which is 81% of gross pay. What is left after that has to cover the tax bill and the retirement contribution, and we are about to find out whether it does.

A plan does not want forty line items. It wants each section as one number a year, which is the monthly total times twelve, and it wants savings as contributions on the accounts they go into rather than as expenses. That is how both plans in this lesson were built:

  • Each expense section is one living expense line named for the section, with its monthly total times twelve in 2026 money. Housing is $2,325 × 12 = $27,900, Transportation is $850 × 12 = $10,200, and so on.
  • Insurance and health is a healthcare line rather than a living expense, so the tool can switch it to Medicare at 65 and so it shows up under its own heading.
  • The 401(k) is an account with two contributions, Riley's deferral and the employer's match, and the match is marked as employer-funded so it counts as income rather than as something Riley paid.
  • Salary is $65,000 until retirement at 67, Social Security is estimated from a statement and claimed at full retirement age, and there is no student loan. If you carry one, it goes on the plan as a loan and appears as its own line.

The 50/30/20 rule is a target, and it is one line long: half of take-home pay to needs, three-tenths to wants, a fifth to savings. Riley's wants are inside their range. Needs are four-fifths of take-home, which is normal on a first salary in a city with real rents. Savings are 4% against a target of 20%, and that gap is the shortfall the lesson is looking for. The buckets say where it can come from: a little from wants, and more from the one need that is bigger than all the others.

Rules of thumb are not laws, and every one of them has exceptions by city and by circumstance. They are useful because they say which sections deserve a hard look. Here are the ranges this lesson uses, measured against Riley's checklist as a share of gross pay. A section inside its range is fine. A section above it is where to look first.

Each highlighted row comes with a reason, because the reason is what makes the number worth acting on.

Transportation is over by a little, and the car payment is why. A car payment is the one line in this section that a budget cannot trim; it ends when the loan ends. The lever this year is small, which is shopping the insurance and cutting the rideshares. The bigger lever is the next car, which should cost less.

Dining out and entertainment is over by three points of pay, which is about $2,000 a year. It is the most flexible line on the whole checklist, and the rule of thumb is 5% because that is roughly where a single person on one salary can eat out regularly without it being the thing the budget is built around.

Retirement saving is under by ten points of pay, and the first three of those are free. The employer matches half of the first 6%, so raising the deferral from 3% to 6% costs Riley about $160 a month before tax and collects about $80 a month that is currently left on the table. Nothing else on this page has that return.

The emergency fund is under, which is the reason the "life happens" line exists and the reason it is not enough. A hundred dollars a month covers the parking ticket. It does not cover a month without a paycheck, and three months of Riley's expenses is about $13,000.

Rent is at the line rather than over it, and the housing row is marked watch rather than over because the all-in band is the loosest of these and depends most on where you live. But housing is the section where the room is, if Riley wants it. A roommate does not change the rule; it changes the rent.

Small leaks sink great ships. SLSGS

The trims are $620 a month, small beside Riley's budget. Kept for forty years, they move the plan from succeeding about half the time to succeeding more than nine times in ten.

  • Write every line down, including the zeros. The checklist's job is to find the lines you never see, and the tax line alone is a sixth of Riley's pay.
  • Total each section as monthly times twelve and put it on the plan as one line. The tool then does the arithmetic, including the tax and the leftover, every year for the rest of your life.
  • Sort into needs, wants, savings and unexpected, and measure against the rules of thumb. The point of a range is not the verdict; it is that a section over its range is where to look first.
  • The first money to move is the money that is already free. A deferral below the employer match is a pay cut you chose.
  • The room in a first-job budget is usually in housing and in dining out, and it is worth about twice the net worth at retirement in this plan. How much of that room to take is a lifestyle decision, and it is easier to make once you can see what it buys.
