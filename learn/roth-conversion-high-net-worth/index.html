When bracket-filling stops working

The situation Helen and Arturo Vance are 66 and 64 and retired after selling a business. Most of their money sits in a taxable brokerage account, with $10 million of it in a Treasury fund, and $5.5 million is in a traditional IRA. Everything they read says retirees should convert to Roth in their low-income years. Their portfolio's interest alone already puts them in the 32% bracket. Is a Roth conversion still worth doing, and how?

Where it is in the app bracket

Where it shows in the forecast

The Brackets view for Treasuries, fill to 24%. Portfolio income fills each column into the 32% bracket and the policy converts nothing. From 2035 the required distributions on the untouched IRA push every year into the 37% bracket.

This is the policy working correctly. A fill-to-bracket rule only has something to do when there is an empty bracket below the household's income, and a portfolio this size fills those brackets itself. Raising the target does not rescue it either. The 37% bracket has no top, so "fill to 37%" has no amount to aim at, and a household at this level chooses a conversion amount on purpose instead of filling a bracket.

The cost of doing nothing arrives in 2035. The IRA has grown untouched for eight years, and its first required distribution is about $385,000. Stacked on the interest, that puts every year from then on in the 37% bracket. Forced traditional RMDs shows the same pressure on a smaller household.

A municipal bond is a loan to a state, a city, or an authority that runs a project such as a toll road, a hospital or a water system. Some are paid from the issuer's general taxes. Others are paid only from what the project earns. Either way, the bond is only as good as the borrower's ability to pay. The chance that it pays late, pays less, or stops paying is credit riskThe chance that a borrower pays late, pays less than promised, or stops paying. Every bond carries some except US Treasuries, which are treated as free of it. A municipal bond is only as sound as the state, city, or project behind it, and a higher yield is usually the price a borrower pays for more of this risk.Full entry →.

Treasuries are treated as free of credit risk, because the federal government borrows in the currency it issues. Municipal bonds are not. Defaults on rated municipal bonds have been rare, far rarer than on corporate bonds of the same rating, but they happen. Detroit's bankruptcy in 2013 cut payments to some of its bondholders, and Puerto Rico restructured most of its debt after 2017. A household that buys the bonds of its own state for the state tax break also ties a large share of its money to one state's finances.

The forecast cannot show this risk. The muni fund in this plan carries price swings like any bond fund, but no plan in Tutilis models a default as an event. When Munis, fill to 24% ends ahead of the Treasury plan, part of what it is showing is the absence of a risk the model does not draw.

There's no free lunch. TNFL

The muni sleeve keeps more after tax and opens room for conversions. The price is credit risk the Treasury fund does not carry, and a spread across many issuers is what keeps it small.

Bracket-filling is one reason to convert, and at this level of wealth it is usually unavailable. Other reasons get stronger as wealth rises.

  • Required distributions stack on the floor. A large IRA left alone grows, and its required distributions are added on top of the portfolio income. A dollar converted at 35% now may otherwise come out at 37% later.
  • A survivor files alone. When one spouse dies, the other files as single, and the single brackets are about half as wide. The same income is taxed at higher rates for the rest of the survivor's life.
  • Heirs pay the tax on what is left. Most heirs must empty an inherited traditional IRAA traditional IRA held by someone who inherited it. It keeps its tax character, so every withdrawal is ordinary income, but the owner's lifetime RMD rules stop at the death and the beneficiary rules take over: for most adult children, the account must be empty by the end of the tenth year after the death. No one may contribute to it, and withdrawals carry no 10% early-withdrawal tax at any age.Full entry → within ten years, on top of their own earnings. Inheriting an IRA: the 10-year rule shows how that lands. A Roth passes to them tax-free.
  • Above the estate exemption, conversion tax leaves the estate. The federal estate taxA tax on what a person owns at death, paid by the estate before heirs receive it. The federal tax applies only above a large exemption ($15 million per person in 2026) at a top rate of 40%. About a dozen states and the District of Columbia have their own estate tax, most with far lower exemptions.Full entry → takes up to 40% of an estate above the exemption. Income tax paid on a conversion is money that is no longer in the estate, and a traditional IRA gets no step-up in basisAt death, the cost basis of investments in a taxable account, and of real estate, resets to their market value that day. The gains built up during the owner's life are never taxed as income, and an heir who sells right away owes little or no capital-gains tax. A traditional IRA gets no step-up.Full entry → at death, while the taxable account does.

Fixed $500k a year keeps the Treasuries and converts $500,000 every year from 2027 through 2034. With the floor already in the 32% bracket, that $500,000 runs through the 35% bracket into the 37% bracket. The household pays more tax in those eight years. In return, the IRA is about half the size by 2035, and the first required distribution is about $182,000 instead of about $385,000. The later years stay in the 35% bracket instead of the 37% bracket.

Comparing these plans by ending net worth misleads, because net worth counts a dollar in a traditional IRA the same as a dollar in a Roth. The traditional dollar still owes income tax, and the Roth dollar owes none. Tutilis's outcome card adds a row for this: the ending net worth after the income tax heirs would still owe on traditional balances, at 24% unless you choose another rate.

Read that row on each plan, with the simulation set to 1,000 paths so the comparison is steady. At the medianThe middle outcome — half above, half below — as against the average, which a few enormous results can drag upward. Over long horizons the two come apart badly, and the median is the one that answers "what does a typical path look like?"Full entry →, Fixed $500k a year leaves the heirs about $1 million more than Treasuries, fill to 24%, on an estate of about $70 million. Heirs of an estate this size are likely to pay more than 24%, and at a 37% heir rate the fixed plan's lead grows.

The weak paths tell the other half. At the 10th percentileA position along outcomes sorted from worst to best. The 10th percentile (P10) is the outcome only one in ten falls below; the 50th is the median; the 90th (P90) is the one only one in ten beats. Forecasts report P10 and P90 to show the downside and upside without leaning on the single best or worst run.Full entry → the fixed plan ends a little behind. The conversion tax is paid up front, and in a poor market the Roth has less growth with which to earn it back. A conversion at the top rate is a bet that the money will grow and that later rates will be at least as high, and on most paths that bet pays modestly.

The difference is small beside the estate, and that is a fair description of the trade at this level. A conversion at 37% is close to even, and it wins at the margins: smaller required distributions, fewer years at the top rate for the survivor, and less taxable income for the heirs. The estate-tax argument from the last section adds to it. These plans have estate tax switched on, so the outcome card also has an After estate tax column. At the median, the fixed plan's estate pays about $280,000 less federal estate tax than the fill-to-24% plan's, because the conversion tax has already left the estate. The card keeps the two adjustments apart: the after-estate column does not also take off the income tax the heirs still owe on the traditional IRA, and the heirs' row does not take off the estate tax. Read together, both favor the conversion.

Munis, fill to 24% ends well ahead of both, because it pays far less tax every year. Read that lead together with the credit-risk section above.

  • The estate tax and the heirs' income tax are not combined. The outcome card shows each on its own. Heirs who inherit a traditional IRA from a taxable estate can also deduct the estate tax paid on it from their income tax, which this does not model.
  • Filing statusWhich set of tax tables applies — single, married filing jointly, and the less common ones. It sets the bracket widths, the standard deduction, and most of the income thresholds a plan tests against, so a household's filing status changes what every other tax number means.Full entry → does not change at a death. The plan files jointly for its whole length. To see the survivor's narrower brackets, build a second plan that files as single from the year you expect the first death.
  • The heirs' rate is one flat number. Real heirs spread an inherited IRA over ten years at their own rates, which this does not model.
  • No default is ever modeled. The municipal fund's credit risk is described, not simulated.
  • No state is selected. State income tax on a conversion, and state tax on municipal interest from other states, both change the answer. Where to retire on $20 million takes up the state.

A conversion at this size is usually planned with a tax professional and an estate attorney, alongside trusts, gifts and charitable plans this forecast does not represent. The plan gives that conversation numbers.

A large taxable portfolio pays an income floor of its own, and at this level the floor can fill the brackets that conversion advice is built around. A fill-the-bracket policy then converts nothing, and that is the correct answer to the question it was asked. Municipal bonds lower the floor and bring the room back, at the price of credit risk. Above the floor, a conversion is an amount chosen on purpose, and it is judged by later rates, the survivor's brackets, the heirs and the estate rather than by the bracket it fills.
