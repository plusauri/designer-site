Both rows come from the same loan, so both raise the same card. The top of the card holds the loan's terms: the remaining balance, the rate, the monthly payment and the home it finances. The Flow value section below them lists the payment and its two halves, each with its first and last year. The sparkline at the bottom draws both halves over the life of the loan.

  • Principal starts lower and trends up. Later years put more of each payment toward paying off the loan.
  • Interest starts higher and trends down. Early years are interest-heavy, and as the balance shrinks, less interest is due.

The Home mortgage card. In the sparkline, the two lines cross partway through the loan: interest falls toward zero while principal rises to make up nearly the whole payment.

That is what “paying off a mortgage” looks like in cash-flow form: you begin under the burden of interest, and you end by retiring the principal. Same monthly commitment in spirit; the mix of the two outflows flips over the life of the loan.

For the math lovers — why interest falls and principal rises

A fixed-rate loan of \(P\) repaid over \(n\) months at monthly rate \(r\) has a constant payment

\[ M = P\,\frac{r}{1-(1+r)^{-n}} \]

Each month's interest is the rate times what is still owed, \(I_k = r\,B_{k-1}\), and the rest of the payment retires principal. The balance only falls, so the interest only falls. The principal part grows by a factor of \(1+r\) every month:

\[ P_k = (M - rP)\,(1+r)^{k-1} \]

On a 30-year loan at 6.5%, interest is about 86% of the first payment, and principal first exceeds interest around month 233, in the twentieth year.

Honest frame for Springfield

The mortgage is a material cash-flow drain during work and often into retirement planning horizon years — large enough to compete with lifestyle and savings, not a trivial line item. It is not the whole story — dual incomes, portfolio, and living phases still dominate the drama.

Why Johnsons are not “Springfield minus the loan”

If we only deleted Springfield’s mortgage and left every other number equal, paid-off would look like a free win. These samples are recalibrated on purpose:

  • Johnsons often carry a higher lifestyle (especially in retirement) and a slightly leaner portfolio so success stays in a comparable near-miss band.
  • Same state / filing shape so the housing pivot is not confounded by a different tax map.

So: compare mechanisms (P&I present vs absent, cash left for investing / spending), not “which family is richer.”
