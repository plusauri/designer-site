Noor lives and works in the United States. The salary is in dollars, the living expenses are in dollars, the brokerage account is in dollars. But Noor also owns a condo in Tokyo that is let to a tenant, a savings account left behind after a few years in Frankfurt, and a small villa in Costa Rica bought for retirement. Four currencies, one household, one plan.

If you have only ever planned in one currency, the currency dropdown beside every amount in the plan editor can look like overkill. You type 500,000, and there is a $ USD sitting next to it that you never touch. Why is it there at all?

This lesson is the answer. The Tokyo condo is worth 65,000,000. Sixty-five million of what is the whole question. Sixty-five million yen is a modest apartment; sixty-five million dollars is a skyscraper. The digits are the same, and nothing about them tells you which.

So an amount in Tutilis is three things, not one: a year it was quoted in, a currency it is counted in, and the number. The year is what makes a 2024 appraisal mean 2024 money rather than whatever year the plan starts. The currency is what makes 65,000,000 mean an apartment rather than a skyscraper. Neither is decoration; leave either out and the number is a guess.

For a household that only ever deals in dollars, the currency costs nothing. It is the default, so you never pick it, and when the plan is saved a dollar amount does not even record a currency — "no currency stated" means US dollars. The dropdown you never touch is the whole price of the feature.

Open Dossier on the tagged plan and look at what each item says about itself. Every item carries its own currency, and nothing is converted where it is stored:

Two details are easy to miss. The condo's cost basis is a bare number, 52,000,000, with no currency of its own — it borrows the valuation's. A basis is only meaningful against the value it will be compared with, so the two are kept in one currency by construction, and when you change the valuation's currency in the editor the basis follows at once. And the rent is not a property of the condo at all; it is its own item, a rental-income generator, with its own currency. A yen apartment could in principle be let for dollars, so the model does not assume.

"Recorded, not converted" is the rule. The plan you typed is the plan on disk. Every conversion you will ever see is computed from these native amounts and a rate, on the way to the screen.

The Bird's Eye is the first place the four currencies meet. It shows the plan's items as one list of present values, and it has to pick a single currency to do that — your display currency, dollars here:

The yen condo is worth a bit less than the dollar brokerage account; the colón villa is a small fraction of either. Each converted line carries a marker — (¥↔$), the currency it was modeled in and then the one it is shown in — so a converted figure never passes as a native one. The FX button on the rail lists every currency the plan holds and the rate behind each marker.

The forecast does the same thing, one level deeper. The engine simulates each item in its own currency — the condo appreciates in yen, the rent grows in yen, the management fee inflates in yen — and converts only at the edges, where a year's cash flows are netted and where a balance sheet is totaled. Set the forecaster to 2027 and read the cash-flow statement: the rent arrives as about $12,500 net of the property's expenses, beside a salary of about $160,000. That $12,500 is a yen figure that crossed the boundary once, at the plan's rate.

Taxes cross the same boundary. Noor files in the US, so the yen rent is taxed as US passive income, in dollars, on the same statement as the salary. The tax calculator picks its unit of account from the jurisdiction, not from the item — which is why the item has to say what it is in.

Now switch to Same digits, no tag. Nothing was retyped. Each amount simply stopped saying which currency it is in — which, as above, means the plan reads every one of them as dollars.

Noor is now a nine-figure household on paper, with a condo that earns more in rent each year than the salary does in a decade. Success is a perfect 100%. Every table is internally consistent; every table is wrong.

Nothing in the software can catch this. 65,000,000 is a perfectly legal dollar amount, and a plan cannot tell an untagged yen figure from a real one — they are the same bytes. The only defense is that the amount says what it is at the moment it is typed, by the one person who knows. That is what the dropdown is for.

This is not a hypothetical. The engine once had exactly this bug — a yen valuation read at face value as dollars, on the forecast side only, so the Bird's Eye and the forecaster disagreed by more than a hundred to one. The fix was not to convert more cleverly; it was to make every item carry its currency all the way through the simulation, and a test now holds the engine to that.

The obvious cheaper design is a single currency for the whole plan: one dropdown, in the settings, and every amount inherits it. It works exactly as long as every amount really is in that currency, and it fails the day one isn't.

Desktop app only

That dropdown is the plan's currency choice. On this plan it is set to multiple currencies, and the picker under it only seeds the next amount you type.

The currency choice on Every amount tagged.

Noor's plan has no honest answer to "what currency is this plan in". It is in four. The moment a single foreign item appears, a plan-level setting has to become a per-item one, and every amount already typed has to be gone back over and tagged — by someone who may no longer remember which appraisal was in which money. Amounts entered before the change and amounts entered after it are indistinguishable on disk. That is the lock-in: not that it is hard to add the field later, but that nothing entered before it can be trusted afterward.

Attaching the currency to the amount from the first day is the honest shape. It says nothing a single-currency household has to think about, and it costs a default value that is not even written down. What it buys is that the first foreign item is one more row, not a migration.

The rates behind every conversion above live on the plan. Open Assumptions → Currency to see them: one rate per currency against the dollar, so no combination of edits can be inconsistent with itself. These two plans carry no rates of their own and ride the bundled snapshot; a real plan can type its own, or press Get latest FX and fine-tune.

Desktop app only

Assumptions → Currency on Every amount tagged. One row per currency against the dollar, and Exchange rates set to Fixed.

By default the rates do not move. Every year of the forecast converts at the same rate, which means a plan that has not asked otherwise treats currency as a unit and not as a risk: the yen condo grows at a real-estate rate, and the fact that the yen itself will wander against the dollar over forty years is not part of the spread. That default is deliberate, and it is exactly what every plan did before the currency-risk model existed.

The model is there when you want it. Under the rate table, the same panel offers Exchange rates: Fixed | Modeled. Choose Modeled and each rate becomes part of the simulation: it drifts with its region's inflationA rise in the price level. The same basket of goods costs more dollars than it did. A household plan carries a growth rate on each bill, because one published inflation number is one basket. The reporting rate that restates future dollars into today's dollars is a separate assumption.Full entry → relative to the US and wanders with a real rate that mean-reverts over a few years, sampled per path, so the forecast can say how much of a household's spread comes from its currencies. Because every item already carries its currency through the whole simulation, the model slots in at the conversion boundary and changes nothing about how a plan is written. The lesson Currency risk in the forecast walks through it on a household built for the purpose.

Two smaller levers exist today under TransformsWhat-if levers that change the open plan without rewriting the whole dossier from scratch — try a claim age, spend level, or similar experiment.Full entry →. Convert re-expresses an item in another currency at the plan's rate, so you can ask what the household looks like if the condo is sold and the proceeds brought home. Redenominate relabels the currency without converting — which is the honest name for what the second plan on this page did by accident.

  • An amount is a number, a year, and a currency. Leave any of the three out and it is a guess.
  • The currency is free when you do not need it. Dollars are the default, and a dollar amount does not even record one.
  • Nothing is converted where it is stored. Conversion happens on the way to the screen and at the edges of the forecast, always from the native amount and a rate you can see.
  • The same digits under the wrong tag are not a small error. They are a different household.
  • Recording the currency from the first day is what makes the first foreign item a row instead of a migration — and what lets the currency-risk model slot in at the conversion boundary without touching how a plan is written.
