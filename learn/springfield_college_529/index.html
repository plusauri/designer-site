College is often a household’s largest multi-year outflow after housing. This lesson starts with what a 529 plan is, then looks at what college roughly costs, how saving into a 529 shows up in a plan, and a few ownership and leftover-money points that change planning more than people expect.

The live plan on the right is Springfield College 529. Homer and Marge have two children, Bart and Lisa, as dependents. Each child has a parent-owned 529, a college expense cash-flow template linked to that account, and $500 a month going in until that child starts school.

A 529 plan is a tax-advantaged account for education savings (and some related education uses under current law). In broad terms:

IdeaPlain meaning
ContributionsAfter-tax money you put in (no federal income tax deduction; some states offer a state break — state-specific). Modeled here — see Putting money in below
GrowthEarnings can grow federal income tax deferred while in the plan
Qualified withdrawalsFor qualified education expenses, earnings can come out federal income tax free (and often state-tax free)
Non-qualified withdrawalsEarnings may be taxed and can face an additional tax — rules and exceptions exist; check current IRS guidance
InvestmentsYou choose among the plan’s menu (often age-based or static fund options) — not every broker’s full lineup

The thing worth checking yourself

A contribution is a transfer, not a spend. The money leaves what the household can spend and turns up inside the 529 — it does not leave the household.

The check that shows it: net worth does not move when the saving starts. Not "moves a little" — it is the same number, because nothing was consumed and nothing was earned. The family moves $12,000 a year out of its everyday money and into the two 529s. All of it is still the family's own money, so the total the family owns stays the same.

So why bother?

If it does not change net worth, why model it — or do it? Because of the wrapper. Money inside a 529 grows without the annual tax drag it would carry in a taxable account, and qualifiedA tax word meaning the conditions were met, not a judgment of quality. A qualified Roth withdrawal is tax-free; an unqualified one is not. Qualified dividends get the lower capital-gain rates; ordinary ones do not. Whenever you see it, the interesting question is *which* conditions.Full entry → withdrawals come out federal-income-tax free. Same dollars, different tax treatment, and the gap compounds quietly over a decade. It is a real effect and a modest one at this household's scale — the wrapper is worth something, not everything.

What it actually buys: covered years

The bigger difference is not the tax break at all. It is whether the account still has money in it when the bill arrives.

Follow Bart College 529 through his four college years (2032–2035). On ForecasterA main-rail area with two jobs: (1) run the plan year by year and inspect Forecast Detail, and (2) Simulation Summary — many paths summarized into something like a probability of success., open BS TableA Forecast Detail chip. BS = balance sheet for the selected year — real assets, accounts, liabilities, net worth. Copyable companion to BS Chart. and move the selected year from 2031 to 2035. The account is listed by name, and its balance falls each year as tuition is paid out of it.

With $500 a month behind it, Bart's account still has money in it at the end of his second year and runs dry during his third (2034). The rest of his college is paid from ordinary household assets. Without the monthly saving, the same account would run dry during his second year. Lisa's account follows the same pattern two years later. It lasts into her third year (2036) with the saving and only into her second without it. In this plan, the saving pays for about one more year of college for each child.

That is the practical case for a 529: a funded account absorbs a large, dated, unavoidable expense instead of that expense landing on the rest of the plan.

You can structure accounts so a child is closer to the control of the money (for example some custodial setups). That can take parents out of the decision loop once the child can act on the account — a family and legal choice, not only a tax one.

Why parents often stay as owner

  • Keep the ability to change the beneficiary among eligible family members if one child does not need the full balance.
  • Keep investment and withdrawal decisions with the people funding the plan.

The sample’s 529s sit on the household balance sheet with the parents’ other accounts — the usual “parent owns, child is beneficiary” story for teaching.

Older advice said “student-owned 529s crush aid.” Current federal FAFSA treatment differs:

Situation (dependent student on the FAFSA)Typical FAFSA asset treatment
Parent-owned 529 for the studentReported as a parent asset
Dependent student–owned 529Also reported as a parent asset (not as a high-rate student asset)
UGMA/UTMA and many other student assetsStill student assets, assessed at a much higher rate than parent assets
Independent student who owns a 529Reported as a student asset

Parent assets are assessed at a low maximum rate (commonly cited around 5.6% of countable assets in the federal formula — exact brackets and allowances change). Student assets are assessed far more aggressively (commonly cited around 20%).

Did you know?

A 529 is not tied to the child it was opened for. The owner can change the beneficiary to another member of that child's family, and the switch itself generally costs no income tax and no penalty. The family reaches further than most people guess. Brothers and sisters count, and so do first cousins and even the child's own parents.

So if you pre-fund Bart and he needs less school, or none, what is left can often be redirected toward Lisa or another eligible relative. The money stays in the plan and keeps its tax treatment, and nobody has to take a non-qualified withdrawal.

On this sample: two separate 529 accounts and two college templates make the per-child story visible. In real life you might use one account and change the beneficiary over time, or keep parallel accounts — process and plan menus differ by state program.

Under SECUREThe law (and its sequel) that moved several retirement-account rules: the age RMDs start, catch-up contributions, and how quickly an inherited account must be emptied. Its practical effect on a plan is that dates you may remember from a decade ago are no longer the dates.Full entry → 2.0, unused 529 money can sometimes move into a Roth IRA for the beneficiary, not as unlimited free Roth funding. The valve is deliberately small. The account has to be old, the money has to have been in it for years, each year's move is held to the Roth contribution limit, and the whole lifetime total is capped at $35,000.

Reference — the rollover conditions
ConditionTypical rule of thumb
Account age529 must have been maintained for the beneficiary for at least 15 years
Seasoning of moneyAmounts (and earnings) from contributions in the last five years are generally ineligible
Annual limitRollover counts against the beneficiary’s annual Roth IRA contribution limit for that year
Lifetime cap$35,000 lifetime per beneficiary (aggregate from 529s into Roth IRAs)
Whose RothRoth must be owned by the 529 beneficiary
Earned incomeBeneficiary still needs compensation (and other usual Roth contribution rules that apply)

These are rules of thumb. Verify current IRS and plan rules before acting.

So a 529 is primarily an education vehicle with a capped retirement-side relief valve — useful against “what if they don’t go,” not a substitute for maxing workplace retirement accounts first.

Some state 529 menus charge fees in ways that make each investment option in an account costly (details vary widely by program — always read your plan’s fee schedule).

In this section, growth means stock funds, which can rise more over time but swing harder along the way. Ballast means steadier holdings such as bond or stable-value funds. The word comes from sailing, where ballast is the weight low in a ship that keeps it steady.

Idea: suppose you want roughly 60% growth / 40% ballast for two young children and the plan charges a noticeable fee per fund option held. Putting 60/40 in each child’s account can mean four fee-bearing options. Putting mostly growth in one child’s account and mostly ballast in the other’s can keep a similar household risk mix with fewer options — if the plan’s fee design rewards that.
