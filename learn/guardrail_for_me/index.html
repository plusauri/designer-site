Retirees and researchers have spent decades asking a deceptively simple question: how much can I spend from a portfolio without running out? No single answer fits every household, but a handful of well-known approaches have shaped how people talk about “safe” spending. This lesson invites you to meet those approaches on your own plan — not as abstract percentages, but as side-by-side paths you can compare to the lifestyle you modeled.

With gratitude

The six studies in Guardrails rest on other people’s hard work: historical market analysis, decision-rule design, actuarial tables, and plain-language writing that made those ideas usable. We are grateful to the authors and publishers below. Our sandbox is an educational illustration of their published-style rules under one shared path — it is not a reprint of their papers, not a certification of their results, and not advice. Where a public link is available, we point to it so you can go to the source.

Works cited — the six studies
  • Bengen SAFEMAX. William P. Bengen, “Determining Withdrawal Rates Using Historical Data”, Journal of Financial Planning, 1994 (reprint). The later valuation-aware framing is in A Richer Retirement.
  • Kitces ratchet. Michael Kitces, “The Ratcheting Safe Withdrawal Rate”, Kitces.com, 2015.
  • Guyton–Klinger. Jonathan Guyton and William Klinger, “Decision Rules and Maximum Initial Withdrawal Rates”, Journal of Financial Planning, 2006.
  • Maggiulli 3.65%. Nick Maggiulli, Of Dollars And Data, and The Wealth Ladder. Spending 0.01% a day comes to about 3.65% a year.
  • Modified RMD. Internal Revenue Service, Publication 590-B, Uniform Lifetime Table. The product extends it as a planning table for ages before RMDs start.
  • Safety-first. Wade D. Pfau, Safety-First Retirement Planning: An Integrated Approach for a Worry-Free Retirement, Retirement Researcher Media, 2019. Pfau's safety-first approach covers essential spending with guaranteed income first, from Social Security, pensions and annuities, and invests the rest. The product rule follows that shape: essentials are covered by guaranteed income, and a discretionary rate is spent from the portfolio that is left. It is an illustration of the approach, not a rule taken from a single table in the book.

Guardrails then runs those published-style rules against your portfolio size, horizon, and lifestyle needs under one shared sandbox path. This lesson is about personalizing that comparison: start from the Springfield household nest egg, see what each study would spend, and set it next to the plan’s own incomes and needs. It is not a Monte CarloThe method behind a multi-path run: draw many randomized return paths, run the plan down each one, and read the spread of what happened. Named after the casino, because the engine is repeated random sampling rather than a formula — which is why two runs of one plan differ a little, and why a success rate is a proportion of sampled futures rather than a promise.Full entry → survival score and not investment advice.

Did you know?

Nick Maggiulli's rule of thumb is to spend 0.01% of your portfolio each day. That sounds tiny, but over a year it adds up to 3.65%, a little under the famous 4% rule. On a $1 million portfolio it comes to about $100 a day.

Study onset at primary retirement

Published rules are framed for someone who is retiring (or already retired). Guardrails matches that footing:

  1. Before primary retirement — the sandbox portfolio only grows. There is no study withdrawal \(S_t\). The nest egg that appears at retirement is what the rule would start with if you opened Guardrails that year.
  2. From retirement onward — each rule’s first draw year is rule-relative year 0 (seed rates, CAPECyclically Adjusted Price-to-Earnings — a valuation measure that averages earnings over ten years to smooth the cycle. It is used as a rough gauge of whether a market is expensive, and it is a weak short-term signal: high CAPE has preceded both poor decades and long rallies.Full entry → cell, Kitces base, and so on land once, on the grown nest egg).
  3. Already retired (or no retirement year on the primary owner) — draws start at forecast start (“now”).

On Springfield, retirement is still years ahead, so early years on Spending paths sit near $0 study spend while the portfolio compounds. That is intentional, not a broken chart.

Portfolio return is nominal

The Return μ knob is a constant nominalA face-value figure, with no adjustment for inflation. A balance that doubles in nominal terms over 25 years may buy little more than it does today. The counterpart is real, which is inflation-adjusted and closer to what the money will actually buy — always check which one a chart shows.Full entry → annual rate after each withdrawal (default 8%, with default inflationA rise in the price level. The same basket of goods costs more dollars than it did. A household plan carries a growth rate on each bill, because one published inflation number is one basket. The reporting rate that restates future dollars into today's dollars is a separate assumption.Full entry → 2.5% ≈ mid-single-digit real for a balanced book). The sandbox keeps both knobs in a reasonable multiyear band (non-negative through about 15%) so the path stays an illustration of published rules, not a deflation or “50% forever” toy.

Why not 5% or even 7% as the default? At 5% nominal (~2.5% real) both Bengen SAFEMAX and Kitces ratchet tend to spend the whole portfolio on multi-decade horizons. At 7%, Bengen SAFEMAX still often depletes near year 40 under its valuation-based starting rate (~5.1% at CAPE 22) with pure COLACost-Of-Living Adjustment — the annual raise applied to a benefit so it keeps pace with prices. Social Security has one; many pensions do not, which is the difference that decides whether a fixed payment still buys anything in twenty years.Full entry → — common when the study horizon matches a long plan. That is arithmetic, not a UI bug: study-style starting rates assume something closer to historical balanced returns (roughly 7–8% nominal / ~4–5% real for a 50/50–60/40 mix). The default sits at the upper end of that band so the first open does not look like “every rule fails.”

You can still set 7% or 5% within the slider to see which paths drain — that is a useful experiment, not a bug.

Sequence-of-returns caveat (read this)

Bengen SAFEMAX comes from historical return sequences, not from a single average return. A constant 8% path can only illustrate the mechanism of the rule (COLA, valuation-based initial rate, depletion markers). It cannot reproduce SAFEMAX or prove a safe spending rate. Treat the chart as a comparison sandbox, not a historical replay.

Is 4% still safe?

William Bengen's 1994 study found that an inflation-adjusted 4% withdrawal survived every 30-year period in US history, and the Trinity study of 1998 reached a similar answer with a different method. Wade Pfau did much of the research that tested how far that answer travels. In 2010 he ran the same kind of test on 109 years of market data from 17 developed countries, and a 4% real withdrawal would have been safe in only 4 of them. In 2013, with Michael Finke and David Blanchett, he showed that when bond returns are set to the low yields of that year rather than their historical average, the estimated failure rate of a 4% plan with half in stocks rises from about 6% to 57%.

Neither paper says 4% is wrong for every household. Both say that a safe rate depends on the returns the next decades deliver, and that the US past was unusually kind. That is the reason the Return μ knob in this sandbox matters, and the reason to try the lower settings in Try this. Pfau's later answer to the problem is the safety-first approach that the Safety-first rule illustrates: build a floor of guaranteed income for essential spending, so less depends on any withdrawal rateYearly spending from the portfolio as a share of its value. A safe withdrawal rate is the highest rate that historically never ran out over a chosen horizon — the origin of the "4% rule". Variable approaches (VPW, guardrails) recompute the rate each year instead of fixing it at the start.Full entry → being safe.

Coverage buffer vs Monte Carlo success

On Springfield Baseline under defaults the coverage buffer is usually green / solvent and Extra needed is $0 for every rule — a comfortable nest egg clears the study-bound funding story. Forecaster Monte Carlo on the same household often still reports something like ~80% success at the default Silver threshold (not 100%). Those results can both be true — they answer different questions. A green buffer is not “MC is wrong,” and a high Silver % is not “every published rule always funds this lifestyle.”

To see a tight or red buffer while Monte Carlo still looks livable, lower the portfolio control here, or open When Guardrails says you need a bigger nest egg (near-retire foil with a thinner nest egg). So:

  • Buffer red ≈ “this rule’s allowable spend never fully funds the modeled lifestyle under the sandbox path,” including years when plan stacks alone run short before the rule starts drawing.
  • High Silver success ≈ “most engine paths still fund the plan under residual draws and markets” — and the outcome mix still matters (clean success vs liquidity recovery vs failure; left-tail net worth).
  • Buffer green + Extra needed $0 on Springfield defaults ≈ the study-bound lifestyle story clears under these knobs — still not the same as Monte Carlo plan survival.

Which picture for “risk of failure”?

  • For “will this plan survive as the app actually forecasts it?” prefer Monte Carlo (success mix + downside tail — not the headline % alone). See What does success mean?.
  • For “if I discipline draws to this published rule, is lifestyle still funded?” prefer the coverage buffer, Modeled with study income, and Min portfolio / Extra needed on Rule summary. That question is intentionally stricter.
  • Neither alone is complete. Do not read high MC success as “a 4% study always funds this lifestyle,” and do not read a red buffer as “Monte Carlo is lying.”

Toolbar on the coverage buffer: Withdraw to need (default on) banks only the study spend needed after other incomes cover lifestyle — leave it on for a lifestyle-funding story. There is no separate “start buffer at retirement” switch: onset already lives in the study path, and the buffer carries full-horizon plan nets.

  • Not a spending policy in your forecast. The Forecaster never applies any of these rules; it spends what your cash flows and residual strategy say.
  • Not a claim that 8% (or any μ) will be earned every year.
