Portfolio insurance with puts and collars

The situation Dana and Lee Whitcomb are 63 and 61 and will retire next year with about $13 million, most of it in an S&P 500 index fund in a taxable account with a large unrealized gain. A crash in their first years of retirement is the outcome they fear most, and selling the stock to hold less of it would trigger a large tax bill. They are weighing index puts, or a collar, for the six years around retirement.

A household that holds most of its money in stocks can buy protection against a crash, much as it buys insurance on a house. The protection is an index put optionA put option bought on something you own, so that it pays when the price falls past a chosen floor. It works like insurance on the holding: it costs a premium in every year it is held, and it pays only in the bad ones. In Tutilis it is a hedge on an account, with a floor, settled once a year on the year's return.Full entry →: a contract that pays when the market ends the year below a chosen floor. Like any insurance it costs a premiumWhat you pay for coverage — a monthly insurance or Medicare cost — and not a bonus or a prize. Confusingly, the same word means "above face value" when applied to a bond. In a plan it is nearly always the insurance sense: a recurring outflow that rises with age and, past a threshold, with income.Full entry → every year, including the years nothing goes wrong, and the premium is priced so that on average the buyer pays more than the protection returns. This lesson follows one HNWHigh net worth — shorthand for a household wealthy enough that different planning problems dominate. Surcharge tiers, the investment surtax, and estate questions bind earlier, while running out of money is rarely the binding risk. There is no single official dollar line.Full entry → couple through three plans to show what the insurance pays in a crash, what it costs over a lifetime of quiet years, how a CollarA protective put paid for by selling a call on the same holding. The call gives away any return above its cap, so the holding keeps returns between the floor and the cap. A collar can cost little or nothing in cash; its cost is the best years it gives up.Full entry → trades away the best years to pay for it, and how the gains and losses are taxed.

The lesson is advanced because it leans on two ideas from elsewhere: a distribution of outcomes over many random futures, and sequence-of-returns riskThe order the good and bad years arrive in. Two plans with identical average returns can end in very different places if one takes its losses early while money is being withdrawn — selling into a fall removes shares that are then not there for the recovery. It is why retirement risk is not summarized by an average, and why the years either side of the day you stop working carry more weight than their count suggests.Full entry →. Timing a crash introduces both.

The three plans are the same household with one thing changed.

  • No hedge holds the portfolio as it is.
  • Index puts buys one-year puts on the S&P 500 at a floor 10% below the market, on the whole $9.8 million of the index fund, and rolls them every year from 2027 through 2032. The puts sit in the brokerage account.
  • Collar holds the same puts and pays for them by selling one-year calls 20% above the market, which gives away any return past 20% in a year.

The hedge covers the six years around retirement on purpose. Those are the years in which a crash does the most lasting damage to a plan that has started to spend, and after them the couple goes back to holding the portfolio unhedged.

A put with a floor of −10% pays whatever the index loses past 10% in the year. In a year the S&P 500 falls 35%, it pays 25% of the value it insures, and in a year the index falls 8% or rises it pays nothing. The plan settles the puts once a year, so the payoff for a year is the return the forecast draws for the index fund that year, and the puts pay exactly when the holding they insure has a bad year.

The price comes from the same forecast. The plan's assumption for large US stocks is a mean return of about 7.6% with a volatilityHow much an investment's returns bounce around from year to year, not whether it goes up or down overall. Written σ (sigma) in the product. Two holdings can have the same average return and feel completely different to live through — and high volatility drags the growth you actually compound below that average.Full entry → of about 16.4%, and on that distribution a put 10% below the market pays back about 1.2% of the insured value in an average year. Tutilis charges twice that by default, about 2.4% a year, because index puts have historically cost well more than they went on to pay. From 1990 to 2018 the option market's expected swing in the S&P 500, the VIX, averaged 19.3% a year, while the swing that followed averaged 15.1% (Bondarenko, for Cboe, 2019). Priced at that ratio, this put costs about twice what it pays back on average. That gap is called the volatility risk premium, and it is why insurance is a cost rather than a free lunch. In 2027 the premium on Index puts is about $249,000, and it is charged every year the puts are held, whatever the market does.

The 100% markup is an assumption, and it sits at the low end of the evidence. Markets also price options on the risk-free rate rather than on the stock's expected return, and they charge more for puts far below the market, and counting both puts this put at up to about 270% over its average payoff. You can change the markup on the hedge in the account editor, or try other values in Transforms.

For the math lovers — what a put pays on average

If the year's return \(r\) is normal with mean \(\mu\) and volatility \(\sigma\), a put with floor \(F\) pays on average

\[ E[\max(F - r, 0)] = (F - \mu)\,\Phi(d) + \sigma\,\varphi(d), \qquad d = \frac{F - \mu}{\sigma}, \]

where \(\Phi\) and \(\varphi\) are the standard normal distribution and density. With \(\mu = 7.56\%\), \(\sigma = 16.42\%\) and \(F = -10\%\), \(d\) is about \(-1.07\) and the expected payoff is about 1.2% of the insured value. A call with cap \(C\) is the mirror image:

\[ E[\max(r - C, 0)] = (\mu - C)\,\Phi(e) + \sigma\,\varphi(e), \qquad e = \frac{\mu - C}{\sigma}. \]

A bought put and a sold call at the same strike together pay \(F - \mu\) on average, which is how the two formulas check each other.

A collar pays for the puts by selling calls. The buyer of a call collects whatever the index gains past the cap, so the couple keeps every return up to 20% and gives away the rest. Calls are priced much closer to what they pay back than puts are, so Tutilis sells the call for 20% less than its average payoff. On the plan's own distribution that covers most of the put, and the Collar plan's net premium is about 0.69% a year, a little over a quarter of what the puts alone cost.

All of this sits on the brokerage account, in the Hedges section of its editor: what the hedge insures, its floor and cap, the years it is held, how it is taxed, and its price.

The cost has not gone away. It has moved from the cash flow into the best years. A year the index gains 30% is a 20% year on Collar, and those are the years a portfolio recovers in after a crash. The next two sections show where that matters.

The plainest test of insurance is the accident it is bought for. Turn on the Sequence-of-returns risk modifiable in Transforms, choose the Global Financial Crisis of 2007, and start it in 2027. Each plan then lives through the crisis's yearly returns from its first year of retirement, and its deepest year, a fall of about 41%, lands in 2028. The year after it is a gain of about 56%.

Open the cash flow for 2028 on Index puts. The puts pay about $2.9 million into the brokerage account, against a premium of about $225,000. The plan has more cash than it can spend, so it invests about $2.2 million, which buys stock at the bottom of the crash.

The same year on No hedge has no income at all. The plan sells about $529,000 to pay for living expenses and taxes, and about $296,000 of that is the S&P 500 fund, sold after it has fallen.

That one year is most of the difference. The crisis of 2008 fell for about a year and a half. The crash that began in 1929 fell for three years. In Transforms, choose the Great Crash of 1929 and start it in 2027. The S&P 500 then loses about 23% in 2027, 28% in 2028 and 61% in 2029, and gains about 140% in 2030.

Collar owns the same puts, and they pay just as much. In 2030 the index more than doubles, and the calls the plan sold take everything above 20%. The plan pays about $4.2 million on its calls that year, and it sells about $5.5 million of its holdings to do so. A collar pays for its protection with the recovery, and after a crash this deep the recovery is most of what the plan needs.

Both hedged plans lower the average, which is what insurance does when it costs more than it pays back. At this price the puts' trade is close to even at the bottom. Index puts pays about 2.4% in every year it is held and collects only in the bad ones, so its 10th percentile barely moves while its median falls by about $2.6 million and its mean by about $5 million. Its success rate still rises a little, because the plans that fail are the ones a crash catches, and those are the ones the puts pay in. Collar raises the 10th percentile the most and costs the most on average, because the plan's prices work against it twice: it pays more than fair value for the puts, and it receives less than fair value for the calls. A cheap collar is cheap in cash, and it is paid for in upside.

There's no free lunch. TNFL

Every hedged plan gives up some of its average outcome to buy a better bad one.

Options on a broad-based index such as the S&P 500 index itself are Section 1256 contractsA US tax category that includes options on a broad-based stock index and regulated futures. Each one is marked to market at the end of the year, so its gain or loss is taxed that year even if it is still held, and 60% of the gain or loss is treated as long-term and 40% as short-term whatever the holding period. Options on an exchange-traded fund or a single stock are not Section 1256 contracts.Full entry →. They are marked to market every December, so each year's gain or loss is taxed that year whether or not the position was closed, and 60% of it is treated as long-term and 40% as short-term regardless of how long it was held. Options on an exchange-traded fund or a single stock are taxed as ordinary equity options instead, and a one-year option's gain or loss is short-term.

Tutilis taxes a hedge in a taxable account this way. A year's net gain adds 60% to long-term gains and 40% to ordinary incomeIncome taxed at the regular graduated rates — wages, interest, pension payments, and withdrawals from traditional retirement accounts. The point of the label is the contrast: long-term capital gains and qualified dividends get their own, usually lower, rate schedule.Full entry →. A year's net loss, which is the usual year because the premium is spent, offsets that year's other capital gains and then up to $3,000 of ordinary income. The plan keeps no capital-loss carryforward, so the rest of a large loss goes unused, which understates the hedge's tax benefit rather than overstating it.

A hedge held in an IRA settles inside the IRA. Nothing is taxed, the premium comes out of the account's balance, and a payoff stays in the account. That keeps the taxes simple, and it means the payoff cannot be spent outside the account without a taxable distribution. Many custodians allow an IRA to buy puts and sell covered calls, and fewer allow anything more.

Rules that limit hedging a low-basis position

  • Constructive saleA hedge the tax law treats as a sale of the holding it hedges (IRC §1259), so the holding's gain is taxed in that year even though nothing was sold. It applies to a hedge that removes substantially all of a holding's risk of loss and chance of gain, such as a short sale or a short future on the same or substantially identical property. A collar can qualify when its floor and cap sit close together; there is no bright-line test.Full entry → (IRC §1259). A hedge that removes nearly all of a position's risk and reward can be treated as a sale, which realizes the gain the hedge was meant to defer. There is no bright-line test for a collar; practitioners keep the put and call strikes well apart.
  • Straddles (IRC §1092). An option that offsets a position in substantially similar property can defer the option's loss until the position is sold, and can suspend the holding period. An index option hedging an index fund that tracks it is the kind of pair these rules can reach.
  • Covered calls the straddle rules exempt. Some calls sold against stock fall outside the straddle rules, and whether one does depends on its strike and its term.

These rules turn on facts the forecast does not model. A tax advisor who works with options is the right person to read them for a particular position.

Portfolio insurance answers a narrow question: how to keep a large stock position through a dangerous stretch without selling it. It suits a household like the Whitcombs, whose basics are in place, whose stock carries a large gain, and for whom a crash in the first years of retirement would change the plan for good. It also suits an owner of a single concentrated stock who cannot sell yet, where a collar is the usual tool.

Most households have better places to look first, and each of them costs nothing a year:

  • An emergency reserve in cash covers a bad year's spending without selling anything.
  • An allocation and a glide pathA planned shift in allocation over time — classically toward bonds as retirement approaches, so risk comes down as the years available to recover from a loss come down with it. The word that matters is *planned*: a portfolio drifting toward whatever has been winning is not on a glide path, it is unrebalanced.Full entry → decide how much stock to hold. Holding less stock is the cheapest insurance there is, and in a traditionalA retirement account where the tax is paid on the way out — not a description of an old-fashioned account. Money went in before income tax, grew untaxed, and is taxed as ordinary income when you take it out — traditional 401(k)s and IRAs work this way. The contrast is Roth (taxed going in, tax-free coming out) and taxable (brokerage, taxed as you go).Full entry → or RothA retirement account where you pay income tax on the money going in, and qualified withdrawals later come out tax-free — the mirror image of traditional. Named after the senator behind the law, so the word tells you nothing on its own. Plans care because Roth dollars do not add to taxable income later, which keeps them clear of income-driven cliffs.Full entry → account it costs no tax to change.
  • Taxes, and in particular where each holding sits and the order accounts are drawn from.
  • Insurance on lives, health and property, which covers losses a portfolio cannot.
  • Estate basics: wills, beneficiaries and powers of attorney.

Options strategies are a specialist's work, and a household can approach them either way. Some work with an advisor who runs a hedging program, and others place the trades themselves. Tutilis models both the same way: a hedge on an account, with a floor, a cap and a price.

A third way to hedge is a short index future, which costs no premium and gives up the gains along with the losses. Hedging a stock position with index futures follows the Whitcombs through it, and through the constructive-sale rule it raises.

  • The skew in real option prices. Markets charge more for puts far below the market than the normal distribution the forecast uses suggests. A collar with a −10% floor usually costs nothing at a cap well below 20%, so real collars give up more of the upside than Collar does.
  • The markup is one assumption. The 100% markup on puts and the 20% discount on calls stand for the volatility risk premium in every year. The real premium varies, and it is highest right after a crash, when insurance is most wanted. Both can be changed on the hedge, or tried in Transforms.
  • The year is the step. The forecast settles the puts once a year on the year's return. A market that falls 30% in March and recovers by December pays nothing here, though a real put could have been sold at a profit in March.
  • Trading costs and timing. Rolling options every year costs commissions and bid-ask spreads that the plan does not charge.
  • The tax rules that limit hedging. The constructive-sale and straddle rules above, and the option to carry a Section 1256 loss back three years, are not modeled.

  • A put pays in the years the market falls past its floor and costs a premium in every year. Across many futures it raises the bad outcomes and lowers the average.
  • A collar makes the put cheap in cash by giving away the best years, which are the years a portfolio recovers in.
  • Index options are Section 1256 contracts, taxed 60% long-term and 40% short-term every year. Where the hedge sits decides whether it is taxed at all.
  • Holding less stock is the simplest insurance. Options earn their cost when selling stock would cost more, as it does for a large low-basis position.
