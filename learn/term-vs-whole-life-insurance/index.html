Alex has two life policies. One is a twenty-year term policy bought at 57, the other is a whole-life policy that has been building cash value for years. Neither belongs in an account, and neither is a real asset in the way a house is, but both cost money every year, one of them is worth something today, and each promises a payment at a death the plan already has a year for. This lesson shows where each of those three things lands.

Open the DossierA main-rail area. Where the household plan is defined — people, assets and liabilities, cash flows. In these lessons you mainly view; on desktop you enter and edit your own plan.. On the Bird's Eye, each policy has a row under Assets, and hovering the row raises the policy's card. The whole-life policy's row is its cash value, which is net worth; the term policy's row carries no value at all, because a term policy is not worth anything until it pays. In the desktop app the same cards sit under Insurance on the balance sheet.

The term policy is the simpler one. It has a death benefit, a premium, and coverage years. If Alex dies inside those years, it pays; if the years run out first, it has cost its premiums and pays nothing, which is what most term policies do.

The card says so plainly. Its Pays row reads Expires before the insured's death, because the coverage ends in 2046 and Alex's death age puts the death after the plan's last year.

The whole-life policy is permanent. It covers Alex for life while the premium is paid, and it has a cash value that grows at a modest crediting rate.

Its Pays row names Alex's death year, and the card carries the cash value and its growth as well as the benefit and the premium.

Open the CF TableA Forecast Detail chip. CF = cash flows for the selected year — incomes, expenses, taxes, and invest / sell / borrow totals. on any year before 2046 and both premiums are there among the expenses, each under the policy's own name.

Where it shows in the forecast

The year after, only the whole-life premium remains. A term policy's premium stops with its coverage. A permanent policy's premium stops when the insured dies, or earlier if its premium years say the policy is paid up.

Open the BS TableA Forecast Detail chip. BS = balance sheet for the selected year — real assets, accounts, liabilities, net worth. Copyable companion to BS Chart.. The whole-life policy's cash value is listed among the real assets, under the policy's name, and it counts toward net worth from the first year.

Where it shows in the forecast

It grows at its own rate, four percent a year unless the policy says otherwise, and the forecast never sells it or borrows against it. A term policy has no line here, because it has no cash value.

A death benefit is paid in the year the insured dies. Who receives it depends on who is left. When one of two owners dies first, the benefit is the survivor's cash, and the plan invests it like any other surplus. When the insured is the last owner, the plan ends at that death, and the benefit lands in the plan's final year, which is where an estate reads it.

Alex is the only owner here, so the whole-life benefit lands in 2058, the last year of the plan.

It is the year's only inflow, and no tax is assessed on it. A life-insurance death benefit is not income, and the tax pass leaves it alone.

Desktop app only

Open the term policy and push its coverage end past 2059. Its Pays row changes to name Alex's death year, and the final year's statement gains a second benefit line.

The term policy, Alex 20-year term. Coverage years are what you push past 2059.

Then open the whole-life policy and set Owned by to Irrevocable trust. Nothing on the cash-flow statement changes; the benefit still arrives. What changes is the estate: on the Net Worth Distribution card, with estate tax on, the trust-owned proceeds are left out of the taxable estate and pass to heirs untaxed.

The whole-life policy. Owned by is the toggle at the bottom of the editor.

The plan never draws on the cash value. A policy loan, or surrendering the policy for its cash value, are both real choices, and neither is modeled yet. The benefit is level, so a decreasing-term policy is entered at its average, and a policy on a dependent's life pays in that person's death year like any other.
