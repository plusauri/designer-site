A forecast prints hundreds of numbers, and most of them are fine. When you ask a Cogilis assistant to look over your plan, it does not start by reading all of them. It first asks the engine what kind of plan it is looking at, and lets the answer choose the few tables worth reading.

This lesson shows that first step on two households with very different shapes. The Springfields are a married couple with three kinds of account and Social Security ahead. Maya Reyes is a single parent whose home is half of what she owns, and her son Sam starts college next year. As in the other Cogilis lessons, the commands name built-in plans with --sample, so you can paste any of them into a Cogilis folder and get exactly the output shown. In your own session the assistant names your plan with --dossier.

The engine's answer is a table of classifiers. Each classifier looks at one property of the plan and places it in exactly one band. The assistant runs it before anything else.

The assistant asks what kind of plan this is

Before I read any forecast numbers, I asked the engine what kind of plan yours is:

Every row has the same five columns. Band is where the plan landed. Measure is the number the band was cut from, and Why says how that number was computed and gives the cut points, so you can see how close the plan sits to the next band. Cost says what the classifier had to read. The balance sheet rows read only what the plan owns and owes today. The plan sketch rows read its income and spending year by year, in today's dollars. The forecast and sweep rows read a forecast of the plan on the GMFGMF ✦ (also FID 0 / GMF) is the product north star — the deterministic geometric mean forecast path you steer by, not a random Monte Carlo draw and not “most likely.” Prefer it for a stable year-by-year read of the plan. Switch away when you want to explore other randomized scenarios or paths in the multi-path cloud. The ✦ mark matches the live FID control (compass / north star, not a rating star).Full entry → path.

The assistant reads the bands as a list of places to look. It does not read them as a verdict on the plan.

Start with the rows about strain on the portfolio. WithdrawalStrain and SequenceOfReturnRisk are both Low: the first retired years ask little of the Springfields' investments. TaxDiversification is AllThree, so they hold money in taxable, tax-deferredTax postponed, not avoided. Growth compounds without a yearly tax drag, and the bill arrives when the money comes out — which is why a large tax-deferred balance and a required withdrawal age are the same conversation.Full entry → and tax-freeNo income tax due when the money comes out — the Roth promise, and the reason Roth dollars keep a withdrawal clear of income-driven thresholds. The word hides a condition: the withdrawal has to be qualified, which turns on age and how long the account has been open. It also hides a scope: municipal-bond interest is tax-free on the federal return yet still counts toward whether Social Security is taxed.Full entry → accounts and can choose which to draw from.

Two rows are High. PortfolioDependence is High because once they retire, Social Security covers only about a quarter of their spending, and the portfolio pays the rest. Its own Why column says that reliance on its own is not a concern. It only matters if the portfolio cannot carry the spending, and the two strain rows say that it can.

That leaves CliffProximity, also High. Its measure lists the years the Springfields' income sits just under a line where a tax or a premium steps up: an IRMAAIncome-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount — a surcharge on Medicare premiums for higher-income households. It works in tiers, so one dollar of extra income can raise the premium for a whole year. It looks back at a tax return from a couple of years earlier, which is why a one-off spike (a big conversion, a property sale) shows up as a bill much later.Full entry → tier on Medicare premiumsWhat you pay for coverage — a monthly insurance or Medicare cost — and not a bonus or a prize. Confusingly, the same word means "above face value" when applied to a bond. In a plan it is nearly always the insurance sense: a recurring outflow that rises with age and, past a threshold, with income.Full entry → in six years from 2047, and the NIITNet Investment Income Tax — an extra tax (commonly cited at 3.8%) on investment income once income passes a threshold. It applies on top of ordinary income tax rather than instead of it, which is why a plan can show the same dollar taxed on more than one line.Full entry → floor and the Additional Medicare floor in a few more. The workspace's instructions pair this band with the cliffA threshold where one extra dollar of income triggers a step cost — a surcharge tier, a lost subsidy — instead of a smooth increase. Cliffs are why plans care about landing just under a number, and why a small year-end decision can be worth far more than its size.Full entry → radar for one of the years it names, so the assistant looks at 2047.

The assistant checks the tax lines in 2047

Your income comes close to a tax line in 2047, so I checked how much room is left under it:

The Distance column is the room left under the IRMAA tier that year. A Roth conversionDeliberately moving money from a traditional account into a Roth one and paying the income tax on it now, so it grows and comes out tax-free later. The usual reason is a low-income year — between retiring and claiming benefits, say — where the conversion is taxed at a lower rate than the future withdrawal would be. It also shrinks later RMDs.Full entry → or a large sale in 2047 would use that room up, and going over a tier raises the year's Medicare premiums by the whole step. RothConversionPotential is Moderate for the same household, so the two rows point at one question. For the Springfields, what matters is when they take income in retirement, and from which account.

The same question about Maya's plan

Before I read any forecast numbers, I asked the engine what kind of plan yours is:

Liquidity is High: about half of Maya's gross assets are her home, and a home cannot pay a bill without being sold. LeverageExposure is Moderate because what she still owes on the mortgage is about a quarter of her gross assets. Neither row is a problem on its own, but together they say that the money she can actually spend is a smaller share of her wealth than her net worth suggests.

WithdrawalStrain is High, with a measure of 6.2% drawn in 2048, her first year without wages. For a plan that leans on its investments like that, the workspace's instructions say to look at how hard the plan draws on them in each year.

The assistant looks year by year

Your plan leans on its investments, so I looked at how hard it draws on them in each year:

The table tells a different story from the band. The sketch behind the band is a quick estimate that runs no forecast, and the forecast's own rate for 2048 is about half of it. The heavy years are elsewhere. From 2027 to 2030 the rate runs between 7% and 9.5%, and from the early 2050s it climbs every year as the balance shrinks, passing 7% in 2062. The negative rates in between are the years when she puts more into her investments than she takes out.

The heavy years start next year, so the assistant shows one of them.

One of the heavy years

Here is 2028, the second of Sam's four college years:

Maya's wages fall short of her spending and taxes by about the cost of Sam's college. The gap is paid by selling holdings, and the Sales rows list which ones. You can see the same four years on the canvas. Switch to the Maya Reyes chip and look at the CF ChartA Forecast Detail chip. CF = cash flows. Money in above the line, money out below, by category over the years. The net line, drawn in thick black, is net cash flow — often the thing you came for..

The band was right that the plan leans on its investments. The forecast showed where. The first strain comes two decades sooner than the band's measure suggested. For Maya, what matters is paying for college without drawing down the savings that her retirement depends on.

The two households asked the same question and got two different first answers, one about taxes and one about cash. That is what the classifiers are for. They let the assistant spend its attention where the plan is unusual, instead of reading every number and judging each one itself.

The workspace's instructions hold the assistant to three rules when it uses them:

  • It quotes a band with its measure and says which cut point the plan fell under. "CliffProximity is High" says much less than "income sits just under an IRMAA tier in six years from 2047".
  • It never offers a band as an answer. A band says where to look, and the answer comes from the table it points to. Maya's plan shows why: the band's year was the wrong year.
  • It explains Undetermined. A classifier that could not decide says why in its Why column, often because the plan leaves something out. The assistant passes that on rather than skipping the row.

There is a faster version of the same table. dossier.classifiers skips the forecast, so its forecast and sweep rows read Undetermined with the measure no forecast, and the rest are identical. The assistant uses it for a quick look across several plans, and forecast.classifiers once it has picked the one to work on.
