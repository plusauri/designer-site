Costs you keep paying

The situation Robin and Jae Marsh are 52 and 50, still working, and manage their own money: about $1.1 million in three funds spread across a taxable brokerage account, two 401(k)s and two Roth IRAs. They want to know which costs are worth chasing, and whether it matters which account holds the bonds.

Returns are the part of investing nobody controls. Costs are the part you do. Two of them come out of a portfolio every year whether you look or not: the fund's own fee, its expense ratioWhat a fund charges each year to run it, as a percent of what you hold. It is taken out of the fund's assets day by day, so it never arrives as a bill; it shows up as a slightly lower return. Broad index funds commonly charge well under 0.1% a year, and many actively managed funds charge 0.5% to 1%.Full entry →, and the tax on what a fund pays out in a taxable accountAn ordinary taxable investment account — no special retirement wrapper, no contribution limit, and no withdrawal age rules. The trade-off is that dividends and realized gains are taxed as they happen rather than deferred. Often called the taxable bucket next to traditional and Roth.Full entry →. Both compoundGrowth on top of previous growth — this year's return is earned on last year's gains as well as the original money. It is why a plan curve bends upward instead of climbing in a straight line, and why the early years matter more than their size suggests.Full entry →, the same way returns do, so a cost that looks small in one year can be large over a working life and a retirement. Many do-it-yourself investors, and the BogleheadsInvestors who follow the ideas of John C. Bogle, who founded Vanguard and in 1976 launched the first index fund sold to individual investors: own broad index funds, keep costs low, choose a simple mix and hold it through good years and bad. The community gathers on the forum and wiki at bogleheads.org.Full entry → community in particular, build their portfolios around keeping both low. This lesson follows one couple through three versions of the same plan to show what each cost adds up to and which choices move it.

Robin is 52 and Jae is 50. Both work, both plan to retire at 62, and they manage their own money. They have about $1.1 million in three funds: a US stock fund, an international stock fund and a bond fund, with a little cash. A 403(b) is the same idea for schools and nonprofits, a 457 for government workers.Full entry → deferrals, their employers' matches and a small amount into a brokerageAn ordinary taxable investment account — no special retirement wrapper, no contribution limit, and no withdrawal age rules. The plan has no state selected, so every tax figure in this lesson is federal. The lesson opens three plans, and each changes one thing.

  • Index funds holds the three funds as index fundsA fund that holds the securities in a market index, in the index's proportions, instead of choosing among them. Its return follows the market it tracks, less its cost. Index funds typically have low expense ratios and low turnover, so in a taxable account they tend to pay few capital-gain distributions.Full entry →, with expense ratios of 0.03% to 0.05%.
  • Same mix, active funds holds the same mix in actively managed funds, at 0.5% to 0.9% a year, and the stock funds pass out capital gains every year.
  • Bonds in the taxable account goes back to the index funds and swaps where they sit: the bond fund in the brokerage, the stocks in the 401(k)s.

All three plans give every fund the same return before its costs. That is a choice made for the comparison, not a claim about any fund: an actively managed fund aims to earn more than its index, and some do. The plans ask a narrower question, which is what the costs take when the returns are the same.

A fund's expense ratio is a yearly percentage of what you hold, taken out of the fund's assets a little each day. It never arrives as a bill. It shows up only as a return slightly lower than the fund's holdings earned. In Tutilis it sits on the holding card, next to the fund's expected return, and every holding of that fund shares it.

A difference of under one percentage point a year looks like a rounding error. On the Marshes' plan it compounds into most of a million dollars of fees.

The second cost appears only in a taxable account. A fund that sells holdings at a gain must pass that gain out to its shareholders, as a capital-gain distributionA fund passing its own realized gains through to you. The trap is that it is taxable in the year it is paid even though you sold nothing — the fund did the selling. Distinct from the gain you realize by selling your shares.Full entry →, and the shareholder owes tax on it that year, even though they sold nothing. An actively managed fund trades more, so it realizesA gain that has been locked in by selling — and, in a taxable account, the moment it becomes taxable. Until then it is unrealized: the holding is worth more than you paid, but nothing has happened yet and no tax is due. The same word cuts the other way: a loss is only realized once you sell, which is why a downturn you ride through costs nothing you did not sell.Full entry → more gains; that is its turnoverHow much of a portfolio is sold and replaced in a year — a fund with 30% turnover trades away about a third of its holdings annually. In a taxable account the selling realizes gains, so turnover is a tax cost as well as a trading one. Tutilis does not model turnover; the nearest input is a holding's capital gain distribution yield.Full entry →. A broad index fund rarely sells, so it rarely distributes gains.

A capital-gain distribution is taxed at the long-term capital gains rates, so for the Marshes it costs about $2,000 a year while they work. Their total 2030 tax is about $48,000 on the active plan and about $46,100 on the index plan. That is real money, and for this household it is far smaller than the fee, because only about a quarter of their money sits in the taxable account. A household with most of its savings in a brokerage account pays this cost on most of its money.

Inside the 401(k)s and RothA retirement account where you pay income tax on the money going in, and qualified withdrawals later come out tax-free — the mirror image of traditional. Named after the senator behind the law, so the word tells you nothing on its own. Plans care because Roth dollars do not add to taxable income later, which keeps them clear of income-driven cliffs.Full entry → IRAs the distributions are taxed at nothing, so there turnover costs only its fee.

Which kind of account holds each fund is called asset locationWhich kind of account each holding sits in: taxable, tax-deferred, or tax-free. Asset allocation is the mix of stocks and bonds; asset location is where each piece of that mix lives. The same mix can cost more or less tax each year depending on its location.Full entry →, and it is a cost choice too. Switch to Bonds in the taxable account. The funds are the same low-cost index funds and the overall mix is the same. Only the placement changed.

The common rule of thumb follows: hold bonds where their interest is sheltered, and broad stock index funds in the taxable account. It is a rule of thumb rather than a law, and how much room each account has, and when each will be drawn on, can bend it.

Most of that is the fee compounding for forty years, and the rest is the tax on distributions and the growth that tax would have earned. If your own active funds earn enough more than their index to cover their costs, the comparison changes; the plan lets you test that by giving a fund a return of its own.

If you also pay for advice, that fee is a separate question with its own trade-offs. Cost and benefit of an advisor prices it, and what good advice can be worth.

  • Find your funds' expense ratios. Each fund's fact sheet or prospectus states it. Enter it on the holding card, and the Fund expenses row under the cash-flow statement shows what it costs each year.
  • Find their distributions. A fund's past capital-gain distributions are on its website and on your 1099-DIV. Enter them as the fund's yields, and the federal statement shows the tax in the years it arrives.
  • Check where each fund sits. Compare the interest and dividends in your taxable account on the federal statement with what the same funds would pay inside your retirement accounts.

  • Returns are the same before costs. A fund that beats its index by more than its costs would change the answer; the plans do not assume one does or does not.
  • Distributions are a steady yearly rate. Real funds distribute unevenly, with large distributions in some years and none in others.
  • Trading costs inside a fund are not separate. Bid-ask spreads and commissions a fund pays are part of what it earns, not of its expense ratio.
  • No state is selected. A state income tax adds to the cost of both distributions and bond interest in a taxable account.

A fund's fee and the tax on its distributions both come out every year, and both compound. The fee is printed on the fund page and shows in Tutilis as Fund expenses under the cash-flow statement; the tax shows on the federal statement in the years a fund distributes. For the Marshes, low-cost index funds and bonds kept in sheltered accounts leave several million dollars more at the end, for choices that cost them nothing to make.
