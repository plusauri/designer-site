The five-year retirement runway

The situation Dan and Sarah are both 57 next year. They want to retire at 62 with $2 million invested, they have about $1.5 million today, and they save $50,000 a year into their 401(k)s. In retirement they plan to spend $9,000 a month and both wait until 70 to claim Social Security. What should they check in each of the five years that remain, while there are still paychecks to fix what they find?

Rachael Camp is a CFP® who teaches retirement planning on her YouTube channel, and in this video she lays out a checklist for the five years before a planned retirement date. Her reasoning is that most problems found just before retirement are not fatal, but each one is much cheaper to fix three, four or five years earlier, while there are still paychecks to fix it with. So she gives one checkpoint for each of the last five years and follows a single couple through all of them. This lesson rebuilds that couple in Tutilis, checks her numbers, and shows each checkpoint on the plan.

Her couple are Dan and Sarah, both 57 in 2027, the first year the plans forecast. They want to retire at 62 with $2 million invested. They have about $1.5 million today, almost all of it in two traditionalA retirement account where the tax is paid on the way out — not a description of an old-fashioned account. Money went in before income tax, grew untaxed, and is taxed as ordinary income when you take it out — traditional 401(k)s and IRAs work this way. The contrast is Roth (taxed going in, tax-free coming out) and taxable (brokerage, taxed as you go).Full entry → 401(k)sAn employer-sponsored retirement account, named after the section of tax law that created it — so the name says nothing about what it does. Contributions are usually payroll-deducted, often matched by the employer, and come in traditional (pre-tax) and Roth flavors. A 403(b) is the same idea for schools and nonprofits, a 457 for government workers.Full entry →, and they put $50,000 a year into those accounts, including the employer match. They take home about $15,000 a month and spend it, and they plan to live on $9,000 a month in retirement. Medicare starts at 65, both of them will claim Social Security at 70, and their required minimum distributionsRequired Minimum Distribution — once you reach an age set by statute, the law makes you withdraw a minimum amount each year from traditional retirement accounts, whether or not you need the money. The withdrawal is taxable income, so RMDs can push a household into a higher bracket or over a surcharge threshold. Roth accounts are generally not subject to them.Full entry → begin at 75.

For the math lovers — where 2.95% comes from

Write the starting balance as \(B_0 = \$1{,}500{,}000\), the yearly saving as \(c = \$50{,}000\) added at the end of each year, and the return as \(r\). After five years the balance is

\[ B_5 = B_0 (1+r)^5 + c \, \frac{(1+r)^5 - 1}{r} \]

At \(r = 3\%\) this is about \(\$1{,}738{,}900 + \$265{,}500 = \$2{,}004{,}400\), just over the goal, and the rate that lands exactly on \(\$2{,}000{,}000\) is about \(2.95\%\). Saving at the start of each year instead would lower the rate a little further.

That number changes the question about risk. A portfolio that needs only 3% does not need 90% stocks, and stocks are what can push the retirement date back. Near the end of a career the balance is so large that one bad year in the market moves it far more than a year of contributions can. She credits the retirement researcher Michael Kitces with a name for this, retirement date riskThe chance that a bad market in the last years of work pushes the retirement date back. Near the end of a career the portfolio is so large that one poor year can undo several years of saving, so two savers with the same average return can reach their goal years apart depending on when the poor years fall. Michael Kitces named it in a 2016 article, *Retirement Date Risk and Sequence of Returns*. It is the working-years side of sequence of returns, and holding less stock as the date approaches is the usual answer when the plan needs only a modest return.Full entry →. His illustration has two savers with the same average return who receive it in opposite orders, and the one whose bad years come last reaches the goal years later.

Her remedy is not to sell everything and hold cash. That would only work because the couple need so little. A couple who needed 7% or 8% a year would trade one problem for another. For Dan and Sarah she suggests a glide pathA planned shift in allocation over time — classically toward bonds as retirement approaches, so risk comes down as the years available to recover from a loss come down with it. The word that matters is *planned*: a portfolio drifting toward whatever has been winning is not on a glide path, it is unrebalanced.Full entry →, which lowers the stock share a step at a time as the date approaches. The aim is not to remove risk but to make the date depend less on what the market does in the last few years.

She sees the years from 62 to 70 as an opportunity, because with no salary the couple can choose how much taxable income to show. She points to 62 to 65 as the bigger problem. Until Medicare, the couple buy health insurance on the marketplace, and the help they get with the premiumWhat you pay for coverage — a monthly insurance or Medicare cost — and not a bonus or a prize. She does not suggest working out the tax bill to the dollar four years ahead, because too much will change. A rough map is enough to show where the gaps and the opportunities are, and four more years of salary leave time to act on them. The third checkpoint fixes what the map found. She asks her clients to stop thinking of the portfolio as one round number. Two couples with $2 million each can have very different freedom in retirement, depending on how the money is split between traditional, RothA retirement account where you pay income tax on the money going in, and qualified withdrawals later come out tax-free — the mirror image of traditional. Named after the senator behind the law, so the word tells you nothing on its own. Plans care because Roth dollars do not add to taxable income later, which keeps them clear of income-driven cliffs.Full entry → and taxable accounts.

Her change for Dan and Sarah is to stop filling the 401(k)s past the employer match. They keep contributing enough to collect the match and send the rest of their savings to a brokerage account, and a Roth account would also serve if they can use one. They give up some of today's tax deductionAn amount subtracted from income before the tax is computed, so it saves tax at your marginal rate rather than dollar for dollar. That is the difference from a credit, which comes off the tax itself: a $1,000 deduction is worth a few hundred dollars, a $1,000 credit is worth $1,000.Full entry →, and in return they get money they can reach at 62 without a penalty and without raising their taxable income. Withdrawals from a brokerage account are taxed only on the gain, so most of each dollar drawn adds nothing to MAGI.

The Full runway plan makes that change from 2029, when the couple are 59. Each of them now defers only what earns the match, and $20,000 a year goes to the joint brokerage account. That is less than the full $26,000 they stopped deferring, which leaves room for the higher tax she warns about. The plan also includes the next checkpoint's savings sweep. Income this low has a rule of its own. Texas did not expand Medicaid, so between 100% and 138% of the poverty line the couple still get the marketplace credit, as they do in 2032 and 2034. Below 100% there is normally no help at all, the gap between Medicaid and the credit, and in 2033 the couple fall below the line. They keep that year's credit only because it was paid in advance on the income they expected, and a household is not made to repay an advance because its income came in lower. A household that planned to show this little income would not get the advance in the first place. A planner would usually draw some of the 401(k) in such years, to stay above the poverty line and to use the lowest tax brackets, and would save the brokerage account for later years. Tutilis does not yet have a withdrawal rule that fills income up to a chosen level, so the plan shows the simpler order.

The fourth checkpoint is about whether Dan and Sarah can actually live on the number the plan gives them. A plan can show that $9,000 a month is affordable, but it cannot show whether the couple will be comfortable spending it, and they may find it tighter than they expected or discover that they need less.

Her answer is a dress rehearsal two years before the paychecks stop. The couple keep taking home about $15,000 a month, but $6,000 of it is moved automatically into a savings account where they will not spend it, so only $9,000 reaches the account they pay bills from. Then they watch what happens. Do the bills get paid, does the travel they planned still fit, did they forget any expense, and how does it feel? She also suggests an optional second test for anyone unsure whether they are ready to stop working: take the longest leave the employer allows and spend it at home on an ordinary week, not on a trip, because a vacation is a poor imitation of retirement.

The Full runway plan carries out the rehearsal from 2030. Its living expenses drop to the retirement budget for 2030 and 2031, and the difference is swept into the Savings account. The last checkpoint asks a simple question: if the paychecks stopped next month, would everything work? By now the big question of whether they can afford to retire has been answered. What remains are the small ones that, left open together, make people hesitate. These include where the monthly money comes from, how the tax gets paid, which health plan to buy and when, and what to do with the 401(k) at the employer they are leaving.

She sets up four things in the final year. The first is a cash reserve of about a year of spending. The second is health insurance for the three years before Medicare, chosen and priced, with the date employer coverage ends. The third is the monthly transfer, $9,000 into checking on the first of each month, and the way tax on the withdrawals will be paid. The fourth is the paperwork, which covers employer benefits that need replacing, any retirement elections, and the transfers between accounts.

Her video does not name the planning software she used, and the plans here supply numbers she did not give, so some differences are expected. Two are worth knowing about.

  • Medicaid. Tutilis calculates the marketplace premium credit, but it does not model Medicaid. The couple live in Texas, which did not expand Medicaid, so the plans are right to show the credit for incomes between 100% and 138% of the poverty line. In a state that did expand it, those same years would bring Medicaid instead of the credit. The credit also assumes the plan's own premium is the benchmark silver plan, so the dollars are an estimate of the credit and not a quote.
  • Expected returns. The plans use Tutilis's own growth assumptions, which expect more than the 3% the couple need, so the middle path ends above $2 million. Her point is the 3% the plan needs, and these plans agree with it.

Her checklist works because each check comes early enough to change the outcome. The forecast confirms the three that can be measured:

  • The couple need only about a 3% return, so a glide path costs little and narrows the range of outcomes at the retirement date.
  • With the money almost all in traditional 401(k)s, every withdrawal from 62 to 70 is taxable income, and the three years before Medicare put their income over the health-subsidy line, so they pay the full premium.
  • Sweeping the difference into savings during a budget rehearsal leaves more than a year of cash on the day the paychecks stop. Spending that cash and the brokerage account before the 401(k)s keeps income under that line in all three of those years, which earns a premium credit of nearly the whole premium, and trades inside the 401(k)s keep the glide path while they do.

The other two checks, living on the budget before it is real and setting up the monthly transfer, are habits rather than numbers, and they are the ones she finds people skip.
