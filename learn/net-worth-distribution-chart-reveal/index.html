Reveal under Net Worth Distribution does not change the plan or the Monte CarloThe method behind a multi-path run: draw many randomized return paths, run the plan down each one, and read the spread of what happened. Named after the casino, because the engine is repeated random sampling rather than a formula — which is why two runs of one plan differ a little, and why a success rate is a proportion of sampled futures rather than a promise.Full entry → draws. It only changes the horizontal scale so a few extreme endings do not squash the mass of paths into an unreadable spike near zero (or near the left of the axis).

This lesson uses the Banks Baseline sample — an HNWHigh net worth — shorthand for a household wealthy enough that different planning problems dominate. Surcharge tiers, the investment surtax, and estate questions bind earlier, while running out of money is rarely the binding risk. There is no single official dollar line.Full entry → household with large balances and a high-volatility concentrated equity position. That mix is where Key Forecasts → Max (and the far right of the cloud) often sits far from the medianThe middle outcome — half above, half below — as against the average, which a few enormous results can drag upward. Over long horizons the two come apart badly, and the median is the one that answers "what does a typical path look like?"Full entry →, so linear scale hurts the most.

Terminal net worth on a long horizon is roughly “wealth × compounded path.” With:

  • Large starting wealth (home + multi-bucket portfolio in the Banks sample), and
  • High-vol holdings (e.g. a concentrated “Bel-Air Tech” style holding with elevated volatilityHow much an investment's returns bounce around from year to year, not whether it goes up or down overall. Written σ (sigma) in the product. Two holdings can have the same average return and feel completely different to live through — and high volatility drags the growth you actually compound below that average.Full entry →),

a small fraction of paths can finish orders of magnitude above the median. On a linear horizontal axis the chart must span Min → Max, so the middle of the distribution (where most lessons care about success at Silver) collapses into a few pixels.

Mass-affluent samples (e.g. Springfield) still have right tails, but absolute Max is closer to the bulk of the cloud — Reveal helps less often, or less dramatically.

Teaching claim: Reveal is most useful when Max (and the right tail) dominate the scale — common on HNW + high-vol books.

  1. ForecasterA main-rail area with two jobs: (1) run the plan year by year and inspect Forecast Detail, and (2) Simulation Summary — many paths summarized into something like a probability of success. → Simulation Summary → Distribution — linear cloud by default.
  2. Key Forecasts — note Min, GMF, Max (and quartiles). If Max is enormous vs GMF / median-ish rows, expect a squashed cloud.
  3. Reveal slider under the distribution (product may nudge “Outliers squashing the dots?”).
  4. Optional: Close up the distribution so scrubbing dots is easier (chart controls).

  1. Open this Banks sample. Simulation Summary is already populated (or Re-simulate if stale).
  2. On Key Forecasts, compare Max to GMF (or Mean) — order of magnitude only. On HNW + vol, Max is often dramatically larger.
  3. Glance at Distribution with Reveal full left (linear). Note how little of the plot width the bulk of the dots use.
  4. Slide Reveal toward log-style. The main cloud should open up; extreme endings still exist — they are not deleted from the run.
  5. Optional: open Springfield Baseline (or another mass-affluent sample) in a second tab. Same control, usually less dramatic need for Reveal because absolute Max is closer to the pack.
  6.
