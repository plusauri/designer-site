Reference — the standard deduction or itemizing?

Before the tax brackets apply, every filer subtracts a deductionAn amount subtracted from income before the tax is computed, so it saves tax at your marginal rate rather than dollar for dollar. That is the difference from a credit, which comes off the tax itself: a $1,000 deduction is worth a few hundred dollars, a $1,000 credit is worth $1,000.Full entry →. You take whichever is larger: the standard deduction, a flat amount set each year ($16,100 for a single filer in 2026), or the total of your itemized deductions. The main itemized deductions are state and local taxes (including property tax, up to a cap), mortgage interest, and gifts to charity.

Alex's Illinois income tax, about $15k, would count as an itemized deduction. On its own it is still less than the standard deduction, so Alex would take the standard deduction anyway. A mortgage or a large property tax bill could tip a real household like this one toward itemizing.

The forecast always uses the standard deduction. It does not itemize, which matters most for households with a large mortgage or high state and property taxes. Property tax, the silent killer shows what that leaves out.

Did you know?

When the Social Security payroll tax began in 1937, it stopped at the first $3,000 of wages. Since 1975 the cap has risen automatically each year with the national average wage. In 2026 it stands at $184,500.

Under the tax statement is an outlined button labeled Inspect US tax tables (2027), with a small ladder icon. Press it to open that year's tax schedules. The Social Security schedule shows 6.2% up to the wage base and nothing above it.

Why this statement does not look like the W-2 $300k card

On the W-2 plan, AGIAdjusted Gross Income — total income minus a specific set of adjustments, and the number many other tax rules build on. It is a midpoint, not your taxable income: deductions still come off after it.Full entry → and earned incomeMoney you were paid for working — wages, salary, self-employment profit. The distinction is not fussiness: earned income carries payroll tax and builds Social Security credit, while investment income does not. It is also what contribution limits and some credits are measured against.Full entry → are both $300,000, and payroll tax starts from the full wages. Self-employment works differently in three ways:

  1. A smaller tax base. Self-employment tax is charged on 92.35% of net earnings: 0.9235 × $300,000 = $277,050. That is the <strong>Basis</strong>What you paid for an investment — the starting point tax measures your gain against. Sell for more than basis and the difference is a taxable gain; the money you originally put in is not taxed again. It is the reason two accounts with identical balances can cost very different amounts to spend from.Full entry → on the Social Security and Medicare lines.
  2. Half of it is deductibleIn insurance, what you pay before coverage starts paying — a high deductible means lower premiums and more exposure in a bad year. Half of the self-employment Social Security and Medicare tax comes off income before income tax. (The Additional Medicare tax does not.) That pulls AGI below $300k.
  3. Lower income tax. A lower AGI means lower ordinary income tax: about $62k here, against about $67k on the W-2 plan. Total federal tax is still higher, because of the larger payroll tax.

The wage cap is a rule you do not control. You applied it to a plan and read the result as Original beside Modified. Once you have done that, the same steps work for other rules. Two of the other what-ifs in the Modifiables list answer questions most planning tools never ask.

A wealth tax, if one arrives

There is no US federal wealth tax today, but the Wealth tax what-if in Modifiables prices one against your plan the same way: rate, exemption, whether the exemption rises with inflation, how often it is charged, and whether the home and retirement accounts count. Erin's plan sits far below any exemption a real proposal uses, so it shows nothing here. A wealth tax on your plan walks through it on a $110 million household, with the proposals, the history and the revenue studies behind it.

What an advisor actually costs

An advisor fee is not a policy, but the same Original and Modified comparison prices it. The Advisor fee what-if in Modifiables charges either a share of assets under managementAssets under management — the market value of the portfolio an advisor or firm manages for you, and the usual base for their fee. A fee quoted as a percentage of AUM (say 1% a year) is charged on the balance rather than on gains, so it is owed in flat and losing years too, and it grows in dollar terms as the portfolio does. In the Transforms advisor fee what-if it costs a plan twice over: once as its own cash-flow line each year, and again as the compounding those dollars would have done for every year that follows — which is the part a fee schedule never shows.Full entry → each year or a fixed yearly amount. You set the rate. It starts at 1%, and you can change it to any rate, such as 0.5% for an advisor who gives you a discount. The fee shows up as its own line in cash flow, and the money that pays it stops compoundingGrowth on top of previous growth — this year's return is earned on last year's gains as well as the original money. It is why a plan curve bends upward instead of climbing in a straight line, and why the early years matter more than their size suggests.Full entry → for the rest of the plan.
